# Chapter 8: Key People and Groups — The Mongol Empire

> [!exam-tip]
> For AP purposes, you need to know each Mongol leader's **region** and **key action** — not their biography. The AP exam tests consequences, not names.

---

## Genghis Khan (Temujin, c. 1162–1227)
**Time Period:** Late 12th–early 13th century
**Region:** Mongolia → Central Asia, northern China, Persia

- Unified warring Mongol tribes in 1206 through a combination of military genius, political ruthlessness, and meritocratic organization; declared Genghis ("Universal Ruler") Khan at a *kurultai*
- Launched a series of devastating conquests westward into Central Asia and the Khwarezmian Empire, and eastward into northern China (Jin Dynasty); his successors continued into Russia, the Middle East, Korea, and Song China
- His political innovations — decimal military units, the yam postal system, promotion by merit over tribal birth — were as consequential as his conquests; they created an administrative framework that survived his death

## Ögedei Khan (r. 1229–1241)
**Time Period:** Early-to-mid 13th century
**Region:** Central Asia, Russia, Eastern Europe

- Second Great Khan; son of Genghis Khan; continued the western expansion, launching the devastating invasion of Russia (1237–1242) and reaching Poland and Hungary
- The Mongol withdrawal from Eastern Europe after Ögedei's death (the kurultai had to convene) spared Western Europe from conquest — a reminder that Mongol political succession shaped geopolitics
- Completed the conquest of northern China (Jin Dynasty falls 1234) and began expansion into Song China

## Hulagu Khan (r. 1256–1265)
**Time Period:** Mid-13th century
**Region:** Persia, Middle East

- Grandson of Genghis Khan; founder of the **Ilkhanate**; led the campaigns that destroyed the Abbasid Caliphate and sacked Baghdad in 1258
- The destruction of Baghdad — burning its famous libraries (the "House of Wisdom"), slaughtering its population, killing the Caliph — is one of the most consequential events of the period; it ended the Abbasid Caliphate permanently and left the Islamic world without its symbolic center
- Later Ilkhanate rulers, including Ghazan Khan (r. 1295–1304), converted to Islam, illustrating how Mongol rulers often adopted the religions of the peoples they conquered

## Kublai Khan (r. 1260–1294)
**Time Period:** Late 13th–early 14th century
**Region:** East Asia (China)

- Grandson of Genghis Khan; founded the **Yuan Dynasty** in China (1271); completed the conquest of the Song Dynasty (1279); received Marco Polo at his court at Khanbaliq (Beijing)
- Attempted two invasions of Japan (1274, 1281), both repelled by a combination of Japanese resistance and typhoon storms — the "divine winds" (*kamikaze*) that became legendary in Japanese culture
- Adopted Chinese administrative terminology and some governance structures, but the Yuan did not restore Confucian civil service examinations in full — the exams were suspended for decades and only partially revived after 1315. Top positions remained dominated by Mongols and their Central Asian allies, making it more accurate to say the Yuan adapted Chinese administrative *forms* while maintaining Mongol ethnic supremacy in practice; his court was a cosmopolitan meeting point of Chinese, Persian, Central Asian, and European cultures

## Batu Khan and the Golden Horde
**Time Period:** Mid-13th century onward
**Region:** Russia, western steppes

- Grandson of Genghis Khan; led the conquest of Russia (1237–1242) and established the **Golden Horde** khanate controlling the western steppes
- Russian principalities paid tribute to the Golden Horde for over two centuries; Moscow rose to prominence partly through its role as tribute collector for the Mongols — an ironic foundation for Russian centralized power
- The Golden Horde's trade city Sarai on the Volga River was a major center of Silk Road commerce, connecting Russian fur and slave trades to the broader Mongol network

## Timur (Tamerlane, r. 1370–1405)
**Time Period:** Late 14th–early 15th century
**Region:** Central Asia, Persia, India, Anatolia

- Central Asian Turkic-Mongol ruler who claimed descent from Genghis Khan and briefly reunified Central Asian trade routes; his capital Samarkand became a center of the **Timurid Renaissance** (art, architecture, scholarship)
- His conquests were nearly as destructive as Genghis Khan's — he sacked Delhi (1398) and defeated the Ottoman Sultan Bayezid I (1402), temporarily disrupting Ottoman expansion
- His empire fragmented quickly after his death, failing to restore the Pax Mongolica; represents the last major attempt at Mongol-style Eurasian unification before the rise of the gunpowder empires

## Conquered Peoples — The Survivors
**Time Period:** 13th–14th centuries
**Region:** Eurasia broadly

- An often-overlooked group: the skilled artisans, administrators, doctors, engineers, and scholars who were captured rather than killed and forcibly relocated across the Mongol Empire
- Chinese engineers built siege weapons for Mongol armies in Persia; Persian administrators ran the Yuan bureaucracy; Islamic astronomers worked at Kublai Khan's court — forced migration became a mechanism of technology transfer
- Their story challenges the narrative of Mongol conquest as purely destructive: the Mongols were brutal destroyers who also created the most culturally diverse court environments in the pre-modern world

## The Plague Bacterium as Historical Actor
**Time Period:** 1330s–1350s
**Region:** Central Asia → Middle East, Europe, North Africa

- The AP exam treats the Black Death as a transformative historical force — its spread via Mongol-enabled trade networks killed approximately 25 million in Europe (1/3 of the population) and comparable numbers in the Middle East and China
- The plague's demographic consequences were more transformative than any single Mongol military campaign: it ended the Yuan Dynasty, weakened surviving Mongol states, triggered labor shortages in Europe that eroded serfdom, and left a generation-long trauma across Eurasian cultures
