# Practice & Review — Topic 4.2

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 206–207*

**Questions 1–3** refer to the following passage:

> "When the Portuguese go from Macao, the most southern port city in China, to Japan, they carry much white silk, gold, perfume, and porcelain and they bring from Japan nothing but silver. They have a great ship that goes to Japan every year, and brings back more than 600,000 coins' worth of Japanese silver. The Portuguese use this Japanese silver to their great advantage in China. The Portuguese bring from China gold, perfume, silk, copper, porcelain, and many other luxury goods."
>
> — Ralph Fitch, British merchant, account of travels to the East Indies, 1591

---

**Question 1.** Which conclusion about the Portuguese is best supported by the passage above?

A. They manufactured luxury goods that they could sell in China.
B. They made great profits transporting goods between Asian countries.
C. They primarily wanted to accumulate silver.
D. They preferred to trade with China rather than Japan.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The passage describes Portuguese carrying goods *between* Macao/China and Japan — classic intermediary trade. They aren't manufacturing goods (eliminates A), and while they collect silver, they use it to buy Chinese goods rather than hoarding it (eliminates C). There's no indication of a preference for China over Japan — they're trading in both directions (eliminates D).

---

**Question 2.** Which statement best describes the point of view of the source, Ralph Fitch?

A. He was ridiculing the Portuguese for working so hard for so little profit.
B. He was embarrassed that the Portuguese were taking advantage of the Chinese and Japanese.
C. He was hoping to make profits just as the Portuguese were doing.
D. He was criticizing the Portuguese for being so focused on acquiring wealth.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Fitch is a British merchant writing an account of East Indies trade. His detailed, admiring description of Portuguese profits ("to their great advantage") suggests he is observing a lucrative operation with the eye of someone who wants in. Nothing in the passage signals mockery (A), embarrassment (B), or criticism (D) — the tone is matter-of-fact and commercially focused.

---

**Question 3.** Which statement best explains why Portugal established a trading post empire?

A. It had a large navy and was able to conquer nearby lands.
B. It was a landlocked country and could not expand except by sea.
C. It had a small population and navy so controlling large territories was not possible.
D. It had fallen behind other European powers in development of navigational technology.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Portugal was a small nation — insufficient population and naval capacity to occupy and administer large territories across three continents. The trading post model (forts at chokepoints, licensing trade) was the efficient solution: control commerce without the cost of territorial empire. A is wrong — Portugal's navy was not large enough for conquest. B is wrong — Portugal has Atlantic coastline. D is backwards — Portugal *led* European navigation technology.

### SAQ 1 — Chart-Based (European Voyages)

*Refer to the table: European Voyages in Search of a Water Route to Asia (Spain/Columbus 1492, England/Cabot 1497, Portugal/Cabral 1500, France/Cartier 1535, Holland/Hudson 1609)*

**Part A**: Identify ONE technological improvement in the period 1500–1750 that originated outside of Europe yet helped Europeans in their voyages of discovery.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> The magnetic compass, originally developed in China and transmitted to Europe via the Islamic world, allowed sailors to determine direction at sea regardless of weather or visibility. Without reliable directional navigation in open water, the sustained Atlantic and Pacific voyages of the 1490s–1500s would have been far more difficult or impossible.

**Part B**: Explain how ONE explorer listed in the chart affected the empire that sponsored him beyond the impact identified in the last column.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Columbus's 1492 voyage did more than initiate Spanish colonization of the Americas — it triggered the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494), in which Spain and Portugal divided the non-European world between themselves under papal arbitration. This diplomatic outcome demonstrated Spain's newfound status as a major Atlantic power and established the legal framework for Spanish imperial expansion for the next century.

**Part C**: Explain how ONE explorer listed in the chart affected the indigenous population of the Americas.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Columbus's 1492 arrival initiated sustained European contact with the Caribbean, bringing with it Old World diseases — especially smallpox — to which indigenous peoples had no immunity. Within decades, epidemic disease killed an estimated 50–90% of indigenous Caribbean populations. This demographic collapse preceded and enabled the full-scale Spanish conquest of the mainland empires.

### SAQ 2 — Comparison of Colonial Powers

**Part A**: Identify ONE social similarity between Spanish and French settlements in the Americas in the period 1500–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Both Spanish and French colonizers included Catholic missionaries who sought to convert indigenous peoples to Christianity. Jesuit and Franciscan missionaries operated in both Spanish and French colonial territories, establishing missions and schools among indigenous populations throughout the Americas.

**Part B**: Explain ONE political or economic difference between English and French settlements in the Americas in the period 1500–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> English colonial settlements prioritized permanent agricultural settlement and territorial control, leading to a population of approximately 1 million European settlers by 1750. French settlements, by contrast, centered on the fur trade, which required cooperation with indigenous peoples rather than displacing them. As a result, New France had only about 60,000 European settlers by 1750 — a stark difference reflecting their contrasting economic models.

**Part C**: Explain ONE political or economic difference between Dutch and French settlements in the Americas in the period 1500–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Dutch settlements operated through chartered companies — particularly the Dutch West India Company — making their colonialism more commercially organized and profit-driven than French state-directed colonialism. New Amsterdam existed primarily as a trading hub and commercial node, exporting furs and tobacco to the Netherlands, while New France operated as a more integrated state project under direct French royal oversight, with missionaries and military officers playing major roles alongside traders.

## Historical Reasoning Practice

### Think As a Historian: Making Connections (AMSCO p. 208)

Relate each economic development to a development in a *different* field:

**1. Mercantilism → Political/Military History**
Mercantilist theory held that wealth was finite and states competed for it zero-sum. This logic drove European states to build navies, fight wars over trade routes, and form alliances based on commercial rivalry. The Anglo-Dutch Wars (1650s–1670s) and the wars of the Spanish Succession were fundamentally about which state would dominate global commerce — mercantilism made these conflicts rational.

**2. Trading Post Empire → Military History**
Portugal's trading post empire required constant armed enforcement. Portuguese forts at Hormuz, Goa, and Malacca were not just commercial outposts — they were military installations whose cannon gave Portugal the power to tax and license all passing trade. When Dutch and English forces arrived with better-funded militaries in the 1600s, Portugal's forts fell, and with them its commercial empire. Military capability was inseparable from commercial dominance.

**3. Increased Tax Revenue → Social History**
Colonial wealth flowing into European royal treasuries funded the Renaissance courts, patronized artists and scientists, and accelerated the rise of a wealthy merchant class. The influx of American silver also caused the **Price Revolution** — sustained inflation across 16th–17th-century Europe that undermined feudal landlords (whose fixed rents lost value) and enriched merchants (who could raise prices). Colonial revenue remade European social structure.

### Reflect on the Essential Question

*In 1–3 paragraphs, explain the causes and effects of state-sponsored expansion of maritime exploration.*

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> European maritime exploration after 1450 was fundamentally state-sponsored because its costs exceeded what private actors could bear and its benefits accrued primarily to states. Three interlocking causes drove state involvement: economic (mercantilism required states to find gold and silver; Italian monopolies on Asian trade raised European costs), political (inter-state rivalry meant that any power that failed to explore risked being permanently outcompeted by those that did), and religious (Christian states viewed overseas conversion as a duty, providing ideological justification for expansion at state expense).
>
> The effects were transformative at multiple scales. In the short term, Portugal built a trading post empire that redirected Indian Ocean commerce away from overland routes; Spain colonized the Americas and extracted the silver that fueled a global economy; and France, England, and the Netherlands established competing colonial networks across North America and Asia. In the long term, European exploration triggered the Columbian Exchange (the transfer of plants, animals, and diseases between hemispheres), the rise of the Atlantic slave trade, and the integration of the Americas into a global economic system for the first time. It also shifted the center of global economic power from the Indian Ocean world to the Atlantic world, a shift that would define the next four centuries.
>
> The contrast with China illustrates that this outcome was not inevitable. China possessed the technology and state capacity for maritime exploration — Zheng He's fleet dwarfed anything Europe produced. But the Ming court chose to withdraw after 1433, driven by Confucian scholars hostile to merchants and external ventures. Europe's expansion, by contrast, was propelled by competitive pressure: no single European state could afford to stop exploring without ceding advantage to rivals. It was the structure of European inter-state competition, as much as any individual explorer's courage, that made sustained maritime expansion a near-certainty.
