# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 4.2

**[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|Mercantilism]]**
The dominant economic theory of early modern European states: a nation's wealth was measured by its stockpile of gold and silver. Governments set policies to maximize precious metal inflows (by exporting goods) and minimize outflows (by restricting imports). Mercantilism made state sponsorship of exploration rational — finding new sources of silver and gold was a national security priority. (AMSCO p. 200)

> [!tip]
> Mercantilism explains *why* states paid for exploration. It also explains later policies like the Navigation Acts (England) and [[Concept Notes/Encomienda|encomienda]] system (Spain) — all designed to channel wealth back to the home country.

**Trading Post Empire**
Portugal's model of global commercial control through a network of fortified coastal outposts rather than territorial conquest. Key forts at Hormuz (1507), Goa (1510), and Malacca (1511) gave Portugal choke-point control over Indian Ocean and Southeast Asian spice routes. Ships trading between these posts were required to buy Portuguese licenses. (AMSCO p. 201)

> [!warning]
> Distinguish the Portuguese **trading post empire** from the later Spanish model of **territorial empire** (encomienda, large-scale colonization). Portugal extracted profit through trade control; Spain extracted it through land, labor, and tribute.

**Reconquista**
The centuries-long Christian campaign to recapture the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rule, completed in 1492. Its completion freed Spanish resources and instilled a crusading mentality that carried directly into overseas exploration — the same year Columbus set sail. King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella, fresh from expelling the Moors, sponsored his first voyage. (See [[Unit-1.6/Medieval Europe Overview|Medieval Europe Overview]])

**Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**
An agreement brokered by the Pope that divided the non-European world between Spain (west) and Portugal (east) along a line in the Atlantic Ocean. It gave Spain the Americas and Portugal Africa, India, and Brazil (which fell just east of the line). The treaty reveals how central **state diplomacy** was to exploration — exploration was a geopolitical competition, not just individual adventure.

**Northwest Passage**
A hypothetical sea route through or around North America that would connect the Atlantic to the Pacific and allow direct access to Asian markets. French, English, and Dutch explorers spent the 1500s–1600s searching for it. None found it, but their searches produced major territorial claims: Canada (France), New England (England), Hudson Valley (Netherlands). (AMSCO p. 203)

**Galleons**
Large, heavily armed Spanish sailing ships used primarily in the Manila Galleon trade (1565–1815). Galleons carried silver from the Americas across the Pacific to Manila, where it was exchanged for Chinese silk, porcelain, and spices. The galleon trade created one of the earliest global trade circuits, connecting the Americas, East Asia, and Europe. (AMSCO p. 202)

> [!exam-tip]
> The **Manila galleon trade** is a high-yield AP connection point. It links Topic 4.2 (exploration), 4.3 (Columbian Exchange), and later global economic integration. Be ready to explain how American silver reached China and what China traded in return.

**Manila**
Founded by Spain in 1571 in the Philippines, Manila became the hub of the transpacific silver trade. Chinese merchants came to Manila to trade silk and luxury goods for American silver. Manila's role made the Philippines the pivot point of early global commerce — connecting the Atlantic world (Spain's American silver) to the Asian world (Ming China's demand for silver). (AMSCO p. 201–202)

**Cape of Good Hope**
The southernmost tip of Africa, rounded by Bartholomew Dias in 1488. This geographic breakthrough confirmed that the Atlantic and Indian Oceans were connected, opening the possibility of an all-water route from Europe to Asia. Vasco da Gama exploited this route in 1497–1498, reaching India and transforming global trade. (AMSCO p. 200)

**Jamestown (1607)**
England's first successful permanent colony in the Americas, established by approximately 100 English colonists on the James River in Virginia. Named for King James I. Jamestown marked England's serious entry into American colonization following the defeat of the Spanish Armada (1588). (AMSCO p. 203)

**New Amsterdam**
Dutch settlement established on the southern tip of Manhattan island, today's New York City. Founded after Henry Hudson's 1609 voyage. New Amsterdam became a key node in Dutch transatlantic trade — buying furs from northern trappers and tobacco from Virginia, then shipping both to the Netherlands in exchange for manufactured goods. (AMSCO p. 204–205)

**New France**
The French colonial territory in North America, centered on the St. Lawrence River valley and Great Lakes region. Unlike Spanish or English colonies, New France prioritized the fur trade over permanent settlement and maintained relatively cooperative relations with many indigenous peoples. By 1750 its European population was only ~60,000, compared to ~1 million in English colonies. (AMSCO p. 203)
