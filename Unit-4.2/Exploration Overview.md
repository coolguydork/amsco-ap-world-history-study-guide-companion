# Topic 4.2 – Exploration: Causes and Events

**Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections | Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)**

## Learning Objectives

- Explain the causes of state-sponsored European maritime exploration.
- Identify the key explorers, their sponsoring states, and the regions they claimed.
- Understand the shift from Italian Mediterranean monopoly to Atlantic-based European empires.
- Compare the goals, methods, and outcomes of different European powers' exploration.

## Why Did Europeans Explore?

Before 1450, Italian city-states (Venice, Genoa) controlled the profitable overland and Mediterranean trade routes connecting Europe to Asia. They set the prices for silk, spices, and other luxury goods, giving them enormous economic leverage. Spain, Portugal, France, England, and the Netherlands were locked out of this monopoly — and they were determined to find alternative routes.

**The four main causes of exploration:**

**1. Economic**: Italian monopoly on Asian trade drove other European states to seek direct sea routes. Explorers hoped to find gold, silver, and marketable resources. The profit motive was central.

**2. Political**: European states were competing with each other for territory, resources, and power. Rivalry accelerated the pace of exploration — if Spain claimed a territory first, Portugal couldn't, and vice versa.

**3. Religious**: Christianity was deeply woven into European governance. Many Europeans believed it was their Christian duty to convert non-Christians. States sponsored missionary activities alongside commercial ones.

**4. Technological**: Breakthroughs in sailing (caravel, lateen sail, compass, astrolabe) made bold voyages possible for the first time. See [[Unit-4.1/Technological Innovations Overview]].

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam often asks you to explain multiple causes of exploration. Use the acronym **PRTE** — Political, Religious, Technological, Economic — and give specific evidence for each. Never say "Europeans wanted to explore" without explaining *why*.

## The Role of States in Maritime Exploration

Voyages were extraordinarily expensive. Individual merchants could rarely afford them. States provided:
- **Financing** for ships, crews, and supplies
- **Authority** to claim territories in the monarch's name
- **Military backing** to defend claims against rival European powers

**Mercantilism** — the dominant economic theory of the period — further pushed states into exploration. Mercantilists believed a nation's wealth was measured by its gold and silver reserves. To maximize precious metals coming in (through exports and colonial extraction) and minimize them going out (through imports), governments needed colonies and trade monopolies. This required direct state involvement in overseas ventures.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse mercantilism with capitalism. Under mercantilism, governments *controlled* trade to maximize their national wealth. Capitalism, which came later, relies on private market forces. The AP exam sometimes tests whether students know which came first.

## Portuguese Exploration

Portugal led European exploration and was the model for state-maritime partnership.

**Prince Henry the Navigator (1394–1460)**: Sponsored expeditions along Africa's Atlantic coast; began importing enslaved Africans by sea, replacing the overland slave trade.

**Bartholomew Diaz (1488)**: Sailed around the southern tip of Africa — the **Cape of Good Hope** — proving that a sea route to the Indian Ocean from Europe was possible. His crew threatened mutiny, forcing him to turn back.

**Vasco da Gama (1498)**: Completed Diaz's route, landing in India and establishing Portugal's foothold in Indian Ocean trade. This was transformative — Portugal now had a direct sea route to Asia's luxury goods.

**Afonso de Albuquerque**: Ruthless Portuguese admiral who conquered Arab trading networks in Southeast Asia, taking control of Malacca (in present-day Malaysia/Indonesia) and establishing a series of fortified **trading posts** stretching from Hormuz (Persian Gulf) → Goa (India, 1510) → Malacca (1511). This network gave Portugal a **global trading post empire** — small outposts controlling key chokepoints rather than large territories.

**Portugal's vulnerability**: Portugal was a small nation. It lacked the workers, ships, and administrative capacity to enforce such a vast empire. Corruption, independent merchants ignoring royal orders, and the rise of Dutch and English rivals eventually eroded Portugal's dominance by the 17th century.

**Portugal and Japan**: Portuguese traders reached Japan in the early 16th century, followed by Christian missionaries in 1549. Japanese initially tolerated them, but rulers eventually banned Catholicism and expelled missionaries by the 1630s.

**Portugal in East Asia (China)**: Portuguese arrived in China in 1514. Initial contact had little impact, but Jesuit missionaries like **Matteo Ricci** (arrived 1582) impressed the Chinese court with scientific and technical knowledge, though they failed to win many elite converts.

## Spanish Exploration

**Christopher Columbus (1451–1506)**: Italian navigator who convinced Spain's monarchs, Queen Isabella and King Ferdinand, to fund a westward voyage to Asia in 1492. He landed in the Caribbean, believing he had reached the East Indies — mistakenly calling the inhabitants "Indians." His voyages initiated the era of European colonization of the Americas.

**Ferdinand Magellan**: Led the first circumnavigation of the globe, proving the earth could be sailed around. He died in the Philippines in 1522, but one ship in his fleet completed the journey. Spain claimed the Philippines (1521), which became a critical hub of **Pacific trade** — especially the [[Manila Galleon Trade]] that exchanged American silver for Chinese silk and goods.

**Spain and silver**: Once the Spanish discovered enormous silver deposits in Mexico (Zacatecas) and Peru (Potosí), the Americas became spectacularly profitable. Silver flowed across the Pacific to China and across the Atlantic to Europe. See [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Overview]].

## French Exploration

The French focused primarily on North America in search of a **northwest passage** — an ocean route through or around North America to Asia.

**Jacques Cartier (1535)**: Sailed the St. Lawrence River, claiming much of what is now Canada for France.

**Samuel de Champlain (1608)**: Founded Quebec and expanded French presence in North America. French traders searched for furs; missionaries sought converts.

**French strategy**: Unlike the Spanish, the French rarely settled permanently. They traded with Native Americans for furs rather than displacing them. This produced relatively better relations with indigenous peoples, but also slower population growth — by 1754, New France had only 70,000 European settlers while English colonies had one million.

## English Exploration

**John Cabot (1497)**: Sent by the English king to find a northwest passage. Claimed lands from Newfoundland south to the Chesapeake Bay. England lacked the naval power to follow up effectively.

**Defeat of the Spanish Armada (1588)**: England's surprise victory over Spain's massive fleet transformed England into a major naval power and opened the door to Atlantic competition.

**Jamestown (1607)**: England's first successful permanent colony in the Americas — about 100 settlers traveled 60 miles inland from the Virginia coast and built a settlement named for King James I.

## Dutch Exploration

**Henry Hudson (1609)**: Sent by the Dutch to find a northwest passage. Explored the Hudson River and Hudson Bay, claiming the Hudson River Valley and Manhattan for the Netherlands.

**New Amsterdam**: Dutch settlement on the tip of Manhattan island (today New York City). Became a major node in the Dutch transatlantic trade network, connecting fur traders to the north with tobacco planters in Virginia, and sending goods to the Netherlands in exchange for manufactured items.

## Comparing Transoceanic Voyages

| Empire | Key Explorer | Region | Purpose | Outcome |
|--------|-------------|--------|---------|---------|
| China | Zheng He | India, Middle East, Africa | Open trade, spread Chinese culture | China withdrew from exploration |
| Portugal | Vasco da Gama | Africa, India | Direct sea route to Asia | Trading post empire; Indian Ocean dominance |
| Spain | Columbus | Caribbean, Central America | Sea route to Asia going west | Colonization of the Americas |
| Spain | Magellan | South America, Philippines | Prove circumnavigation possible | Pacific trade links; Spain claims Philippines |
| Britain | John Cabot | North America | Northwest passage | Claimed territory in Canada |
| Dutch | Henry Hudson | North America | Northwest passage | Hudson River Valley; New Amsterdam |

## AP Themes

**Governance**: State authority was essential — financing, legal authority to claim territory, and military protection all came from the state. Mercantilism shaped colonial policy.

**Economic Systems**: Exploration was driven by commercial motives; the discovery of American silver and sugar transformed the global economy.

**Cultural Developments**: Christian missionary activity accompanied every European expansion. Conflicts between religious groups drove some settlers (Protestants, religious minorities) to seek new homes in the Americas.
