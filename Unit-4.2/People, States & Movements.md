# People, States & Movements — Topic 4.2

## Key Explorers

**Prince Henry the Navigator (1394–1460)**
Portuguese prince and the first European monarch to systematically sponsor maritime expeditions. Henry established a school of navigation at Sagres, funded voyages down the African coast, and sought both an all-water route to Asia and African gold. Under his patronage, Portugal began importing enslaved Africans by sea, initiating what would become the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]]. His work laid the foundation for all subsequent Portuguese exploration. (AMSCO p. 200)

**Bartholomew Dias (c. 1450–1500)**
Portuguese explorer who became the first European to round the Cape of Good Hope in 1488, proving that the Atlantic and Indian Oceans were connected. His crew threatened mutiny in unknown waters, forcing a return before reaching India. The route he opened was completed a decade later by Vasco da Gama. (AMSCO p. 200)

**Vasco da Gama (c. 1460–1524)**
Portuguese navigator who completed the first all-water voyage from Europe to India (1497–1498). By sailing around Africa and reaching Calicut, India, da Gama opened a direct Portuguese trade route to the Indian Ocean — bypassing Italian and Ottoman middlemen and transforming European commerce. He established Portuguese territorial claims in India. (AMSCO p. 200)

**Christopher Columbus (1451–1506)**
Genoese explorer sailing for Spain who reached the Caribbean in 1492 while seeking a westward route to Asia. Sponsored by King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella — who had just completed the Reconquista — Columbus's voyages initiated sustained European contact with the Americas. Although he never acknowledged he had found continents unknown to Europeans, his journeys triggered the full wave of Spanish and European colonization. (AMSCO p. 199)

> [!exam-tip]
> Columbus is notable not just for "discovering" the Americas but for demonstrating that **state patronage** could fund what individual explorers and merchants could not. His voyages were as much a political and economic event as a geographical one.

**Ferdinand Magellan (c. 1480–1521)**
Portuguese explorer sailing for Spain who led the first expedition to circumnavigate the globe (1519–1522). Magellan died in the Philippine Islands in 1521, but his fleet's completion of the voyage proved that the earth was circumnavigable and that the Pacific was a viable — if enormous — trade route. Spain subsequently claimed the Philippines. (AMSCO p. 201)

**Matteo Ricci (1552–1610)**
Italian Jesuit missionary who arrived in Macau in 1582 and eventually gained access to the Ming imperial court. Ricci used European mathematics, astronomy, and cartography to impress the Chinese scholar-gentry. He represents the Jesuit strategy of **cultural accommodation** — adopting the language and intellectual customs of a target culture to gain converts among elites. He had limited success: the scholar-gentry largely viewed Europeans as barbarians. (AMSCO p. 201)

**John Cabot (c. 1450–1499)**
Italian-born explorer sailing for England who reached the coast of North America (Newfoundland to the Chesapeake Bay) in 1497. His voyage gave England its territorial claim in North America, though the English did not seriously pursue colonization for nearly a century afterward. (AMSCO p. 203)

**Jacques Cartier (1491–1557)**
French explorer who in 1534 sailed from the Atlantic into the St. Lawrence River, claiming parts of present-day Canada for France. He searched for a northwest passage and a route to Asian riches; instead, he opened France's path into the North American interior. (AMSCO p. 203)

**Samuel de Champlain (c. 1574–1635)**
French explorer and founder of Quebec (1608), the first permanent French settlement in North America. Champlain explored the Great Lakes region and helped establish the French fur trade network that would define New France's colonial economy. (AMSCO p. 203)

**Henry Hudson (c. 1565–1611)**
English explorer sailing for the Dutch [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] who in 1609 explored the East Coast of North America and sailed up what became the Hudson River. His voyage gave the Netherlands its claim to the Hudson Valley and Manhattan. Hudson later died on a voyage for England while still searching for a northwest passage. (AMSCO p. 204)

## Key States

**Portugal**
The pioneer of European maritime exploration, driven by its Atlantic geography, state investment in navigation, and desire to bypass Italian and Ottoman trade monopolies. Portugal built a **trading post empire** across the Indian Ocean — Hormuz, Goa, Malacca — and established the first direct sea route from Europe to Asia. By the late 1500s, its overextension made it vulnerable to Dutch and English competition. (AMSCO pp. 200–201)

**Spain**
Entered exploration after Portugal, energized by the 1492 Reconquista. Spain pursued a **territorial empire** in the Americas, conquering the Aztec and Inca empires and extracting enormous quantities of silver. Spain also colonized the Philippines (1565), creating the Manila galleon silver trade that linked the Americas to East Asia. Spain briefly dominated Atlantic and Pacific commerce before challenges from England and the Netherlands. (AMSCO pp. 201–203)

**France**
Focused its North American exploration on finding a northwest passage and exploiting the fur trade. Founded Quebec (1608) and explored the Great Lakes and Mississippi River valley. Maintained better relations with indigenous peoples than Spain or England by trading rather than settling. French colonial population in North America remained small throughout the colonial era. (AMSCO p. 203)

**England**
Lagged in early exploration due to lack of sea power. The defeat of the Spanish Armada in 1588 changed everything, establishing England as a major naval power and opening Atlantic competition. Jamestown (1607) launched English territorial colonization; by 1750, English colonies held ~1 million European settlers. (AMSCO p. 203)

**Netherlands**
Entered exploration through the [[Concept Notes/Dutch East India Company\|Dutch East India Company]] (VOC) and the voyages of Henry Hudson. The Dutch seized Malacca from Portugal (1641) and built Fort Batavia in Java (1620) to control the spice trade. New Amsterdam (1624) anchored their North American trade network. The Dutch exemplified **chartered company colonialism** — state-backed but privately operated. (AMSCO pp. 201, 204)

## Key Movements

**Jesuit Missions in Asia**
The Society of Jesus (Jesuits) followed Portuguese traders into Asia, using a strategy of cultural accommodation — learning local languages, adopting elite customs, and presenting European science as a path to influence. Matteo Ricci in China and Francis Xavier in Japan represent this movement. Jesuit missions in Japan grew until Japanese rulers outlawed Catholicism and expelled missionaries in the 1600s, fearing foreign political influence. (AMSCO p. 201)

**The Reconquista and Crusading Mentality**
The Reconquista — the 700-year Christian campaign to retake Iberia — concluded in 1492, the same year Columbus sailed. Spain's overseas expansion was deeply infused with this crusading mentality: conversion of non-Christians was simultaneously a religious duty and a political justification for conquest. The Reconquista provided Spain with battle-hardened soldiers, experienced commanders, and a proven model of conquest. (See [[Unit-1.6/Medieval Europe Overview|Medieval Europe Overview]])
