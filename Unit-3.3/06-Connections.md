# Unit 3.3: Connections

#apwh #unit-3 #connections

---

## Builds On (Earlier Chapters)

- [[Unit-1.1/00-Overview|Unit 1.1 — Islam's Early Spread]]: The Sufi orders that spread Islam in this period trace their roots to the classical period of Islamic mysticism (8th–12th centuries); the Sunni-Shia split that defines Unit 3.3 originated in the early Islamic caliphate (Unit 1)
- [[Unit-1.2/00-Overview|Unit 1.2 — Mongol Empire]]: Mongol religious tolerance (Unit 1–2) created conditions in which multiple religions coexisted across Eurasia, setting the stage for the syncretic encounters of Ch 13; Rumi wrote his Masnavi under Mongol-era conditions in Anatolia
- [[Unit-1.3/00-Overview|Unit 1.3 — Christianity in Byzantium/Europe]]: The medieval Catholic Church whose authority Luther challenged had been shaped by centuries of Byzantine and Roman imperial patronage; the Pope's authority that Henry VIII rejected had roots in Constantine's adoption of Christianity (Unit 1)
- [[Unit-2.1/00-Overview|Unit 2.1 — Silk Roads]]: Sufi orders spread Islam along the same overland trade routes examined in Ch 7; Buddhism's spread along the Silk Roads (Unit 2) is the direct parallel to both Islamic and Christian missionary expansion in Unit 3.3
- [[Unit-3.1/00-Overview|Unit 3.1 — Empire Expansion]]: The Ottoman-Safavid rivalry (Unit 3.1) is the political backdrop for the Sunni-Shia institutionalization examined in Ch 13; Mughal religious policies (Unit 3.1–12) directly caused the development of Sikhism's martial identity

## Sets Up (Later Chapters)

- [[Unit-4/00-Overview|Unit 4 — Transoceanic Expansion]]: The Jesuits traveled with European merchants and conquistadors; Catholic missionary activity in the Americas is inseparable from colonialism; Protestant vs. Catholic competition influenced which European powers colonized which regions
- [[Unit-4/00-Overview|Unit 4 — African Diaspora Religion]]: Vodou and Candomblé — syncretic religions discussed briefly in Unit 3.3 — are direct products of the Atlantic slave trade examined in Unit 4
- [[Unit-5/00-Overview|Unit 5 — Revolutions]]: The Enlightenment (Unit 5) grew partly from the Protestant emphasis on individual conscience, vernacular literacy, and questioning of traditional authority; the Thirty Years' War's devastation contributed to the desire for a more rational, secular basis for governance
- [[Unit-6/00-Overview|Unit 6 — Industrialization consequences]]: The Wahhabi movement (late Unit 3.3) gains global significance in the modern era as a response to Western imperialism and cultural challenge — a direct bridge to Units 7–9

## Comparative Civilizations and Contrasts

| Compare | Contrast |
|---|---|
| Protestant Reformation vs. Wahhabi movement | Both were purifying reform movements within their traditions (Christianity / Sunni Islam) attacking what they saw as corruption and innovation |
| Jesuit accommodation strategy vs. Sufi missionary methods | Both used cultural adaptation to spread their faith; Jesuits worked top-down (through rulers), Sufis worked bottom-up (through communities) |
| Sikhism vs. Vodou as syncretic traditions | Both emerged from cultural encounters; Sikhism from voluntary Hindu-Muslim coexistence, Vodou from forced African-Catholic encounter through slavery |
| Luther's challenge to papal authority vs. Sikh Gurus' challenge to Mughal authority | Both used religious conviction to challenge imperial/institutional power; different outcomes (Luther succeeded, Sikh Gurus were martyred but the tradition survived) |
| Tokugawa Japan suppressing Christianity vs. Aurangzeb suppressing Sikhism | Both rulers used state power to repress religious minorities; both were partially successful but created lasting resentment and underground resistance |
