# Unit 3.3: Key Terms

## The Protestant Reformation and Its Consequences

**Protestant Reformation** — The 16th-century movement begun by [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Martin Luther|Martin Luther]] in 1517 that challenged the authority of the Roman Catholic Church and led to the creation of new Protestant Christian denominations. (Significance: permanently fractured Western Christianity; triggered religious wars (Thirty Years' War, 1618–1648); challenged the idea that a single church could hold all Christians; sparked debates about individual conscience vs. institutional authority that influenced political thought.)

**Indulgences** — Certificates sold by the Catholic Church that promised to reduce the time a soul spent in purgatory; Luther's 95 Theses specifically attacked the sale of indulgences as corrupt and theologically unsound. (Significance: the indulgence controversy is the immediate trigger of the Reformation, but the deeper issue was the question of whether the church hierarchy had the authority to mediate between humans and God.)

**95 Theses** — Martin Luther's list of arguments against indulgences, posted (according to tradition) on the door of the Wittenberg church in 1517; the starting point of the Protestant Reformation. (Significance: the printing press allowed Luther's ideas to spread across Europe within weeks — a landmark example of technology enabling religious and political change.)

**Calvinism** — The Protestant theological tradition developed by John Calvin in Geneva, emphasizing predestination (God has already determined who will be saved) and a highly disciplined, morally strict communal life. (Significance: spread to France (Huguenots), the Netherlands, Scotland, and eventually New England Puritans; strongly associated with later capitalism — Max Weber's "Protestant work ethic" thesis.)

**Anglican Church (Church of England)** — The Protestant church created by King Henry VIII of England when he broke with Rome in 1534, primarily to obtain a divorce. (Significance: uniquely political origins — the Reformation in England was driven by royal politics rather than theological conviction; the monarch, not the pope, became the head of the church — a fusion of religious and political authority.)

**Counter-Reformation (Catholic Reformation)** — The Catholic Church's internal reform movement in response to the Protestant challenge, codified at the [[Council of Trent]] (1545–1563). (Significance: defined Catholic doctrine more precisely, reformed corruption in the clergy, launched the Jesuit missionary movement, and produced the Baroque artistic style as a tool of religious re-engagement.)

**Council of Trent** — The series of Catholic Church councils (1545–1563) that defined official Catholic doctrine in response to Protestant challenges and enacted internal reforms. (Significance: clarified Catholic positions on salvation, scripture, and the sacraments; rejected Protestant innovations while addressing genuine corruption; launched the missionary era.)

**Jesuits (Society of Jesus)** — The religious order founded by [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Ignatius of Loyola|Ignatius of Loyola]] in 1540, known for rigorous intellectual training and global missionary work. (Significance: the most important agents of Catholic expansion in the early modern world; worked in China (Matteo Ricci), Japan (Francis Xavier), India, Africa, and the Americas; used cultural accommodation — learning local languages, presenting Christianity in local intellectual frameworks — as a missionary strategy.)

**Baroque** — The dramatic, emotional, and visually intense artistic style that emerged in Catholic Europe as part of the Counter-Reformation, designed to inspire awe and devotion. (Significance: Baroque art (Bernini, Caravaggio, Rubens) was a deliberate tool of Catholic propaganda — its grandeur was intended to communicate the beauty and power of Catholicism; spread to Latin America, where it fused with indigenous artistic traditions. Note: Rembrandt, though a contemporary, was Dutch Reformed Protestant and is not an example of Catholic Baroque propaganda.)

## Islam: Expansion and Division

**Sufism** — Islamic mystical tradition emphasizing direct personal experience of God through prayer, meditation, music, and the guidance of a spiritual master (sheikh). (Significance: Sufi missionaries were the primary agents of Islam's spread into sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia — their accessible, emotionally engaging approach was often more effective than formal legal Islam; Sufi orders (tariqas) were also important social networks in Islamic cities.)

**Sufi Orders (Tariqas)** — Organized brotherhoods of Sufi mystics following a particular spiritual lineage and method; examples include the Qadiriyya (influential in West Africa), the Naqshbandiyya (Central Asia, India), and the Mevlevi ("Whirling Dervishes" of Turkey). (Significance: tariqas were transnational networks that spread Islamic practice, education, and social solidarity across vast distances; the Safavid dynasty itself originated in the Safaviyya Sufi order.)

**Sharia** — Islamic religious law derived from the Quran and the hadith (sayings of the Prophet), interpreted and administered by the ulama (religious scholars). (Significance: in Islamic empires, sharia governed personal status (marriage, divorce, inheritance) and sometimes criminal law; the relationship between sharia and secular (imperial) law was a constant point of negotiation in the Ottoman, Mughal, and Safavid empires.)

**Wahhabi Movement** — A strict, puritan reformist movement within Sunni Islam founded by Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in Arabia in the 18th century, allied with the Saudi tribal leader Muhammad ibn Saud. (Significance: a late-Unit-3 / early-Unit-4 development; Wahhabism rejected Sufism, Shia practices, and perceived innovations in Islam; it became the theological basis for the modern Saudi Arabian state and remains influential globally — a long-term consequence of the religious debates of this period.)

## New and Syncretic Traditions

**Sikhism** — A monotheistic religion founded in the Punjab region of South Asia by [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Guru Nanak|Guru Nanak]] (1469–1539), drawing on both Hindu and Islamic traditions while rejecting the caste system and emphasizing equality before God. (Significance: a classic example of religious syncretism — it emerged from the encounter of Hinduism and Islam in the Mughal Empire; the Sikh Gurus were later persecuted by Aurangzeb, creating a community with a strong martial tradition.)

**Syncretism** — The blending or combination of different religious or cultural traditions into a new, integrated system. (Significance: one of the most important AP World History concepts — nearly every religion that spread into a new region underwent syncretism; examples: Sikhism (Hindu + Islamic), Vodou (African + Catholic), Candomblé (African + Catholic + indigenous), Japanese Buddhism (Buddhist + Shinto), Akbar's Din-i-Ilahi.)

**Vodou** — A syncretic religion developed by enslaved Africans in Haiti, blending West African spiritual traditions (especially from Dahomey/Fon and Yoruba peoples) with elements of Catholicism. (Significance: a product of the Atlantic slave trade that will be covered in Unit 4; introduced here as a prime example of religious syncretism emerging from forced cultural contact.)

**Confucianism (under pressure)** — The philosophical and ethical system based on the teachings of Confucius, emphasizing hierarchy, filial piety, and moral self-cultivation; it remained the official ideology of the Chinese state under the Ming and Qing. (Significance: Jesuit missionaries to China (especially Matteo Ricci) attempted to reconcile Christianity with Confucianism — the "Chinese Rites Controversy" — ultimately rejected by the Pope, which severely limited Christianity's growth in China.)

**Animism** — The belief that natural objects, phenomena, and the universe itself possess spirits or souls; the dominant religious framework of many sub-Saharan African, Siberian, and indigenous American communities before contact with Islam and Christianity. (Significance: understanding animist traditions helps explain *how* Islam and Christianity spread — often through partial accommodation of local spirit beliefs rather than outright replacement; syncretism was the norm, not the exception, in religious conversion.)
