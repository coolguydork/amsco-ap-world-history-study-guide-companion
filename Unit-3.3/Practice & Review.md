## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO p. 174. Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage.*

> "He is very valiant and has a great liking for warfare and weapons of war, which he has constantly in his hands: we have been eye-witnesses of this because, whenever we were with him, he was adjusting his [swords], testing his [muskets], etc. . . . This is the great experience, which he has obtained of warfare over so many years, that he makes it in person and from the first has made him a fine soldier and very skilled, and his men so dexterous that they are little behind our men in Europe. He has introduced into his militia the use of and esteem for [muskets], in which they are very practiced. Therefore it is that his realm has been so much extended on all sides. . . . All the above mentioned soldiers, who will total some 100,000, receive pay for the whole year."
>
> — Father Simon, a European Roman Catholic priest, in a report to the pope on meeting the Safavid Shah Abbas I, 1588

**Question 1.** Which claim about Father Simon's purpose for meeting Shah Abbas I is best supported by the above passage?

A. Simon was impressed by the new technology used by the Safavids.
B. Simon wanted the pope to know of the Safavids' military power.
C. Simon hoped the pope would sell new weapons to the Safavids.
D. Simon thought the Safavids could teach Europeans about how to organize a military.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Father Simon is writing a report *to the pope* — his primary purpose is informational: conveying the military strength of the Safavid shah. The passage emphasizes troop numbers, discipline, weapons, and territorial expansion. That is a diplomatic intelligence report, not an arms sales pitch (C) or a training recommendation (D). While Simon does note the technology (A), "impressed by technology" is too narrow — the whole passage is about military power holistically. The key context: a Catholic priest reporting to Rome on a Shia Muslim empire's military capacity. That's a GOV + CDI question about cross-religious diplomacy.

**Question 2.** Which most strongly contributed to the historical development reflected in Father Simon's description of the Safavid military structure?

A. Safavid intolerance for the Sunni population
B. Mughal support for Sikhism
C. Ottoman development of shariah
D. The Thirty Years' War

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Father Simon describes a powerful, disciplined, professional army paid year-round and equipped with muskets. This military capacity was built by the Safavids in part *because* of their intense conflict with the Sunni Ottoman Empire — driven by the Safavid imposition of Shia Islam on a formerly Sunni population and the resulting permanent state of hostility. The Safavid military was built for war with the Ottomans and for internal suppression of Sunni resistance. The Thirty Years' War (D) was a European conflict beginning in 1618 — after this 1588 report. Mughal support for Sikhism (B) and Ottoman shariah (C) are unrelated to Safavid military development.

**Question 3.** This source supports the claim that the Safavids were similar to the Ottomans and the Mughals in that all three:

A. used gunpowder weapons
B. practiced identical religious beliefs
C. threatened to attack regions of Europe
D. were ahead of the Europeans in military power

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage explicitly mentions muskets — gunpowder weapons — as central to Safavid military power. All three Gunpowder Empires (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal) are named precisely because their military power rested on gunpowder technology. B is wrong — they had very different religious beliefs (Sunni, Shia, syncretic Mughal). C is wrong — the Mughals did not threaten Europe. D is wrong — Father Simon says Safavid soldiers are "little *behind*" Europeans, implying Europeans were ahead or equal, not behind.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO p. 175. Refer to the following passage.*

> "Let no one think that this Commandment entirely forbids the arts of painting, engraving, or sculpture. The Scriptures inform us that God Himself commanded to be made images of Cherubim [a category of angel], and also the brazen serpent. The interpretation, therefore, at which we must arrive, is that images are prohibited only inasmuch as they are used as deities to receive adoration, and so to injure the true worship of God. . . . He [the pastor] will also inform the unlettered . . . of the use of images, that they are intended to instruct in the history of the Old and New Testaments, and to revive from time to time their memory that thus, moved by the contemplation of heavenly things, we may be the more ardently inflamed to adore and love God Himself. He should, also, point out that the images of the Saints are placed in churches, not only to be honored, but also that they may admonish us by their examples to imitate their lives and virtues."
>
> — Council of Trent: Catechism for Parish Priests, 1566

**SAQ 1A.** Identify ONE way in which the passage reflects a response to the Protestant Reformation during the period 1500 to 1600.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization/Identification
> The passage reflects a direct response to the Protestant Reformation's rejection of Catholic religious imagery. Protestant reformers (especially Calvinists) condemned the use of religious images as idolatry, arguing that veneration of paintings and statues violated the Second Commandment. The Council of Trent's 1566 Catechism explicitly defends the use of religious images — arguing they are not idols but educational and devotional tools — which is a direct Counter-Reformation rebuttal to Protestant iconoclasm. The defensive framing ("Let no one think that this Commandment entirely forbids...") reveals the Church is answering a Protestant argument, not originating a new doctrine.

**SAQ 1B.** Explain ONE way in which the passage reflects how the centralization of states impacted the role of religion during the period 1450 to 1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation/Explanation
> The passage shows the Catholic Church attempting to standardize religious practice through a single authoritative text (a Catechism distributed to parish priests), which reflects the Counter-Reformation's response to the loss of religious monopoly caused by state-sponsored Protestantism. As monarchs like Henry VIII and German princes broke from Rome and took control of local churches, the Catholic Church had to centralize and systematize its own teachings to compete. The Catechism is an instrument of religious centralization — it tells priests exactly what to teach on contested topics. This is the Church behaving more like a centralized state bureaucracy: issuing standardized instructions to local officials (priests) to maintain doctrinal uniformity.

**SAQ 1C.** Explain ONE way in which the passage reflects how the challenges to the Roman Catholic Church were similar to the religious challenges in the Gunpowder Empires between 1500 and 1600.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Like the Catholic Church defending its authority against Protestant challenges in Europe, the Sunni Ottoman Empire faced challenges from the Safavid imposition of Shia Islam. In both cases, a dominant religious establishment faced a rival claim to religious legitimacy. The Council of Trent's defensive posture — clarifying doctrine, standardizing teachings — mirrors how the Ottoman ulama worked to define and defend Sunni orthodoxy against Safavid Shia claims. In both the European and Islamic cases, religious schism forced the dominant institution to articulate more precisely what it believed and to use state power to enforce those beliefs.

## Historical Reasoning Practice

### Think As a Historian: Point of View

*AMSCO p. 176*

**Exercise 1 — Protestant Reformation.** In each pair below, identify which statement more clearly represents a *point of view* (not just neutral description) and explain the POV:

A. "Paris is well worth a Mass," King Henry IV wrote in the 16th century, shedding light on how he and other monarchs of his time were willing to bargain and compromise, ruling with practicality rather than theology.

B. A Roman Catholic monk, Martin Luther, presented a set of objections to various Church practices, a document known as the 95 Theses, to Church leaders in Wittenberg, Germany, on October 31, 1517.

> [!tip] Model Response — Point of View
> Statement A more clearly represents a point of view. The author frames Henry IV's conversion as evidence of *pragmatic political calculation* over sincere faith — using words like "bargain," "compromise," and "practicality rather than theology." This framing reflects a *secular/modern perspective* that interprets 16th-century religious decisions through a political lens. A historian with strong religious convictions might frame Henry IV's conversion very differently — as apostasy, or as a genuine act of faith. Statement B is purely descriptive: it states what Luther did, when, and where, without interpretation. Point of view requires an interpretive lens — B has none.

**Exercise 2 — Religion and Toleration.**

A. The final great religious war was the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648), which involved the Netherlands, Denmark, Sweden, France, and Spain.

B. "The humanist project for changing the world was soon caught up in a 'theological road-rage' of grimly competing religious orthodoxies. The liberation symbolized by 1517 took effect in the savage suppression of the Peasants' Revolt, and in the relentless oppression of radicals by Protestants and Catholics alike, culminating in the evisceration of Europe in the Thirty Years' War." — Fleur Houston

> [!tip] Model Response — Point of View
> Statement B (Fleur Houston) clearly represents a point of view. Houston's language is charged: "theological road-rage," "savage suppression," "relentless oppression," "evisceration." She frames the Reformation not as religious liberation but as a cycle of violence that betrayed humanist hopes. Her POV reflects a *critical, revisionist perspective* — likely influenced by later scholarship that emphasizes the Reformation's destructive social consequences rather than its theological achievements. The vivid, negative language suggests Houston's values prioritize human suffering over doctrinal progress. Statement A is neutral description with no evaluative stance.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

**In one to three paragraphs, explain how different belief systems endured or changed during the period from 1450–1750.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> The period 1450–1750 was an era of profound religious transformation across Eurasia, characterized by both the fracturing of established traditions and the emergence of new ones. Christianity, which had dominated Western Europe as a unified Catholic institution since the fall of Rome, shattered into competing Protestant denominations — Lutheranism, Calvinism, and Anglicanism — each shaped by political forces as much as theological ones. The Protestant Reformation (1517) and the subsequent Wars of Religion culminated in the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which institutionalized religious pluralism in Europe and weakened the Catholic Church's claim to universal authority. Yet the Church itself endured, using the Counter-Reformation to retain its dominance in southern Europe and extend its reach globally through Jesuit missionary activity.
>
> In the Islamic world, the ancient Sunni-Shia divide hardened from a theological disagreement into a geopolitical fault line when Shah Ismail I imposed Twelver Shia Islam on Persia in 1501. The Ottoman Empire, aligned with Sunni orthodoxy, and the Safavid Empire, built on Shia identity, were locked in a rivalry that gave religious difference the force of state conflict. Meanwhile, the Mughal Empire under Akbar (r. 1556–1605) demonstrated a radically different path: religious syncretism and deliberate tolerance of Hinduism, Christianity, and the newly founded Sikhism. Sikhism itself represents the era's clearest example of religious creation through cultural contact — blending Hindu devotional tradition with Sufi Islamic mysticism to form a new monotheistic faith.
>
> Across both Christian and Islamic contexts, a clear pattern emerges: belief systems endured not as static institutions but as contested identities that rulers shaped to serve political purposes. The result was not religious decline but religious multiplication — more denominations, more official schisms, and, paradoxically, the beginning of a secular challenge in the form of the Scientific Revolution's empirical epistemology.
