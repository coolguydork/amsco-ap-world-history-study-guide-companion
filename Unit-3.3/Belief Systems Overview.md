# Topic 3.3 — Empires: Belief Systems

> [!summary] Essential Question
> How did different belief systems **endure or change** during the period from 1450–1750?

## Religion as a Double-Edged Sword

Religion in this period was both a **unifying force** and a **divisive one**. Rulers used it to legitimize their authority (see [[Unit-3.2/Empires Administrations Overview]]), but religious conflict tore apart empires and triggered some of the bloodiest wars in European and Islamic history. Understanding the religious landscape of 1450–1750 is essential for understanding why empires rose, stabilized, and fell.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests **religious change and continuity** in Period 2. Key patterns: (1) Christianity splits into Protestant and Catholic factions in Europe; (2) Islam splits deeper into Sunni vs. Shi'a in the Middle East; (3) Sikhism emerges as a syncretic new religion in India. Be able to explain **cause** of each split and **consequence** for political power.

## The Protestant Reformation

### Background: Why the Church Was Vulnerable

By the early 16th century, the Roman Catholic Church faced mounting pressures:
- **Corruption**: selling of indulgences (remission of sin's punishment) and simony (selling church offices)
- **Political** challenge: centralizing monarchs wanted Church lands and authority
- **Theological** challenge: earlier reformers like John Wycliffe (England) and Jan Hus (Bohemia) had attacked Church practices; Hus was burned at the stake
- **Credibility loss**: the "Babylonian Captivity" (1309–1377) — when the papacy relocated to France — made the Church look politically compromised
- **Black Death failure**: the Church's inability to explain or stop the plague undermined faith in its spiritual authority

> [!tip]
> The Gutenberg printing press (c. 1450) was *crucial* to the Reformation's success. Luther's 95 Theses could be copied and distributed in days. Without the press, the Reformation might have been suppressed like Wycliffe's or Hus's earlier movements. **Technology enabled religious revolution.**

### Lutheranism

**Martin Luther** — a monk in Wittenberg, Germany (within the Holy Roman Empire, 800–1806) — concluded that several Church practices violated Scripture:
- **Indulgences** granted absolution from sin's punishment for money
- **Simony** sold Church offices to the highest bidder

In response, Luther nailed his **95 Theses** to a church door, challenging these practices. His theological core: **"sola fide"** (faith alone) — salvation came through faith in God, not through purchasing indulgences or the mediation of priests.

Pope Leo X **excommunicated** Luther in January 1521. But German political leaders saw an opportunity to break from papal authority — they sided with Luther. A theological debate became a political fracture.

**Social impact of Lutheranism:**
- Women could have direct access to God — promoted **women's literacy**
- Women had significant family roles, especially teaching children to read the Bible
- But Protestant churches didn't maintain convents — Catholic women had leadership opportunities in convents that Protestant women lacked

### Calvinism

French theologian **John Calvin** broke with the Catholic Church c. 1530 and helped reform the religious community in Geneva, Switzerland. Key ideas:
- **Predestination**: God had already decided who was "elect" (destined for heaven)
- The elect ran the community; governance by church elders
- Simple church buildings and plain living (no Catholic ornamentation)

Calvin's followers:
- **Huguenots** in France
- **Reformed Church of Scotland** (John Knox)
- **Puritans** in England — wanted to "purify" the Church of England of Catholic remnants

The sociologist Max Weber connected Calvinism to the **"Protestant work ethic"**: Calvinists worked hard and reinvested profits because prosperity was interpreted as a sign of being among the elect. This ethic arguably contributed to the rise of early capitalism.

### Anglicanism

The third major Reformation strand arose in **England**. King **Henry VIII** (ruled 1509–1547) wanted a male heir. When his wife produced only daughters, Henry asked the pope to annul his marriage so he could remarry. The pope refused (worried about the Holy Roman Emperor's reaction). 

With Parliament's approval, Henry declared himself head of the new **Church of England (Anglican Church)** — independent of papal authority.

> [!warning]
> Henry VIII's break with Rome was **political, not theological**. He didn't start as a Protestant reformer; he wanted a divorce. The result, however, was a genuinely Protestant church that would go through decades of further change under his successors.

## The Counter-Reformation (Catholic Reformation)

The Catholic Church did not accept the Reformation passively. It launched a vigorous **Counter-Reformation** using three main tools:

1. **The Inquisition** — established in the late 12th century to root out heresy; the Counter-Reformation expanded its use; torture was sometimes permitted

2. **The Jesuits** (Society of Jesus) — religious order founded in 1540 by **Ignatius of Loyola**; actively opposed Protestantism; ran missionary activity throughout the Spanish Empire, and in Japan and India

3. **The Council of Trent** (1545–1563) — corrected the worst abuses (simony, indulgence sales); reaffirmed Catholic sacraments and rituals; published the *Index of Prohibited Books* (banning Protestant Bibles and Copernicus's writings)

The Counter-Reformation was successful: Catholicism remained dominant near the Mediterranean. And because colonial powers followed the home country's religion, most Spanish, Portuguese, and French colonies became Catholic.

**Philip II** of Spain took the Catholic crusade to the Netherlands (1556–1581) and tried to conquer England. His **Spanish Armada** was defeated by English naval power in **1588**.

## Wars of Religion

Religious division produced catastrophic conflict in Europe:

**Schmalkaldic War (1546–1547)** — Charles V vs. the German Lutheran Schmalkaldic League. Result: the **Peace of Augsburg (1555)** — each German state could choose Catholic or Lutheran. Subjects had to follow the state's religion (or leave).

**French Wars of Religion** — Catholics and Huguenots fought for decades. King Henry IV (a Protestant) converted to Catholicism in 1593 to unify France: *"Paris is well worth a Mass."* In 1598, he issued the **Edict of Nantes** — religious toleration for Huguenots for 87 years. In 1685, Louis XIV revoked it; thousands of skilled Protestant craftsmen fled France, damaging the French economy.

**Thirty Years' War (1618–1648)** — The final great Catholic-Protestant war. Started as religious conflict in the Holy Roman Empire; became a broader European power struggle. Caused economic catastrophe, widespread famine and disease. Ended with the **Peace of Westphalia** — each area of the Holy Roman Empire could choose Catholicism, Lutheranism, or Calvinism. This gave states much more autonomy; **Prussia** and **Austria** began asserting themselves. Prussia's wartime suffering led to the development of a strong military tradition that would shape European history into the 20th century.

## The Orthodox Church and Russia

In Russia, Peter the Great asserted authority over the **Orthodox Church** (see [[Unit-3.2/Empires Administrations Overview]]):
- Abolished the position of **patriarch** (head of the Church)
- Established the **Holy Synod** — clergymen overseen by a secular official answering to the tsar
- Raised minimum age for monks to 50 (preferred young men serve first as soldiers)
- Reforms opposed by peasants and **"Old Believers"** (a sect resisting earlier liturgical reforms)

This incorporated the Church into the state — one of Peter's most controversial acts.

## Islamic Religious Schisms

### Ottoman Empire: Sunni Dominance

Before 1453, the region around Istanbul was controlled by the **Byzantine Empire** and Orthodox Christianity. After Mehmed II's conquest, the dominant religion became Islam. A **sultan** replaced the emperor; **shariah** (Islamic law governing criminal justice, marriage, inheritance, and other matters) replaced Byzantine Justinian Law.

The Ottoman Empire was predominantly **Sunni Muslim**, though Suleiman showed some tolerance in the empire's early centuries.

### Safavid Empire: Shi'a State Religion

Shah Ismail used **Shi'a Islam** as a unifying force — denying legitimacy to any Sunni ruler. This state religion:
- Created internal unity and ideological distinction from the Sunnis
- Caused **constant hostility with the Sunni Ottoman Empire**
- The Safavid-Ottoman border (at Tabriz) became the Sunni-Shi'a dividing line — visible today in Iraq-Iran relations

> [!exam-tip]
> The **Sunni-Shi'a split** originated in 632 CE — a dispute over Muhammad's rightful successor. The Safavids institutionalized the split politically for the first time, turning a theological difference into a **geopolitical divide**. This is a classic example of continuity (old religious dispute) + change (new political form).

### Mughal India: Tolerance and its Limits

**Akbar** tolerated *all* religions:
- Gave money and land to both Hindus and Muslims
- Funded a Catholic church in Goa
- Provided land grants for **Sikhism** (a new monotheistic faith combining Hindu and Islamic Sufi influences)
- Gave Hindus positions in his government (zamindars could be Hindu)
- Exempted Hindus from the poll tax paid by non-Muslims
- Invited Catholic priests to Delhi to discuss Christianity
- Tried (unsuccessfully) to prohibit child marriage and sati (the ritual suicide of widows)
- Promoted a syncretic religion called **Din-i Ilahi** ("divine faith") — failed to catch on

Under **Aurangzeb** (1658–1707): reversed Akbar's tolerance. Reinstated poll taxes on non-Muslims, alienated Sikhs and Hindus, triggered revolts. His intolerance accelerated Mughal decline.

**Sikhism** — Founded in the Punjab region of India, combining Hindu and Sufi Muslim influences. Monotheistic; recognized the rights of other faiths. By the 21st century, the world's fifth most popular religion.

## The Scientific Revolution

In the early 1600s, scientific thinking spread through northern Europe, shaped by:
- **Empiricism** (Francis Bacon, 1620): knowledge must be based on observable, testable evidence — not tradition or revelation
- **Isaac Newton's *Principia*** (1687): combined Galileo's terrestrial motion laws and Kepler's planetary laws into a theory of **gravitational force**

The Royal Academies of Science in France and England formalized scientific exchange. Many intellectuals concluded that the world was **ordered and rational** — governed by natural laws. This thinking was the direct foundation of the **[[Concept Notes/Enlightenment\|Enlightenment]]** (see [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment and Political Revolutions]]).

> [!tip]
> The Scientific Revolution is placed in Topic 3.3 because it *emerged from* the same Period 2 context — the religious wars loosened the Church's grip on intellectual life, and the printing press spread new ideas rapidly. Science didn't replace religion overnight, but it created an alternative framework for understanding the world.

## Continuity and Change

**Continuity**: Religion remained the primary ideological framework for legitimate authority across all empires. Whether the caliph's succession, the divine right of kings, or Akbar's syncretic Divine Faith, rulers consistently grounded their authority in religious language and symbolism.

**Change**: For the first time, **major world religions fractured** in ways that became permanently institutionalized. The Catholic-Protestant split was not healed; the Sunni-Shi'a divide deepened into a geopolitical reality; Sikhism emerged as a new world religion. Religious pluralism became a political reality that rulers had to manage.
