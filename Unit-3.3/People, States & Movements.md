# People, States & Movements — Topic 3.3: Empires: Belief Systems

---

## Key People

**Martin Luther (1483–1546)**
German Augustinian monk whose 95 Theses (1517) sparked the [[Concept Notes/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. He challenged indulgences, simony, and the Church's role as mediator of salvation (*sola fide*). Excommunicated by Pope Leo X in 1521, he found protection among German princes who used his movement for political gain. Luther promoted women's literacy and Bible reading in the vernacular, but he did not support the German Peasants' Revolt — he was a religious, not a social, revolutionary. (AMSCO p. 168)

**John Calvin (1509–1564)**
French theologian who broke with Rome around 1530 and developed the doctrine of predestination. Established a strict Calvinist community in Geneva that served as a model for Reformed churches across Europe. His theology motivated the Huguenots (France), the Puritans (England/America), and the Reformed Church of Scotland. His "Protestant work ethic" concept was later analyzed by Max Weber as a driver of capitalism. (AMSCO p. 168)

**Henry VIII of England (r. 1509–1547)**
Created the Church of England (Anglican Church) in 1534 primarily for political and dynastic reasons — the pope refused to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. With Parliament's approval, he declared himself head of the new church. His break with Rome was motivated by personal and political concerns, not theological ones, distinguishing Anglicanism's origins from Lutheranism and Calvinism. (AMSCO p. 169)

**Henry IV of France / Henry of Navarre (r. 1589–1610)**
Protestant-raised king who converted to Catholicism in 1593 to unify France, reportedly saying "Paris is well worth a Mass." Issued the Edict of Nantes (1598) granting Huguenots limited religious freedom. A model of pragmatic, politically motivated religious decision-making. (AMSCO pp. 167, 171)

**Peter the Great of Russia (r. 1682–1725)**
Subordinated the Russian Orthodox Church to state control. Abolished the position of patriarch and replaced it with the Holy Synod, a state-supervised church board. Raised the minimum age for monks to 50 to ensure young men served the military first. His reforms generated fierce opposition from peasants and Old Believers. (AMSCO pp. 169–170)

**Akbar (r. 1556–1605)**
Greatest of the Mughal emperors and the period's most notable practitioner of religious tolerance. Removed the jizya tax, employed Hindus at all government levels, married Hindu wives, invited Catholic priests to court, and provided land grants to Sikhism. Sponsored Din-i-Ilahi (a syncretic blend of Islam and Hinduism) but failed to convert anyone to it. Tried and failed to ban sati and child marriage. Regarded as one of history's outstanding rulers. (AMSCO pp. 172–173)

**Shah Ismail I (r. 1501–1524)**
Founder of the Safavid dynasty. Imposed Twelver Shia Islam on a predominantly Sunni Persian population, using it as a state identity marker and political weapon against the Ottomans. Forced conversion of Sunnis and built a Shia religious establishment to legitimize Safavid rule. His policy permanently entrenched the Sunni-Shia divide as a geopolitical fault line. (AMSCO p. 173)

**Guru Nanak (1469–1539)**
Founder of Sikhism in the Punjab around 1499. His teachings blended Hindu devotional (bhakti) traditions with Sufi Islamic mysticism, creating a new monotheistic faith that rejected caste hierarchy and accepted the validity of other religions. Sikhism emerged directly from the Hindu-Muslim contact zone of Mughal India. (AMSCO p. 172; see [[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]], [[Concept Notes/Bhakti Movement|Bhakti Movement]])

**Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556)**
Spanish soldier-turned-priest who founded the Jesuits (Society of Jesus) in 1540. The Jesuits became the Catholic Church's most effective missionary and educational arm, spreading Catholicism to the Spanish Americas, Japan, India, and China. (AMSCO p. 170)

## Key States

**Ottoman Empire (Sunni)**
After conquering Constantinople (1453), the Ottomans became the dominant Sunni power, replacing Byzantine law with shariah. Relatively tolerant under Suleiman (dhimmi system for Christians and Jews), but tolerance declined under later rulers. Their Sunni identity brought them into permanent conflict with the Shia Safavids. (See [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & [[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires\|Gunpowder Empires]] Expand Overview|European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview]])

**Safavid Empire (Shia)**
Imposed Twelver Shia Islam as a state religion under Shah Ismail I (1501). Used Shia identity to unify Persia and legitimize Safavid rule against Sunni Ottoman claims. Forcibly converted the population; no tolerance for Sunni practice. Created a Shia clerical establishment (ulama) with significant political power.

**Mughal Empire (Sunni, syncretic under Akbar)**
Ruled a Hindu-majority population as a Sunni Muslim dynasty. Under Akbar, adopted a policy of wide religious tolerance — removing the jizya, supporting Sikhism, employing Hindus. Later rulers reversed many of these policies, particularly Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707), who re-imposed the jizya and pursued more orthodox policies.

**Holy Roman Empire (Catholic/Lutheran/Calvinist)**
Fragmented across the Reformation era. The Peace of Augsburg (1555) and Peace of Westphalia (1648) formalized its religious diversity and increased princely autonomy, weakening central imperial authority. Prussia and Austria emerged as dominant states.

## Key Movements

**Protestant Reformation**
Pan-European movement challenging Catholic authority on theological, political, and social grounds. Three main branches: Lutheranism (German states, Scandinavia), Calvinism (France, Switzerland, Scotland, Netherlands, New England), Anglicanism (England). All used the printing press to spread ideas rapidly.

**Counter-Reformation (Catholic Reformation)**
The Catholic Church's response: Inquisition, Jesuits, Council of Trent. Successfully retained Catholicism in southern Europe and extended it to the Americas and parts of Asia.

**Sikhism**
New religious movement founded in Punjab c. 1499 combining Hindu and Sufi elements. Monotheistic, casteless, universalist. Grew into a significant minority community under Mughal rule and remains the world's fifth largest religion. A textbook example of [[Concept Notes/Syncretism|syncretism]].

**Scientific Revolution**
Intellectual movement using observation and reason to challenge authority. Began in northern Europe in the early 1600s. Connected to the Reformation era's questioning of traditional authority, though it challenged religious rather than political power.

> [!tip]
> Comparison power move for the AP exam: contrast how the **Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal** empires each used religion differently as a tool of state legitimacy. Ottomans = selective Sunni tolerance; Safavids = forced Shia uniformity; Mughals (Akbar) = syncretic tolerance. All three used religion politically, but with opposite approaches.
