# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 3.3: Empires: Belief Systems

---

## Protestant Reformation Terms

**Protestant Reformation**
The 16th-century movement that fractured Western Christianity into Catholic and Protestant branches. Triggered by theological disputes (faith vs. works, Church authority) and exploited by monarchs seeking to break free from papal power. (AMSCO pp. 167–169)

**95 Theses**
Martin Luther's 1517 list of objections to Catholic practices, especially indulgences and simony. Nailed to the church door in Wittenberg, it ignited the Reformation. The printing press allowed it to spread rapidly.

**Indulgences**
Certificates sold by the Catholic Church that granted absolution from punishment for sins. Luther's opposition to this practice was a central grievance of the Reformation.

**Simony**
The corrupt practice of buying and selling Church offices. Another of Luther's key objections. Widespread simony eroded Church moral authority.

**Sola Fide**
Latin for "faith alone" — Luther's doctrine that salvation comes through faith, not through Church sacraments or purchased indulgences. This directly undermined the institutional Church's role.

**Lutheranism**
The Protestant denomination founded by Martin Luther's theology. Spread through German-speaking lands, partly because princes adopted it to gain political independence from Rome and seize Church lands.

**Calvinism**
John Calvin's form of Protestantism centered on predestination (God has foreordained who is saved). Calvin established a theocratic community in Geneva. His followers include Huguenots (France), Puritans (England/America), and the Reformed Church of Scotland.

**Protestant Work Ethic**
Concept identified by sociologist Max Weber: Calvinist theology encouraged hard work and reinvestment of profit as signs of divine favor. Often cited as a cultural factor in the rise of capitalism.

**Anglicanism / Church of England**
Protestant denomination created by Henry VIII in 1534 when he broke from Rome to gain control over his marriage (and Church lands). England's monarch serves as head of the Church of England.

**Huguenots**
French Calvinist Protestants. Fought a decades-long religious civil war against French Catholics, temporarily protected by the 1598 Edict of Nantes, then driven into exile when Louis XIV revoked it in 1685.

**Puritans**
English Calvinist reformers who sought to "purify" the Church of England of Catholic elements. A faction emigrated to New England in the 1620s–1630s. Important connection to early American history.

## Catholic Response Terms

**Counter-Reformation (Catholic Reformation)**
The Catholic Church's multi-pronged response to Protestantism: expanded Inquisition, founding of the Jesuits, and the Council of Trent. Successfully halted Protestant expansion in southern and central Europe. (AMSCO p. 170)

**Inquisition**
Church court established in the 12th century to identify and punish heretics. Expanded significantly during the Counter-Reformation; sometimes used torture to extract confessions.

**Jesuits (Society of Jesus)**
Religious order founded in 1540 by Ignatius of Loyola. Emphasized education, missionary activity, and rigorous training. Spread Catholicism throughout the Spanish Empire, Japan, India, and China.

**Council of Trent (1545–1563)**
Catholic Church council that corrected clerical abuses, reaffirmed Church doctrine, and reformed Church governance. Strengthened Catholicism without conceding to Protestant theology.

## Religious Conflict Terms

**Peace of Augsburg (1555)**
Treaty that ended the first round of German religious wars. Established the principle of *cuius regio, eius religio* ("whose realm, his religion") — each German prince could choose Catholicism or Lutheranism for their territory.

**Cuius Regio, Eius Religio**
Latin principle meaning "whoever rules, his religion." The foundational rule of the Peace of Augsburg. Required subjects to follow their ruler's religion or emigrate. Gave rulers enormous religious authority over their people.

**Edict of Nantes (1598)**
Henry IV's decree granting French Huguenots limited religious freedom. Represented a pragmatic attempt to end religious civil war. Revoked by Louis XIV in 1685, causing mass Huguenot emigration and economic disruption.

**Thirty Years' War (1618–1648)**
Europe's final and most destructive religious war, fought mainly in the Holy Roman Empire. Caused massive population loss from war, famine, and disease. Ended with the Peace of Westphalia.

**Peace of Westphalia (1648)**
Ended the Thirty Years' War. Extended religious choice to Calvinism (in addition to Catholicism and Lutheranism). Increased autonomy of German princes; strengthened Prussia and Austria. Often cited as the beginning of the modern state system.

## Islamic Schism Terms

**Shariah**
Islamic legal code governing all aspects of life — criminal justice, marriage, inheritance, social conduct. Implemented by the Ottomans after conquering Constantinople; replaced Byzantine civil law.

**Sunni-Shia Split (632 CE)**
Division in Islam over who should succeed Muhammad as leader of the Muslim community. Sunnis accepted Abu Bakr; Shia believed succession should stay in Muhammad's family (Ali). Became a defining fault line in the Ottoman-Safavid rivalry.

**Twelver Shia Islam**
The branch of Shia Islam that recognizes twelve imams as Muhammad's rightful successors. Shah Ismail imposed it on Persia (Safavid Empire) starting in 1501, making it a state religion and political identity marker.

**Jizya**
Poll tax on non-Muslims living in an Islamic state (dhimmis). The Mughal emperor Akbar notably abolished it to promote religious tolerance among Hindus.

## Mughal / Sikhism Terms

**Din-i-Ilahi ("Divine Faith")**
Syncretic religion promoted by Akbar blending Islam and Hinduism. Gained few converts and dissolved after Akbar's death in 1605. Represents the extreme of Mughal religious experimentation.

**Sikhism**
Monotheistic religion founded c. 1499 by Guru Nanak in the Punjab. Blended Hindu devotional traditions with Islamic Sufi mysticism. Rejected caste hierarchy, recognized the validity of other faiths, and became the world's fifth most practiced religion. A key AP example of [[Concept Notes/Syncretism|syncretism]]. (See also [[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]], [[Concept Notes/Bhakti Movement|Bhakti Movement]])

**Sati**
Hindu practice in which widows immolate themselves on their husbands' funeral pyres. Akbar tried and failed to prohibit it — an example of the limits of Mughal imperial authority over deeply embedded cultural practices.

**Zamindars**
Tax collectors and local administrators in the Mughal Empire. Akbar allowed Hindus to serve at all levels of the zamindar hierarchy, a policy of deliberate inclusion.

## Scientific Revolution Terms

**Empiricism**
Method of knowledge built on observation and evidence rather than faith or tradition. Developed by Francis Bacon (1620). Represents the shift toward rational, systematic investigation of the natural world.

**Scientific Revolution**
17th-century intellectual movement challenging traditional authority with reason and evidence. Key figures: Bacon (empiricism), Galileo (terrestrial motion), Kepler (planetary motion), Newton (*Principia*, 1687 — gravitational theory). Laid groundwork for the Enlightenment (Topic 5.1).
