# Empires: Belief Systems

**Essential Question:** How did different belief systems endure or change during the period from 1450–1750?

Religion was both a unifying force and a divisive one in this era. Christianity fractured across Europe, Islam deepened its Sunni-Shia split, and new syncretic faiths like Sikhism emerged from contact between traditions. Across all cases, rulers instrumentalized religion — tolerating, suppressing, or co-opting belief systems to consolidate state power. (AMSCO pp. 167–173)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves this topic for comparison questions: Ottoman (Sunni, selective tolerance) vs. Safavid (Shia, forced conversion) vs. Mughal (syncretic tolerance under Akbar). Know the contrasts cold.

## The [[Concept Notes/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]] in Europe

The Roman Catholic Church had long dominated European life, but by the early 1500s, corruption, theological dissent, and the ambitions of centralizing monarchs had weakened its authority. The Babylonian Captivity (1309–1377), when the papacy relocated to France under French royal influence, damaged the Church's credibility. Reformers like John Wycliffe and Jan Hus had already challenged clerical authority before Luther — Hus was burned at the stake for it.

**Lutheranism** emerged when Martin Luther, a monk in Wittenberg, objected to the sale of indulgences and simony (selling church offices). His 95 Theses (1517) sparked a break. Luther's doctrine of *sola fide* (faith alone for salvation) challenged the Church's role as mediator. Pope Leo X excommunicated Luther in 1521. German princes saw opportunity — by siding with Luther, they could seize Church lands and assert independence from Rome. Luther's emphasis on Bible literacy had social effects: it promoted women's literacy, though Protestantism also closed convents, removing an avenue for women's institutional leadership.

**Calvinism** followed when John Calvin broke with Rome around 1530. His doctrine of predestination (the elect are foreordained for salvation) shaped a community of plain living and disciplined work in Geneva. The "Protestant work ethic" — reinvesting profits as a sign of God's favor — would have long socioeconomic consequences. Calvin's followers spread as Huguenots (France), Reformed Church of Scotland (led by John Knox), and Puritans (England and New England).

**Anglicanism** arose from politics, not theology. Henry VIII needed a male heir and a papal annulment the pope refused to grant. With Parliament's approval, Henry declared himself head of the Church of England in 1534, severing ties with Rome.

## Wars of Religion in Europe

Europe's fragmentation into Catholic and Protestant states drove decades of conflict. The 1555 **Peace of Augsburg** introduced the principle of *cuius regio, eius religio* — the ruler's religion determines the territory's religion — temporarily pausing German conflicts. France suffered nearly fifty years of Catholic-Huguenot warfare until Henry IV (a Protestant who converted to Catholicism to unify France) issued the **Edict of Nantes** in 1598, granting Huguenots limited religious freedom. Louis XIV revoked it in 1685, sending skilled Huguenot craftsmen fleeing across Europe.

The **Thirty Years' War** (1618–1648) was the final and most destructive religious conflict, spreading famine, disease, and economic ruin across the Holy Roman Empire. The **Peace of Westphalia** (1648) ended it by expanding the options to Catholicism, Lutheranism, or Calvinism — and crucially, it increased the autonomy of German princes and smaller states, fragmenting the Holy Roman Empire further and empowering states like Prussia and Austria.

> [!tip]
> The Thirty Years' War is also significant as a turning point toward *secular* state sovereignty — after Westphalia, religion was less central to interstate relations. This connects forward to [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview]] (state-building) and backward to [[Unit-1.6/Medieval Europe Overview|Medieval Europe Overview]] (Church authority).

## The Orthodox Church and Russia

Peter the Great brought the Russian Orthodox Church firmly under state control. He abolished the position of patriarch and replaced it with the **Holy Synod**, a board of clergy overseen by a secular official answering to the tsar. He also raised the minimum age for monks to 50, preferring young men serve the military first. These reforms met fierce resistance from peasants and **Old Believers**, a conservative sect.

## The Counter-Reformation

The Catholic Church responded to the Protestant challenge with a three-pronged Counter-Reformation: (1) expanding the **Inquisition** to root out heresy; (2) founding the **Jesuits** (Society of Jesus, est. 1540 by Ignatius of Loyola), who undertook missionary work throughout the Spanish Empire, Japan, and India; and (3) convening the **Council of Trent** (1545–1563), which corrected clerical abuses, clarified doctrine, and reaffirmed Catholic teachings against Protestant critiques. The strategy worked — Catholicism remained the world's largest Christian denomination.

## Islamic Religious Schisms: Ottoman vs. Safavid

The Sunni-Shia split (632 CE, over succession to Muhammad) intensified dramatically in this era as the Ottoman and Safavid empires adopted it as a state identity marker.

The **Ottoman Empire** was Sunni. After conquering Constantinople (1453), it replaced Byzantine Orthodox Christianity with Islam and Justinian Law with **shariah** (Islamic law governing criminal justice, marriage, inheritance, and more). The Ottomans were selectively tolerant under Suleiman, less so under later rulers.

The **Safavid Empire** under **Shah Ismail I** (r. 1501) imposed **Twelver Shia Islam** on Persia, forcibly converting the predominantly Sunni population. Shia Islam became the Safavid legitimizing ideology, and Ismail denied any legitimacy to Sunnis. This hardened the Ottoman-Safavid rivalry into sectarian warfare — the two empires clashed repeatedly, making the Sunni-Shia divide geopolitically permanent.

> [!warning]
> A common error: students assume the Mughal Empire was Shia like the Safavids. It was not — the Mughals were Sunni, but under Akbar they practiced wide religious tolerance. The Safavids are the Shia empire.

## Mughal Syncretism and Sikhism

**Akbar** (r. 1556–1605) was arguably the era's most religiously innovative ruler. He removed the *jizya* (poll tax on non-Muslims), gave Hindus high government positions (including zamindars), married Hindu wives, and invited Catholic priests to court for theological debate. He sponsored the syncretic religion **Din-i-Ilahi** ("Divine Faith"), blending Islam and Hinduism — but it attracted few followers and died with him.

Akbar also provided land grants to **Sikhism**, a new monotheistic faith founded around 1499 by **Guru Nanak** in the Punjab. Sikhism blended Hindu devotion traditions with Sufi Islamic mysticism, recognized the validity of other faiths, and rejected caste hierarchy. It became the fifth most practiced religion in the world. (See [[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]], [[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]])

> [!exam-tip]
> Sikhism is a perfect AP example of **religious syncretism** and **new religious movements** arising from cross-cultural contact. Know its origins (Guru Nanak, Punjab, Hindu-Muslim environment) and key features (monotheism, rejection of caste, tolerance).

## The Scientific Revolution (Brief Introduction)

Emerging from the same environment of religious questioning, the **Scientific Revolution** challenged faith-based authority with empirical reason. Francis Bacon (1620) developed **empiricism** — knowledge grounded in observable evidence. Isaac Newton's *Principia* (1687) synthesized Galileo's laws of terrestrial motion with Kepler's planetary laws, establishing gravitational theory. These ideas suggested an ordered, rational universe — laying the groundwork for the Enlightenment (see [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Topic 5.1]]).

## Comparative Summary: Belief Systems and Empire

| Empire | Dominant Faith | Policy Toward Other Religions |
|--------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| Ottoman | Sunni Islam | Selective tolerance (dhimmi system); less tolerance later |
| Safavid | Shia Islam | Forced conversion; no tolerance for Sunnis |
| Mughal (Akbar) | Sunni Islam | Wide tolerance; removed jizya; sponsored Sikhism |
| Holy Roman Empire | Catholic/Lutheran | Cuius regio, eius religio (1555); expanded to Calvinism (1648) |
| Russia (Peter the Great) | Orthodox Christianity | Church subordinated to state; forced modernization |
