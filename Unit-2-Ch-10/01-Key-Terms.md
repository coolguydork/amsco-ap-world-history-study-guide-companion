# Chapter 10: Key Terms — Exchange in the Americas, Africa, and Europe

> [!exam-tip]
> Chapter 10 covers three regions, each with distinct trade systems. The AP exam often asks you to compare across them or contrast them with the Silk Roads and Indian Ocean. Know the **Mali gold-salt trade**, the **Italian city-states**, and the **Americas' isolation** as your three key anchors.

---

## Africa: Trans-Saharan Trade

**Trans-Saharan trade network** — The overland trade system crossing the Sahara Desert, connecting Sub-Saharan West Africa to North Africa and the Mediterranean. (Significance: the primary mechanism connecting West Africa to the Islamic world; gold moved northward; salt, copper, and luxury goods moved southward; Islam spread southward along the same routes.)

**Mali Empire** — The dominant West African state of the 13th–14th centuries, successor to the Ghana Empire, controlling the gold-producing regions of West Africa and the trans-Saharan trade routes. (Significance: the wealthiest state in West Africa at its peak; Mansa Musa's pilgrimage to Mecca (1324–1325) demonstrated Mali's wealth and Muslim identity to the entire Islamic world; key AP comparison point for African states in Unit 1 Ch4.)

**Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337)** — The most famous ruler of the Mali Empire, whose pilgrimage (*hajj*) to Mecca in 1324–1325 with a caravan of thousands and an enormous quantity of gold became legendary across the Islamic world. (Significance: his pilgrimage put Mali on medieval European and Islamic world maps; the gold he distributed caused inflation in Egypt for years — evidence of Mali's extraordinary wealth and the scale of trans-Saharan gold trade.)

**Gold-salt exchange** — The foundational economic relationship of the Trans-Saharan trade, in which West African gold (from the Bambuk and Bure goldfields) was exchanged for Saharan salt (from mines like Taghaza), both of which were scarce and precious in the other's home region. (Significance: one of AP World History's most important examples of complementary resource distribution driving trade; explains Mali's wealth without requiring any other external market.)

**Timbuktu** — A city in the Mali Empire (modern Mali) that grew into a major center of Islamic scholarship and trade at the intersection of the Saharan and Sahel trade routes. (Significance: home to several major madrasas (Islamic universities) including the Sankore Mosque-University; a symbol of African intellectual achievement in the Islamic world; evidence that trans-Saharan trade spread not just goods but also learning and culture.)

**Great Zimbabwe** — A large stone-walled city and kingdom in southern Africa (modern Zimbabwe), the center of a gold-producing state that exported gold to the Swahili coast and into the Indian Ocean network. (Significance: its stone architecture — built without mortar — disproves colonial-era myths that Africans couldn't build permanent structures; evidence of African political complexity and trade participation.)

**Griots** — West African oral historians and storytellers who preserved and transmitted cultural memory, history, and legal traditions within Mali and other Sahelian societies. (Significance: evidence that African societies maintained sophisticated cultural and historical memory without writing; the *Epic of Sundiata* (the story of Mali's founder) was preserved by griots for generations.)

**Camel caravans** — The primary transport technology of the Trans-Saharan trade, using the Bactrian and dromedary camel's ability to carry heavy loads over long distances with minimal water. (Significance: the camel was to Trans-Saharan trade what the dhow was to the Indian Ocean — the enabling technology; without camels, regular trans-Saharan commerce would be impossible.)

## Europe: Commercial Recovery and the Black Death

**Italian city-states (Venice, Genoa, Florence)** — The northern Italian commercial republics that emerged as the western terminal of the Silk Road luxury goods trade during the 12th–14th centuries. (Significance: Venice and Genoa dominated trade in the Mediterranean and into the Black Sea, connecting European markets to Islamic and Asian goods; their commercial innovations (double-entry bookkeeping, marine insurance, banking) transformed European commerce.)

**Hanseatic League** — A commercial and defensive confederation of merchant towns and guilds in northern Europe (based in the Baltic and North Sea regions), founded in the mid-13th century. (Significance: the dominant trading network of northern Europe in this period; controlled the trade in Baltic grain, fish, amber, and timber; represents European commercial development independent of Mediterranean luxury trade.)

**Black Death in Europe** — The bubonic plague epidemic of 1346–1353 that killed approximately 25–50% of Europe's population, transmitted via Silk Road trade routes to the Black Sea and then to Europe via Genoese trading ships. (Significance: the greatest demographic catastrophe in European history; led to labor shortages that eroded serfdom, weakened the Catholic Church's authority, and created social mobility for survivors — unintended consequences that accelerated European social change.)

**Flagellants** — Groups of Christians who publicly beat themselves as penance during the Black Death, believing the plague was divine punishment for sin. (Significance: evidence of the social and psychological trauma of the Black Death; represents one form of religious response to catastrophe; the Church eventually banned the movement.)

**Pogroms against Jews** — Violent persecutions of Jewish communities in Europe, often blamed for spreading the Black Death. (Significance: evidence of how plague trauma fueled scapegoating and religious persecution; the Black Death worsened European antisemitism; Jews were often accused of poisoning wells.)

## The Americas: Regional Networks

**Mesoamerican trade networks** — The exchange systems connecting the Aztec (Mexica) heartland in central Mexico to distant regions through both commercial trade and tribute extraction. (Significance: Aztec pochteca merchants traveled vast distances; Tenochtitlán's market (Tlatelolco) was one of the largest markets in the world at the time of European contact.)

**Pochteca** — Professional long-distance merchants of the Aztec Empire who traveled beyond imperial borders to trade luxury goods (cacao, jade, feathers, obsidian) and also served as spies and diplomatic agents. (Significance: evidence of sophisticated commercial specialization within Aztec society; their guild-like organization and special legal status show the complexity of Mesoamerican commerce.)

**Mit'a** — The Inca system of labor taxation, in which subjects owed a portion of their labor (not goods) to the state; used to build roads, temples, and terraces and to provision armies and state storehouses. (Significance: the Inca's primary economic mechanism; allowed the state to accumulate resources without a market-based economy; the road system built through mit'a is the infrastructure that made Inca exchange possible.)

**Inca road system** — An approximately 25,000-mile network of roads built by the Inca Empire throughout the Andes and Pacific coast, connecting the entire empire to Cuzco. (Significance: the infrastructure of the Inca economic and political system; used by runners (*chasquis*) for communication and by llama caravans for goods transport; one of the great engineering achievements of the pre-Columbian Americas.)

**Mississippian culture** — A mound-building agricultural culture of eastern North America (c. 800–1600 CE), centered on cities like Cahokia (near modern St. Louis), which traded extensively along river networks across the continent. (Significance: evidence of complex political organization and long-distance trade in North America before European contact; Cahokia may have had a population of 20,000–30,000 at its peak — larger than any contemporary European city.)

**Americas' isolation** — The biological and commercial separation of the Americas from the Old World's trade networks. (Significance: the most important fact for understanding why the Columbian Exchange (Unit 4) was so catastrophic — the Americas had no exposure to Old World diseases like smallpox, measles, and influenza, leaving their populations completely vulnerable when contact was made after 1492.)
