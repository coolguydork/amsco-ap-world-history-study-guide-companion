# People, States & Movements — Topic 1.3

*AMSCO pp. 23–32 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Key People

### Lal Ded (Mother Lalla) (1320–1392)
Kashmiri poet-saint whose work opens the AMSCO chapter. Born Hindu, her mystical emphasis on direct experience resonated with Sufi Muslims as well. She embodies the **Hindu-Muslim cultural interaction** that defined South Asia in this period — sometimes tense, sometimes creative. Her poetry illustrates the cross-cultural blending that produced new religious and intellectual movements.

### Rajaraja I (r. 985–1014) — Chola Dynasty
Consolidated the Chola Empire in southern India and built the monumental **Brihadeeswarar Temple** — a symbol of Chola power and Hindu devotion. Demonstrates how southern Indian Hindu kingdoms achieved stability and cultural expression even as the north fragmented.

### Rajendra I (r. 1014–1044) — Chola Dynasty
Extended Chola naval power to Ceylon (Sri Lanka) and mounted naval expeditions into Southeast Asia. Key example of how Indian states projected power through maritime trade and military force, connecting South Asia to the broader Indian Ocean world.

### Jayavarman VII (r. 1181–c. 1218) — Khmer Empire
The most powerful ruler of the Khmer Empire. Built **Angkor Thom** as the new capital and converted the empire from Hinduism to [[Concept Notes/Buddhism\|Buddhism]]. His reign represents both the apex of Khmer power and a key moment of **religious transition** in Southeast Asia. Angkor Thom's temples housed both Hindu and Buddhist artwork — a physical record of the empire's religious evolution.

### Prithviraj III (r. 1178–1192) — Rajput kingdoms
The last major Hindu ruler of Delhi before Muslim conquest. His defeat by Muhammad of Ghori at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192) opened northern India to the Delhi Sultanate. Represents the failure of Rajput disunity to resist organized Muslim military power.

### Al-Beruni (973–1048)
Muslim scholar at the court of Mahmud of Ghazni who wrote extensively about India. **Primary source used in the AMSCO MCQ and SAQ.** His writings show both genuine scholarly curiosity and cultural bias — he criticized Hindu insularity while observing it from the vantage point of a conqueror's court. Good example for discussing **point of view** and **sourcing** on the AP exam.

> [!tip]
> On the AP exam, distinguishing **sea-based** (Srivijaya, Majapahit) from **land-based** (Khmer, Sinhala) Southeast Asian kingdoms is a productive comparison frame. Sea kingdoms taxed trade; land kingdoms taxed agriculture enabled by irrigation.

## Key Movements

### The Bhakti Movement
Began in southern India in the 12th century. Emphasized **personal devotion** (*bhakti* = devotion) to a specific deity, bypassing priestly intermediaries, scripture study, and ritual. Appealed especially to women and low-caste Hindus who were excluded from traditional religious roles. Helped Hinduism spread and adapt as Islam entered the subcontinent. See [[Key Terms & Concepts]].

### Sufi Islam in South and Southeast Asia
Sufi missionaries entered India and Southeast Asia alongside Muslim merchants. Their **tolerance for local religious practices** was the key to their success — converts could adopt Islam while still honoring local customs and deities. Sufis were the primary vectors through which Islam spread peacefully in the region, complementing the earlier violent/political entry of Islam via the Delhi Sultanate. See [[Unit-1.2/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.2]].

### Islam in Southeast Asia
Began with local merchants converting in the 700s CE to improve trading relationships with Muslim partners. Urban phenomenon first. Spread to Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula. Today Indonesia is the world's most populous Muslim country — a direct long-term legacy of this period's trade-driven conversion.

> [!exam-tip]
> When comparing the spread of Islam in South Asia vs. Southeast Asia, note the difference in mechanism: in South Asia, conquest (Delhi Sultanate) preceded and overshadowed peaceful conversion; in Southeast Asia, **trade and Sufi missionaries** were the primary vectors, with conquest playing almost no role.
