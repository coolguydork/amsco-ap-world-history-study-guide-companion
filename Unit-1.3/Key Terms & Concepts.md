# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.3

*AMSCO pp. 23–32 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Political Terms

**Delhi Sultanate** (1206–1526)
Muslim sultanate that governed northern India for ~300 years after conquering Delhi in the early 13th century. Never built an efficient bureaucracy; imposed the *jizya* tax on non-Muslims. Successfully repelled the [[Concept Notes/Mongols\|Mongols]] but fell to the Mughal Empire in 1526. Key political example of decentralized, contested governance in South Asia. (AMSCO pp. 24–25)

**Rajput kingdoms**
Hindu warrior-clan kingdoms of northern India and present-day Pakistan that filled the power vacuum after the Gupta collapse. Constant inter-clan warfare prevented political centralization, leaving northern India vulnerable to Muslim conquest. (AMSCO p. 24)

**Vijayanagara Empire** (c. 1336–1646)
Hindu kingdom of southern India; name means "the victorious city." Founded by two brothers who had converted to Islam under the Delhi Sultanate, then reconverted to Hinduism when they established their own state. Survived until Muslim kingdoms overthrew it in the mid-1500s. (AMSCO p. 24)

**Jizya**
Tax imposed by the Delhi Sultanate on non-Muslim subjects. Symbolized the subordinate status of Hindus and Buddhists under Muslim rule and contributed to resentment of the Sultanate. (AMSCO p. 25)

**Mandala political system**
The political model common in Southeast Asia in which power radiated outward from a center — a king or capital — to subordinate tributary states, rather than being defined by fixed territorial borders.

**Proselytize**
To actively seek converts to one's religion. Islam is a universalizing religion that sought to proselytize, but Muslim rulers found forced conversion ineffective in South Asia; most conversion was voluntary. (AMSCO p. 25)

## Religious & Cultural Terms

**[[Concept Notes/Bhakti Movement\|Bhakti Movement]]**
12th-century Hindu devotional movement originating in southern India. Emphasized personal, emotional connection to a deity over scripture or ritual. Open to women and low-caste Hindus. Parallel to [[Concept Notes/Sufism\|Sufism]] in Islam — both were mystical movements that spread their religions by appealing beyond traditional boundaries. Key figure: poet-saint Mira Bai (16th c.). (AMSCO p. 27)

**Sufism** *(see also [[Unit-1.2/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.2]])*
Mystical branch of Islam emphasizing inner experience and personal connection to Allah. Sufis were crucial missionaries in both South and Southeast Asia because their tolerance for local customs lowered barriers to conversion. (AMSCO p. 29)

**[[Concept Notes/Syncretism\|Syncretism]]**
The blending of two or more cultural or religious traditions. Dominant pattern throughout this topic: Hindu-Buddhist synthesis in Southeast Asia, Hindu-Islamic fusion in northern India's art and architecture.

**Caste system (*jati*)**
India's hereditary social hierarchy, the strongest continuity in South Asian social history. Proved remarkably flexible — absorbed Muslim merchants into occupational subcastes. Conversion to Islam did not allow low-caste Hindus to escape it without accompanying economic opportunity. (AMSCO p. 26)

**Sanskrit as lingua franca**
Sanskrit served as the common language of educated and religious discourse across South and Southeast Asia, facilitating the spread of Hindu and Buddhist ideas.

**Urdu**
Hybrid language that emerged in Muslim northern India — Hindi grammatical structure + Arabic and Farsi vocabulary. Product of the cultural fusion of Hindu and Islamic traditions. Today the official language of Pakistan. (AMSCO p. 27)

**Sati**
Hindu practice of widow self-immolation on a husband's funeral pyre. Referenced in the SAQ source document for this topic (the Vikrama stories). Illustrates the severely restricted status of women in South Asian Hindu society.

## Architecture & Material Culture

**Qutub Minar**
Towering structure in Delhi built during the Delhi Sultanate. An elaborate mosque was built on top of a Hindu temple using materials from nearby Hindu and Buddhist shrines. Visually demonstrates syncretic architecture and the symbolic assertion of Islamic dominance. (AMSCO p. 26)

**Angkor Wat**
Massive Buddhist temple complex built near Angkor Thom in the Khmer Empire (present-day Cambodia). One of the largest religious monuments in the world; testifies to the sophistication of Southeast Asian civilization and the depth of Indian cultural influence in the region. (AMSCO p. 29)

**Arabic numerals**
The numeral system used throughout the world today actually originated in India. Arab scholars translated Indian mathematical advances (algebra, geometry) into Arabic, then spread them throughout the Dar al-Islam and eventually to Europe. (AMSCO p. 26)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests whether students can explain **why** people converted to Islam in South and Southeast Asia. The answers differ by group: merchants sought trade partners; low-caste Hindus sought social mobility; Buddhists found their institutions weakened. Know all three and be ready to explain why forced conversion failed.

> [!warning]
> Do not confuse the **Bhakti Movement** (Hindu) with **Sufism** (Islamic). They are parallel movements within different religions — both mystical, both inclusive — but they are distinct. The AP may ask you to compare them.
