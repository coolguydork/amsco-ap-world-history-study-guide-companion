# Practice & Review — Topic 1.3

*AMSCO pp. 30–32 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the excerpt below. (AMSCO p. 30)*

> "The Hindus believe that there is no country but theirs, no nation like theirs, no kings like theirs, no religion like theirs, no science like theirs. They are haughty, foolishly vain, self-conceited, and stolid. They are by nature reluctant in communicating that which they know, and they take the greatest possible care to withhold it from men of another caste among their own people, still much more, of course, from any foreigner. . . . Their haughtiness is such that, if you tell them of any science or scholar in Khorasan [a region in southwest Asia] and Persia, they will think you to be both an ignoramus and a liar. If they traveled and mixed with other nations, they would soon change their mind, for their ancestors were not as narrow-minded as the present generation is."
>
> — Al-Beruni, Muslim scholar at the court of Mahmud of Ghazni, early 11th century

**1.** Which element of Al-Beruni's point of view does he express most clearly in this passage?

(A) He was a monotheist writing about people he considered polytheists.
(B) He was a non-Hindu who was writing about Hindus.
(C) He belonged to a group that had been conquered by the people he was writing about.
(D) He grew up in a region to the west of the region he was describing.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Al-Beruni's outsider perspective as a Muslim scholar observing Hindu society is the most clearly expressed element — he repeatedly contrasts "their" behavior with an implied external standard. Option A is partially true (he was monotheist, they were polytheist) but it's not *expressed* in the passage. Option C is backward — Mahmud of Ghazni's court was the *conqueror*, not the conquered. Option D describes a geographic fact but is not the *point of view expressed*.

---

**2.** Which part of India's history most directly supports al-Beruni's claim about how the Hindu culture he observed differed from earlier culture in India?

(A) The decreasing importance of the caste system
(B) The policies on religion of Ashoka
(C) The development of the Bhakti movement
(D) The raids by Mahmud of Ghazni

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Al-Beruni claims that Hindus of his era are more closed-minded and insular than their ancestors. The Bhakti movement — which emerged *after* his writing and challenged Hindu insularity by emphasizing universal devotion over caste and ritual — supports the idea that the culture could open up, implying earlier (and later) Hindu culture was less rigid. Option D (Mahmud's raids) is a cause of Hindu defensiveness, not evidence of how it differed from earlier culture. Option B (Ashoka) is about Buddhist religious policy, not Hindu insularity. Option A is wrong — the caste system was not decreasing in importance.

---

**3.** The outcome of the Bhakti Movement was most closely related to which of the ideas expressed by Al-Beruni?

(A) Many Hindus in Southeast Asia converted to Islam.
(B) Muslims expelled nearly all Hindus from South Asia.
(C) Hindus were prohibited from traveling to countries with high Muslim populations.
(D) Hindus and Sufi Muslims found some agreement in religious beliefs.

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The Bhakti Movement's mystical, devotional emphasis closely paralleled Sufism's approach to Islam. Both valued inner spiritual experience over strict ritual; both crossed traditional social boundaries. Al-Beruni implies Hindu insularity could soften with contact — the Bhakti-Sufi parallel is exactly that: cross-tradition spiritual conversation. Options A, B, and C are factually incorrect or not supported by the passage.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Questions from AMSCO pp. 31–32*

### SAQ 1 — Source-Based (Women's Status)

**Source:** An anonymous South Asian story collection (11th–13th centuries) describing a widow who immolates herself on her husband's funeral pyre (*sati*).

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which the status of women in South Asia was **similar** to the status of men in the same region in the 12th or 13th century.

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Both men and women in South Asia were constrained by the caste system — their social roles, marriage partners, occupations, and ritual status were determined by the caste into which they were born, regardless of gender. Jati (subcaste) identity shaped the life of every individual in South Asian society.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which the status of women in South Asia **changed** between 1200 and 1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Change
> The spread of the Bhakti Movement beginning in the 12th century offered women a new religious avenue that traditional Hinduism had denied them. Unlike mainstream Hindu practice, which restricted women from priestly roles and complex scripture study, the Bhakti Movement welcomed women as devotees and even as spiritual leaders. The poet-saint Mira Bai (16th century) became one of its most celebrated figures, demonstrating that gender was not a barrier to spiritual authority within this movement.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which Islam in India between 1200 and 1450 was like Christianity in the Roman Empire.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Like Christianity in the Roman Empire, Islam in India attracted significant numbers of converts from the lowest social strata of the existing hierarchy. Just as Christianity appealed to enslaved people and the poor in Rome by promising equality before God, Islam attracted low-caste Hindus who hoped that its emphasis on the equality of all believers would improve their social standing. Both religions offered an alternative identity and community to those suffering most under the existing social structure.

### SAQ 2 — Non-Source-Based (Political & Islamic Influence)

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which political structures were **similar** between Southern India and Northern India in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Both northern and southern India lacked a unified, centralized government during this period. In the north, the Delhi Sultanate struggled to build an efficient bureaucracy and could not effectively govern the entire subcontinent. In the south, Hindu kingdoms like the Vijayanagara Empire operated as regional powers without consolidating all of southern India under one ruler. Decentralization and regional autonomy defined both areas.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which two kingdoms in Southeast Asia **differed** in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Difference
> The Majapahit Kingdom (Java) and the Khmer Empire (Cambodia) differed fundamentally in how they maintained power. Majapahit was a sea-based empire that sustained itself by controlling maritime trade routes between India and China, collecting tribute from dependent trading ports. The Khmer Empire, by contrast, was a land-based kingdom whose power rested on sophisticated irrigation and drainage systems along the Mekong River, which enabled multiple rice harvests per year and supported a large agricultural population.

**(C)** Explain ONE example of **Islamic influence** in South and Southeast Asia during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Islamic Influence
> In northern India, the Delhi Sultanate's presence produced lasting cultural and architectural fusion. Sultans ordered the construction of mosques and other Islamic structures on or from the materials of Hindu temples, most notably the Qutub Minar in Delhi. This towering structure combined elements of both Hindu and Islamic architectural traditions and served as a visible symbol of Muslim political and cultural dominance. It illustrates how Islamic rule transformed the built environment of northern India while simultaneously absorbing local artistic traditions.

## Historical Reasoning Practice — Identify Claims

*AMSCO p. 32 | AP Skill: Sourcing and Argumentation*

**Skill: Identifying Claims in Al-Beruni's excerpt**

A **claim** is a statement that expresses a viewpoint reasonable people can disagree with — it differs from provable fact and from simple preference. A strong claim is general enough to serve as an argument's thesis, supported by specific evidence.

From the Al-Beruni passage, evaluate which statement is the best **claim**:

1. "[Hindus] are haughty, foolishly vain, self-conceited, and stolid."
2. "The Hindus believe that there is no country but theirs, no nation like theirs, no kings like theirs, no religion like theirs, no science like theirs."
3. "The present generation of Hindus is narrow-minded."
4. "If you tell them of any science or scholar in Khorasan and Persia, they will think you to be both an ignoramus and a liar."

> [!tip] Model Response — Identify Claims
> Statement **2** is the best claim. It is the most general statement — a sweeping characterization of Hindu belief about their own superiority across multiple domains (country, nation, kings, religion, science). It serves as Al-Beruni's overarching argument, from which the other statements (1, 3, 4) follow as supporting examples or elaborations. Statement 4 is too specific (a single observable behavior); statements 1 and 3 are conclusions rather than the premise. However, statement 2 is an **overgeneralization** based on Al-Beruni's limited observations as a court scholar embedded with a Muslim conqueror — it reflects the biases of his position as an outsider rather than rigorous, representative evidence about all Hindus.

> [!exam-tip]
> When the AP asks you to evaluate an author's **point of view**, consider: Who is this person? What is their relationship to the subject? What might they not be able to see or might be incentivized to distort? Al-Beruni is a Muslim scholar at a conquering court writing about a defeated people — that context shapes everything he writes.
