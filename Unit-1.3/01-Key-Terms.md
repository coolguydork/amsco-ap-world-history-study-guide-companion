# Unit 1.3: Key Terms — South and Southeast Asia (1200–1450)

## Vocabulary

**Delhi Sultanate** — A series of Muslim dynasties (1206–1526) that ruled northern India from the city of Delhi; founded by Qutb-ud-din Aibak, a Turkish military commander (mamluk) who had served Muhammad of Ghur (of the Ghurid dynasty). (Significance: first major Islamic political power in South Asia; introduced Persian-Islamic governance, art, and architecture to India; predecessor to the Mughal Empire.)

**Hinduism** — The dominant religion of the Indian subcontinent, characterized by a vast pantheon, the caste system (varna), concepts of karma and dharma, and texts including the Vedas, Upanishads, and Bhagavad Gita. (Significance: provided the cultural and social framework that Delhi Sultanate rulers had to accommodate; persisted among the vast majority of India's population despite Muslim rule.)

**Caste System (Varna/Jati)** — The Hindu social hierarchy dividing society into four main categories (Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras) plus "untouchables" outside the system, with thousands of sub-castes (jati) defining occupation and social status. (Significance: one of history's most enduring social structures; provided stability that allowed Hindu culture to survive Muslim political rule.)

**Bhakti Movement** — A Hindu devotional movement emphasizing direct personal devotion to a deity without the mediation of Brahmin priests; included Muslim-influenced poet-saints like Kabir. (Significance: represents cultural synthesis between Hindu and Islamic spiritual traditions; challenged caste hierarchy; spread popular religion to lower castes.)

**Sufism** — Islamic mystical tradition (see [[Unit-1.2/01-Key-Terms#Sufism]]) that was particularly effective in India because its emphasis on personal devotion resonated with Hindu bhakti traditions. (Significance: Sufi missionaries and shrines became important sites of Hindu-Muslim cultural exchange in South Asia.)

**Khmer Empire** — A powerful Hindu-Buddhist kingdom (802–1431) centered at Angkor in present-day Cambodia; the dominant mainland Southeast Asian state. (Significance: built Angkor Wat (the world's largest religious complex) and Angkor Thom using corvée labor; controlled rice paddies and regional trade; exemplifies the Indian cultural model of statecraft in Southeast Asia.)

**Angkor Wat** — A massive temple complex in Cambodia built by Khmer king Suryavarman II (c. 1113–1150), dedicated to the Hindu god Vishnu; later converted to a Buddhist site. (Significance: demonstrates the scale of Khmer state power and the Hindu cultural model of royal authority; is the world's largest religious monument.)

**Majapahit Empire** — A Hindu-Buddhist maritime empire (1293–c.1527) centered on the island of Java in present-day Indonesia; controlled much of the Indonesian archipelago and trade routes. (Significance: the last great Hindu-Buddhist kingdom in Southeast Asia before Islamicization; its cultural legacy shapes Indonesian identity to this day.)

**Theravada Buddhism** — The "elder" school of Buddhism, emphasizing individual enlightenment through the monastic path; the dominant religion of mainland Southeast Asia. (Significance: spread from Sri Lanka to Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, and Laos during this period, providing a cultural framework for state formation.)

**Mandala State** — A Southeast Asian political model in which power radiates outward from a center (the king's palace) and diminishes with distance, rather than controlling fixed territorial boundaries. (Significance: explains the fluid, overlapping nature of Southeast Asian political authority, which looks very different from Chinese or European state models.)

**Wet-Rice Agriculture** — The cultivation of rice in flooded paddy fields; the economic foundation of most Southeast Asian states. (Significance: enabled large sedentary populations, state revenue collection through rice taxation, and the corvée labor systems that built great monuments like Angkor Wat.)

**Malacca (Melaka)** — A strategically located port city-state on the Malay Peninsula, founded c. 1400; its ruler converted to Islam around 1414. (Note: Samudra Pasai in northern Sumatra, founded c. 1267, was the first Islamic sultanate in Southeast Asia; Malacca became the most commercially dominant and influential center for Islam's spread across maritime Southeast Asia.) (Significance: became the dominant entrepôt of Southeast Asian trade and the base from which Islam spread throughout maritime Southeast Asia. See [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Malacca]].)

**Vijayanagara Empire** — A Hindu empire (1336–1646) in southern India that resisted the Delhi Sultanate's expansion and became a center of Hindu culture and the Telugu language. (Significance: demonstrates the limits of Delhi Sultanate power and the resilience of Hindu political authority in South Asia.)

**Indian Ocean Trade Network** — The web of maritime commerce connecting East Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, South Asia, and Southeast Asia via monsoon wind patterns. (Significance: the most extensive and economically vital trade network in the world before 1500; connected all the regions studied in Unit 1. See [[Unit-2.3/01-Key-Terms|Unit 2 Indian Ocean notes]].)

**Monsoon** — Seasonal wind patterns that reverse direction twice a year, enabling predictable sailing across the Indian Ocean; the foundation of Indian Ocean trade logistics. (Significance: made the Indian Ocean uniquely navigable for ancient and medieval sailors; defined the rhythms of trade, migration, and cultural exchange across Afro-Asia.)

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the **Indian cultural model of statecraft** — the way South Asian political and religious concepts (Hindu kingship, Sanskrit, Theravada Buddhism) spread to Southeast Asia without military conquest, through trade and missionary activity. This is called *Indianization* and is a key APWH concept.
