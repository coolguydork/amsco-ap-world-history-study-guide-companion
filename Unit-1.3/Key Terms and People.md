# Topic 1.3 — Key Terms and People

## Core Vocabulary

**Chola Dynasty (850–1267)**
Southern Indian Hindu kingdom lasting over 400 years. Built naval power, extended rule to Sri Lanka. Participated in Indian Ocean trade. An example of a stable, prosperous state based on trade.

**Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646)**
Southern Hindu kingdom whose name means "the victorious city." Founded by brothers who converted from Hinduism to Islam for mobility under the Delhi Sultanate, then reconverted when they established their own state. Survived until the mid-1500s.

**Rajput kingdoms**
Northern Indian Hindu kingdoms formed after the fall of the Gupta Empire. Multiple competing clans; no central authority. Their decentralization left northern India vulnerable to Muslim invasion.

**Delhi Sultanate (c. 1206–1526)**
The first major Islamic state in South Asia, ruling much of northern India for 300 years. Never built an efficient Chinese-style bureaucracy. Imposed the jizya (non-Muslim tax). Repelled the Mongols but fell to the Mughals in 1526.

**Jizya**
A tax on non-Muslim subjects under Islamic rule, including in the Delhi Sultanate. Generated resentment among Hindu and Buddhist populations.

**Caste system**
India's ancient social hierarchy based on birth. Strongest historical continuity in South Asia — persisted through political upheavals, accommodating newcomers (including Muslim merchants) by assigning them occupational subcastes.

**Bhakti Movement**
A Hindu devotional movement beginning in the 12th century that emphasized emotional attachment to a personal deity over ritual or text study. Did not discriminate by gender or caste. Parallels Sufism in Islam: both were mystical, egalitarian, and spread their faiths effectively.

**Qutub Minar**
A towering minaret in Delhi built during the Delhi Sultanate atop a Hindu temple, using materials from nearby Hindu and Buddhist shrines. A symbol of Indo-Islamic architectural synthesis — and of Islamic political dominance in northern India.

**Urdu**
A language that emerged from Muslim presence in South Asia. Combined Hindi grammar with Arabic and Farsi vocabulary. Today, the official language of Pakistan. A living example of cultural synthesis.

**Proselytize**
To actively seek converts to one's religion. Islam and Buddhism are both universalizing religions — they actively seek converts. Hinduism is generally not proselytizing.

**Srivijaya Empire (670–1025)**
Hindu maritime kingdom on Sumatra (SE Asia). Controlled the Strait of Malacca; charged fees for ships traveling between India and China. Prospered from trade control rather than territorial expansion.

**Majapahit Kingdom (1293–1520)**
Buddhist maritime kingdom on Java (SE Asia) with 98 tributaries. Like Srivijaya, controlled sea routes. The largest Southeast Asian maritime power.

**Khmer Empire / Angkor Kingdom (802–1431)**
Land-based kingdom in modern Cambodia. Built complex irrigation systems enabling multiple rice harvests per year. Famous for the temple complexes at Angkor Thom (Hindu + Buddhist art) and Angkor Wat. Fell to the Sukhothai Kingdom in 1431.

**Angkor Wat**
Ornate Buddhist temple complex in Cambodia, built near Angkor Thom. Evidence of Hindu-to-Buddhist transition in Khmer rulers — Hindu art was preserved, not destroyed, when Buddhist art was added.

**Sinhala dynasties**
Buddhist kingdoms in Sri Lanka. Monasteries and nunneries flourished; priests served as royal advisors; irrigation networks supported prosperity.

**Sinification** *(connection to Topic 1.1)*
The adoption of Chinese practices. In Southeast Asia, Vietnam showed the most complex relationship — adopting Chinese writing and architecture while violently resisting Chinese political domination.

## Key People

**Harihara and Bukka**
Brothers who founded the Vijayanagara Empire. Their story — converting from Hinduism to Islam for career advancement, then reconverting when they had the chance — illustrates the pragmatic, negotiated nature of religious identity in this period.

**Lal Ded (1320–1392)**
Kashmiri Hindu poet quoted in AMSCO. Emphasized personal experience over texts — bridges Hindu tradition and Sufi influence. "What the books taught me, I've practised. What they didn't teach me, I've taught myself."

**Mira Bai (16th century)**
Famous female figure of the Bhakti Movement. A poet who became one of India's most celebrated devotional voices. Her prominence challenges the assumption that women had no role in South Asian religious life.
