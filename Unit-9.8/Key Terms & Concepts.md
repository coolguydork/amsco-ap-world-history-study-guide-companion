# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 9 — Institutions Developing in a Globalized World | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 696–706**

---

**[[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]]**
International organization founded on October 24, 1945 after WWII to maintain peace, promote human rights, and foster international cooperation; replaced the failed League of Nations; grew from 51 founding members to 193 states by 2019. Unlike the League, all major powers joined, giving the UN greater legitimacy and enforcement capacity — though the Security Council veto still limited collective action during the Cold War.

**General Assembly**
UN body in which all 193 member states have equal representation and one vote; decides important questions on peace, security, membership, and budget by two-thirds majority. The only UN body where small and large nations have equal standing — but it can only debate and recommend, not enforce.

**Security Council**
UN body with primary responsibility for international peace and security; five permanent members (US, UK, France, Russia, China) each hold veto power; can authorize peacekeeping missions and military force. The veto structure reflects post-WWII power realities and was deliberately controversial — small nations resented granting five states dominant authority. Cold War US-Soviet vetoes frequently paralyzed the Council.

**Secretariat**
The UN's administrative and bureaucratic arm; staffers must swear loyalty to the UN, not their home governments. Led by the secretary-general, the Secretariat carries out the day-to-day work of all UN agencies and programs — the institutional backbone that makes the organization function across membership changes and political crises.

**secretary-general**
Chief administrative officer and global spokesperson of the United Nations; leads and represents the organization; traditionally chosen from a small, neutral nation to minimize great-power influence; must be approved by all five permanent Security Council members. **Kofi Annan** (1997–2006) is the AP exam's most-tested example: he reformed the UN and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2001.

**Economic and Social Council**
The largest and most complex UN body; directs economic, social, humanitarian, and cultural activities across dozens of specialized agencies; coordinates programs on green energy, wages in developing countries, refugee assistance, and cultural preservation. Represents the UN's shift from purely military security to human security and development.

**Trusteeship Council**
UN body that supervised formerly colonial "trust territories" to prepare them for self-governance and independence; territories included land that became Israel, Papua New Guinea, and Nauru. The council suspended operations in 1994 when Palau — the last trust territory — became independent, marking the effective completion of the decolonization process begun in [[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview|Topic 8.5]].

**International Monetary Fund (IMF)**
International financial organization created in 1945 to stabilize global currency exchange rates and provide short-term loans and economic advice to countries in financial crisis; works closely with the World Bank. Criticized by developing nations for attaching neoliberal conditions (austerity, privatization) to its loans — conditions critics argue served the interests of wealthy member states. Links directly to [[Unit-9.4/Economics in the Global Age Overview|Topic 9.4]]'s debates on economic globalization.

**peacekeeping action**
UN military deployments of civilian, police, and lightly armed troops from member nations sent to conflict zones to monitor ceasefires, protect civilians, hold elections, and support peace agreements. The UN had 5 active peacekeeping operations in 1988; by 1993 this grew to 28; by 2019, 102,000 peacekeepers served in 15 missions. Successes: Namibia, Cambodia, El Salvador. Failures: Rwanda (1994), Somalia, Bosnia.

**World Food Program (WFP)**
UN agency established in 1961 to provide food aid during natural disasters and political crises; first missions in Iran, Thailand, and Algeria in 1962; has fed over 1.5 billion people since founding. Represents the humanitarian dimension of international institutions — using global collective resources to address crises beyond any individual state's capacity.

**UNESCO**
United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization; founded 1945 to repair WWII-damaged schools and cultural heritage; shifted to global literacy programs and designating World Heritage Sites. Politically controversial: the US, UK, Israel, and Singapore all withdrew at various points over disputes about priorities and politics; as of 2019 the US had not rejoined. Illustrates how even humanitarian institutions become sites of geopolitical competition.

**[[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|Human Rights]] Watch**
Major international NGO that monitors and documents human rights abuses in over 100 countries, using the [[Concept Notes/Universal Declaration of Human Rights\|Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] as its benchmark; advocates for policy changes to prevent abuses. Operates independently of the UN but cooperates with it — exemplifies the growing role of civil society and non-state actors in enforcing global norms where governments fail to act.

**International Peace Bureau**
One of the world's oldest international peace organizations, founded in 1891; won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1910; from the 1980s focused on nuclear disarmament and lobbying governments to reduce military spending; by 2019 had 300 member organizations in 70 countries. Shows that global civil society for peace predates the UN — and that NGOs complement intergovernmental institutions in maintaining international norms.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently contrasts the **League of Nations** (1920, failed — US never joined, could not prevent WWII) with the **United Nations** (1945, more successful — all major powers joined, specialized agencies, peacekeeping). Know the structural differences: universal membership, Security Council enforcement power, and specialized agencies are the key improvements.

> [!tip]
> The **IMF** and **World Bank** are both UN-affiliated but focus on economic stability rather than security. The IMF handles currency crises and short-term loans; the World Bank funds long-term development projects (dams, roads, education). Both are criticized for favoring wealthy nations' agendas — link to 9.4's debates on neoliberalism and globalization.
