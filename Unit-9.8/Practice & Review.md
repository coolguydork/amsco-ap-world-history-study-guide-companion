## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 703–704; UN Charter Preamble*

**Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage:**

> "WE THE PEOPLES OF THE UNITED NATIONS DETERMINED
> • to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, which twice in our life-time has brought untold sorrow to mankind, and
> • to reaffirm faith in fundamental human rights, in the dignity and worth of the human person, in the equal rights of men and women and of nations large and small, and
> • to establish conditions under which justice and respect for the obligations arising from treaties and other sources of international law can be maintained…"
>
> — Preamble to the United Nations Charter, 1945

**1. Which best describes the historical context in which the United Nations was founded?**

(A) The impact of trench warfare in World War I
(B) The success of the League of Nations
(C) The long-running U.S.-Soviet nuclear arms race
(D) The causes and effects of both World War I and World War II

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The phrase "twice in our life-time" directly references both WWI and WWII. The League of Nations (B) failed — it is the cautionary tale, not the model. The nuclear arms race (C) developed after the UN's founding. Trench warfare (A) was WWI only. The UN was explicitly created to prevent a third catastrophic world war.

**2. Which is the best example of the United Nations using its power as described in the preamble?**

(A) Negotiating an end to World War II
(B) Sending peacekeeping missions to several different countries
(C) Helping the United States exit the Vietnam War
(D) Preventing the Cold War from spreading

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Peacekeeping missions directly implement the preamble's goal of preventing "the scourge of war." The UN was founded *after* WWII (A), did not manage US withdrawal from Vietnam (C), and did not prevent the Cold War (D) — which began shortly after the UN's founding.

**3. Based on the preamble, what was a primary reason for the United Nations' creation?**

(A) To provide loans to developing nations
(B) To establish an international trading system
(C) To create a framework for preventing future wars
(D) To promote the spread of democratic governments

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Every bullet in the preamble relates to peace, security, human rights, and international law — not trade (B), loans (A), or democracy promotion (D). The economic institutions (IMF, World Bank) were created separately at Bretton Woods, not through the UN Charter.

## Short-Answer Questions

**SAQ 1 — Contextualization and Causation**

Answer all parts of the following question.

(A) Identify ONE historical development that contributed to the creation of the United Nations in 1945.
(B) Explain ONE way in which the United Nations structure was designed to be more effective than the League of Nations.
(C) Explain ONE way in which the structure of the United Nations limited its effectiveness.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> **(A)** The failure of the League of Nations to prevent World War II was a critical cause. The League lacked the participation of major powers (the US never joined, Germany and Japan eventually withdrew), had no enforcement mechanism, and proved unable to stop Japanese aggression in Manchuria (1931) or Italian invasion of Ethiopia (1935). Allied leaders concluded that a stronger, more inclusive organization with enforcement power was essential.
>
> **(B)** Unlike the League, the UN included all major powers as founding members — most importantly the United States, whose absence had fatally weakened the League. The Security Council provided an enforcement mechanism: it could authorize military force against states violating UN principles. Specialized agencies (WHO, UNICEF, UNESCO, WFP) also extended the UN's reach into humanitarian domains the League never attempted.
>
> **(C)** The Security Council veto mechanism gave the US, UK, France, Russia, and China the power to block any collective action they opposed. During the Cold War, the US and USSR routinely vetoed each other's proposals, paralyzing the Council on conflicts from Korea to the Middle East. The Rwanda genocide (1994) demonstrated this structural weakness: Security Council deadlock and reluctance among permanent members prevented intervention even as 800,000 people were killed in 100 days.

**SAQ 2 — TAH Argumentation (AMSCO p. 705–706)**

Two law professors debated whether the UN is "still relevant" in 2006. Professor Burke-White argues the UN is urgently needed because global challenges (terrorism, climate change, WMD) require near-universal participation in legal regimes only the UN can provide. Professor Bell counters that the UN has demonstrably failed on every major problem Burke-White cites — North Korea acquired nuclear weapons, terrorism continues, and climate action has been limited to "words."

(A) Identify ONE argument made by Professor Burke-White.
(B) Explain ONE limitation of Burke-White's argument.
(C) Explain ONE historical situation in the late 20th century in which the development of the United Nations affected global relations.

> [!tip] Model Response — Argumentation
> **(A)** Burke-White argues that global challenges like terrorism and climate change require "near universal participation in legal regimes" that only the UN can facilitate. Because even a small number of defectors (a few safe havens for terrorists, a handful of serious polluters) can undermine an otherwise global regime, the UN's universal membership makes it the only viable institution for addressing these problems.
>
> **(B)** Burke-White's argument conflates institutional design (universal participation) with actual outcomes (problem solving). He demonstrates that the UN creates legal frameworks but does not demonstrate that these frameworks actually reduce harm. Bell's empirical counterexample — North Korea's nuclear program continuing despite UN resolutions — illustrates this gap between lawmaking and problem-solving.
>
> **(C)** The 1994 Rwandan genocide illustrates how the UN's development affected global relations. UN peacekeeping forces were present in Rwanda but lacked authorization to intervene as Hutu extremists killed approximately 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu in 100 days. The Security Council's failure to authorize intervention — driven by reluctance among permanent members following the Somalia debacle — prompted widespread international debate about the UN's "responsibility to protect" civilians and ultimately contributed to the development of new international norms about sovereignty and humanitarian intervention in the early 2000s.

## Long-Essay Question (LEQ) Practice

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which international institutions effectively promoted global cooperation in the period 1945 to 2001.

**AP Skill: Argumentation — Complexity**

> [!tip] Model Thesis (Complexity — Both Sides)
> International institutions created after 1945 — most notably the United Nations and its specialized agencies — significantly promoted global cooperation in humanitarian and development domains (WHO eradicating smallpox, WFP feeding 1.5 billion, UNICEF supporting children's welfare), but proved consistently less effective in security and political disputes where the Security Council veto mechanism paralyzed collective action, as demonstrated by Cold War deadlocks and the failure to prevent the 1994 Rwandan genocide.

**Outline for Full Essay:**

Paragraph 1 (Thesis/Complexity): Distinguish humanitarian success from security failure.

Paragraph 2 (Evidence for effectiveness): UNESCO literacy programs; WFP feeding crises; WHO smallpox eradication 1980; UNICEF; successful peacekeeping in Namibia, Cambodia, El Salvador.

Paragraph 3 (Evidence for limits): Security Council veto during Cold War paralyzes action on Korea, Middle East, Vietnam; Rwanda genocide 1994 (peacekeepers present, no authorization to act); Somalia withdrawal; IMF conditionality harming Global South borrowers.

Paragraph 4 (Complexity/Qualification): NGOs (Amnesty, MSF, HRW) compensated for some UN failures by operating outside state-controlled frameworks; the UN's humanitarian record is genuinely strong even when its security record fails; counterfactual — world without the UN might have seen more direct great-power conflicts.

Conclusion: Restate qualified thesis. The UN's effectiveness varied by domain and time period — not a simple success or failure.

## Key Fact Review

| Institution | Founded | Primary Purpose | Key Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| League of Nations | 1920 | Collective security | US absent; no enforcement |
| United Nations | 1945 | Peace, human rights, cooperation | Security Council veto |
| IMF | 1945 | Currency stability, short-term loans | Conditionality favors wealthy states |
| World Bank | 1944 | Development loans | Ignored environmental/cultural costs |
| UNESCO | 1945 | Education, culture, heritage | Politically controversial |
| WFP | 1961 | Food aid | Depends on member state funding |
| Human Rights Watch | 1978 | Monitor rights abuses | No enforcement power |
