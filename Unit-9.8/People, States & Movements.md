# People, States & Movements

**Unit 9 — Institutions Developing in a Globalized World | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 696–706**

---

## Key Figures

**Kofi Annan** (1938–2018)
Ghanaian diplomat and seventh Secretary-General of the [[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]] (1997–2006); the first to rise from within the UN's own staff. Annan led major UN reforms and expanded its focus on human security (not just state security), arguing that the UN had a "responsibility to protect" civilians from genocide and crimes against humanity. He was jointly awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2001 alongside the UN itself. His tenure also saw failures — including the 1994 Rwanda genocide, which occurred while he was head of UN peacekeeping operations, something he later acknowledged as a personal and institutional failure.

> [!exam-tip]
> Kofi Annan is the most AP-testable individual in 9.8. He represents both the UN's aspirations (reform, human rights, Nobel Prize) and its limitations (Rwanda failure, "responsibility to protect" not always honored). Use him as evidence in Argumentation essays about UN effectiveness.

**Eleanor Roosevelt** (1884–1962)
Former US First Lady and US delegate to the UN General Assembly; chaired the UN commission that drafted the **Universal Declaration of [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|Human Rights]]** (1948). Roosevelt gave the UDHR moral authority and international legitimacy by ensuring its language reflected universal human values rather than purely Western ones — though critics noted it was not legally binding. Her role connects 9.8 to 9.5 (calls for reform and human rights movements).

> [!tip]
> Eleanor Roosevelt bridges Units 7–9: she was politically active from the New Deal era through the early Cold War. On the AP exam, she represents the American commitment to internationalism and human rights advocacy within the UN framework — a contrast to the isolationism that kept the US out of the League of Nations.

**Dean Acheson** (1893–1971)
US Secretary of State under President Truman; described the UN as an "effective instrument of international cooperation." His quote opens Topic 9.8 in AMSCO, framing the American vision of the UN as a pragmatic tool for maintaining US-led global order rather than a purely idealistic institution.

## Key States and Institutions

**The "Big Five" — Permanent Security Council Members**
The United States, United Kingdom, France, Russia, and China are the five permanent members of the UN Security Council, each with veto power. This power structure reflects the victorious Allied coalition of WWII. During the Cold War, the US and USSR routinely vetoed each other's proposals, paralyzing the Security Council on conflicts from Korea to the Middle East. The veto remains controversial — critics argue it lets major powers block accountability for their own actions and those of their allies.

**The League of Nations (1920–1946)**
The UN's predecessor, created at the Paris Peace Conference after WWI; designed to resolve disputes and prevent war. It failed because the US never joined (Senate refused ratification), several major powers withdrew (Japan, Germany, Italy), and it had no military enforcement mechanism. The League's collapse and WWII's outbreak directly motivated the design of the UN — key AP causal context for why the UN was structured as it was.

**Rwanda (1994)**
Site of one of the worst UN peacekeeping failures. In 100 days, Hutu extremists killed approximately 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu civilians while UN peacekeeping troops were present but unauthorized to intervene. The Security Council declined to authorize intervention as the genocide unfolded, paralyzed by great-power reluctance to commit forces. Rwanda is the exam's defining example of international institutional failure — always cite it when assessing the limits of the UN.

**Somalia (1990s)**
Civil war and famine led to a UN humanitarian and peacekeeping mission in the early 1990s. The mission ended in withdrawal as fighting continued, and a US-led operation (Black Hawk Down, 1993) suffered significant casualties before US forces departed. Somalia, like Rwanda, demonstrates the challenge of peacekeeping when there is no peace to keep.

## Key Movements and Organizations

**NGOs (Non-Governmental Organizations)**
Private civil society organizations that operate internationally, often filling gaps left by state institutions and the UN. Major examples include: Amnesty International (human rights documentation), Médecins Sans Frontières / [[Concept Notes/Doctors Without Borders\|Doctors Without Borders]] (emergency medicine in war zones), Greenpeace (environmental activism), Human Rights Watch (rights monitoring), and the International Red Cross (humanitarian aid in conflict). Their rise from the 1970s onward reflects the broader decentralization of international authority under globalization.

> [!tip]
> NGOs are useful AP evidence because they show non-state actors shaping global norms. They are also examples of the **cultural diffusion of ideas** (CDI theme) — human rights values, environmental standards, and humanitarian norms spread globally through NGO networks, not just governments.

**The [[Concept Notes/Universal Declaration of Human Rights\|Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] (1948)**
Milestone UN document drafted under Eleanor Roosevelt's leadership; the first global statement of universal human rights including freedom from slavery, equality before the law, rights to education and healthcare, and freedom of thought and religion. Not legally binding, but set the global standard that later treaties and NGOs like Human Rights Watch use as their benchmark. Translated into over 500 languages. Links directly to 9.5's human rights reform movements.
