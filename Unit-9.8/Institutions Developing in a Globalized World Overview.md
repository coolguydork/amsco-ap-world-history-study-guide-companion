# Institutions Developing in a Globalized World

**Unit 9 — Globalization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 696–706**

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam tests 9.8 primarily through causation (why was the UN created?) and argumentation (how effective has it been?). The most common essay prompt asks you to evaluate the extent to which international institutions successfully promoted global cooperation. Know specific examples of both successes and failures.

## The Big Picture

The catastrophic destruction of two world wars in the first half of the 20th century forced nations to reckon with the limits of unilateral power. In 1945, the major Allied powers created the **[[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]]** to replace the failed League of Nations, building a new framework for collective security, human rights, and international cooperation. Unlike the League, the UN included all major powers — including the United States — and backed its authority with the threat of military force through the Security Council.

The UN's structure reflected the tension between democratic ideals and great-power realism. The **General Assembly** gave every nation an equal vote, but the **Security Council** gave the five victorious Allied powers (US, UK, France, Russia, China) permanent veto authority. This veto mechanism repeatedly deadlocked the UN during the Cold War, as the US and USSR blocked each other's proposals. Despite this structural weakness, the UN expanded significantly — from 51 founding members to 193 by 2019 — and its specialized agencies (UNESCO, UNICEF, WFP, WHO, IMF, World Bank) became major forces in global humanitarian and economic governance.

> [!warning]
> The UN did NOT end the Cold War or prevent all conflicts. Its peacekeeping record is mixed: successes in Namibia, Cambodia, and El Salvador but notable failures in Rwanda (1994 genocide, 800,000 killed) and Somalia. Students often overstate UN effectiveness — the exam rewards nuanced arguments that acknowledge both achievements and limits.

Alongside the UN, a parallel network of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) grew throughout the late 20th century. Groups like Amnesty International, Médecins Sans Frontières ([[Concept Notes/Doctors Without Borders\|Doctors Without Borders]]), Greenpeace, and [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|Human Rights]] Watch applied pressure on governments and filled gaps the UN could not address. This rise of NGOs reflects a broader trend: as globalization deepened, non-state actors became increasingly significant players in international relations.

> [!tip]
> Think of 9.8 in terms of the AP governance theme (GOV): the creation of international institutions represents a fundamental change in how states managed conflicts and shared global responsibilities — from purely bilateral diplomacy to multilateral frameworks with binding obligations and shared norms.

## The United Nations: Structure and Bodies

The UN was born on October 24, 1945, with 51 founding member states. Its six main bodies each serve distinct functions:

The **General Assembly** gives every member nation one vote and decides major questions by two-thirds majority. It debates issues but cannot take binding enforcement action on its own. The **Security Council** holds the real enforcement power — it can authorize peacekeeping missions and even military force. Its five permanent members (US, UK, France, Russia, China) each hold veto power, reflecting the post-WWII power structure. Ten additional members rotate on two-year terms.

The **Secretariat** is the administrative backbone, staffed by international civil servants who swear loyalty to the UN rather than their home countries. The **secretary-general** — typically from a small, neutral nation — leads the organization; all five permanent Security Council members must approve the selection. The **[[Concept Notes/International Court of Justice\|International Court of Justice]]** settles legal disputes between nations, though it lacks enforcement power. The **Economic and Social Council** manages humanitarian, cultural, and economic programs. The **Trusteeship Council** supervised former colonial territories transitioning to independence; its mission was declared complete when Palau, the last trust territory, became independent in 1994.

## Peacekeeping: Successes and Failures

UN peacekeeping missions deploy civilians, police, and lightly armed troops from member nations to conflict zones. The number of active missions surged from 5 in 1988 to 28 in 1993 as the Cold War ended. By 2019, 102,000 UN peacekeepers were deployed worldwide.

Successes include Namibia's transition from South African colony to independent state, ending civil wars in Mozambique, El Salvador, and Cambodia, and stabilizing Haiti after a military dictatorship. Failures include Somalia (civil war raged as troops withdrew), Bosnia (years of conflict with mixed results), and most tragically Rwanda, where UN peacekeepers watched as 800,000 people were killed in 100 days without effective intervention. The Rwanda failure exposed two structural problems: slow response time (by the time nations agree and deploy, conflicts have escalated) and ambiguity of mandate (peacekeepers are often expected to stop fighting but authorized only to monitor ceasefires).

> [!warning]
> Rwanda 1994 is a critical exam example. The UN's failure to intervene — despite peacekeeping forces being present — is the strongest evidence for arguments about the limitations of international institutions. Always connect this to the Security Council veto deadlock and the principle of national sovereignty.

## UN Specialized Agencies

UNESCO (1945) repaired WWII-damaged schools and cultural sites, then shifted to literacy programs and designating World Heritage Sites. UNICEF provides humanitarian aid to children in disaster and conflict zones. The **World Food Program** (1961) provides food aid and has fed over 1.5 billion people. The **World Health Organization** fights epidemics and coordinates vaccines. **Human Rights Watch** monitors abuses in 100+ countries using the [[Concept Notes/Universal Declaration of Human Rights\|Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] as its standard.

The **World Bank** (1944) fights poverty through development loans, initially for infrastructure but later expanding to education and health. The **IMF** (1945) promotes stable currency exchange rates and provides short-term loans; critics argue it imposes conditions (austerity, privatization) that benefit wealthy nations at the expense of developing countries — a key tension in economic globalization debates.

## NGOs and the Broader Landscape

Not all international cooperation runs through the UN. The International Peace Bureau (founded 1891) won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1910 and lobbied for nuclear disarmament from the 1980s onward. The Red Cross (founded 1863) responds to armed conflicts and disasters. Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch pressure governments on rights abuses. These NGOs demonstrate that international civil society — not just states — shapes global norms.

> [!tip]
> For AP essays, use NGOs as evidence that international cooperation extends beyond state-level institutions. They show the limits of state sovereignty and the growing role of civil society in global governance — a theme that connects 9.8 to 9.5 (reform movements) and 9.7 (resistance to globalization).
