# Mit'a System

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

The **mit'a** (also *mita*) was the Incan system of mandatory public service — a labor tax imposed on conquered men in place of tribute goods. It became one of the most distinctive features of Incan imperial administration and was later exploited by Spanish colonizers.

---

## Incan Mit'a (c. 1438–1533)

**From [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|Topic 1.4 Overview]]:**

> *"Conquered people under the Inca did not have to pay tribute. Rather, they were subject to the mit'a system, mandatory public service. Men between the ages of 15 and 50 provided agricultural and other forms of labor, including the construction of roads."*

Key features of the Incan mit'a:

- **Who**: Men aged 15–50 in conquered territories
- **What**: Agricultural labor, road construction (Carpa Nan), military service, mining, textile production
- **In exchange**: The state provided food, tools, and clothing during labor service
- **Purpose**: Built the infrastructure of the empire — roads, bridges, temples, irrigation — without a monetary economy

The mit'a was integrated with the Inca's broader administrative system: the Carpa Nan (25,000-mile road network) was built almost entirely through mit'a labor. So was the terrace agriculture that fed the empire.

> [!tip]
> **Aztec tribute vs. Inca mit'a**: This is a core AP comparison. The Aztecs demanded **goods** (food, cloth, luxury items) from conquered peoples, which generated **resentment** and subject rebellion. The Inca demanded **labor** through mit'a, and provided something in return (food and supplies during service). The Inca system was more administratively integrative — but it also created the infrastructure for later Spanish exploitation.

---

## Spanish Mita (Post-1533)

After conquering the Inca, Spanish colonizers deliberately repurposed the mit'a as the *mita encomienda* — forced labor in silver mines, especially at Potosí (modern Bolivia). The Spanish kept the Quechua name but stripped away the reciprocal elements: indigenous workers received minimal compensation and faced deadly working conditions.

The Potosí silver mines, worked through forced mita labor, produced enormous wealth that funded the Spanish Empire and flowed into global trade networks. This connection — from Incan mit'a to Spanish mita to global silver economy — is one of the AP curriculum's most powerful examples of how colonial systems exploit indigenous institutions.

---

## AP Exam Significance

The mit'a appears most often as:
1. **A contrast to the Aztec tribute system** (labor vs. goods; integration vs. resentment)
2. **Evidence of Incan administrative sophistication** (managing a vast empire without money or writing)
3. **A precursor to Spanish colonial labor exploitation** in Unit 4 (mita encomienda, Potosí silver)
4. **An example of continuity and change** — the institution persists across the conquest but changes in fundamental ways
