# East India Company

The British East India Company (EIC, founded 1600) was a royally chartered joint-stock company that began as a trading enterprise in the Indian Ocean and gradually transformed into a sovereign governing power controlling the Indian subcontinent. It is the premier example of **economic imperialism** evolving into direct colonial rule — a company that fielded its own army, collected taxes, and administered justice across hundreds of millions of people.

## Origins as a Trading Company (1600–1757)

The EIC was chartered by Queen Elizabeth I to compete with Dutch and Portuguese traders in the spice trade. It established **factory** (trading post) networks at Surat, Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta. Unlike the Portuguese Estado da India (a royal enterprise), the EIC was a private joint-stock company — shareholders bore the risk and received the profits.

Initial trade: spices (pepper, cloves), textiles (calicoes, muslins), indigo, and later tea. Indian textiles were so competitive that English wool manufacturers lobbied Parliament for protective tariffs — an early example of deindustrialization through colonial trade.

## Transformation into a Governing Power (1757–1857)

The **Battle of Plassey (1757)** was the turning point. EIC forces under Robert Clive defeated the Nawab of Bengal (backed by France), giving the Company effective control of the richest province of India. The EIC became the **Diwan of Bengal** (tax collector) in 1765 — now governing rather than just trading.

By 1800, the EIC controlled most of India through a combination of:
- **Subsidiary alliances:** Indian princes kept their thrones but surrendered military control and paid the EIC for "protection"
- **Doctrine of Lapse:** British Governor-General Dalhousie annexed princely states without a male heir
- **Direct military conquest:** Mysore Wars, Anglo-Maratha Wars, Sikh Wars

The EIC's army numbered 200,000 by the 1850s — larger than the British Crown's army.

## The Sepoy Mutiny and Company's End (1857–1858)

Indian soldiers (**sepoys**) in the EIC's army mutinied in 1857 — triggered by rumors that rifle cartridges were greased with pig and cow fat (offensive to Muslim and Hindu soldiers respectively), but reflecting deeper resentment of British cultural impositions, land policies, and displacement of Indian elites. The rebellion was suppressed after 14 months of brutal fighting.

The **Government of India Act (1858)** abolished the EIC entirely. The British Crown assumed direct rule; Queen Victoria became Empress of India in 1877. The era of company imperialism ended; formal colonial empire began.

## AP Significance

The East India Company is the AP's clearest example of **economic imperialism preceding political control** — the pattern in which trade networks, debt relationships, and military advantage gradually produced direct colonial governance. It bridges the joint-stock company era (Unit 4) with the Age of Imperialism (Unit 6) and foreshadows 20th-century debates about corporate power and sovereignty.

> [!exam-tip]
> On comparison and causation essays about imperialism, use the EIC to show that colonialism was not always a deliberate state policy from the start — it could emerge incrementally from commercial competition. This nuance elevates essays above the "Europeans wanted to dominate" narrative.
