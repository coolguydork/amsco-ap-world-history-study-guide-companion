# Theocracy

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Theocracy** is a system of government in which religious authority and political authority are fused — rulers govern as divine representatives or as religious leaders themselves. Theocracy appears across multiple AP World History topics as a recurring form of political legitimation.

---

## Definition

A theocracy is rule by religious leaders or by rulers who claim divine authority. The key feature is the **absence of separation between religious and political power** — the ruler's legitimacy derives directly from religious sanction, not from popular consent, hereditary succession alone, or secular law.

---

## Aztec Theocracy

**From [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|Topic 1.4 Overview]]:**

> *"Aztec government was a theocracy, which is rule by religious leaders. At the top was the emperor, known as the Great Speaker, who was the political ruler as well as a divine representative of the gods."*

The Aztec emperor was simultaneously the political sovereign and the gods' earthly representative. Human sacrifice reinforced theocratic authority — by controlling the rituals that repaid the gods' cosmic debt, the emperor demonstrated that the empire's survival depended on his religious role.

## Other AP Examples of Theocratic or Religiously Legitimized Rule

| Civilization | Period | Theocratic Element |
|---|---|---|
| Aztec Empire | 1.4 | Emperor = divine representative of gods; human sacrifice as state ritual |
| Inca Empire | 1.4 | Emperor = son of Inti (sun god); Temple of the Sun as religious center |
| Mayan city-states | 1.4 | Kings claimed descent from gods; priests controlled calendars and warfare |
| Abbasid Caliphate | 1.2 | Caliph = "successor of the Prophet"; political and religious authority combined |
| Delhi Sultanate | 1.3 | Sultan governed under Islamic law (shariah); jizya imposed as religious duty |
| Medieval European monarchies | 1.6 | Divine right of kings; Pope could crown/depose emperors |
| Mali Empire | 1.5 | Mansa used Islamic authority after conversion; mosques and scholars as legitimizers |

> [!exam-tip]
> On AP essays, theocracy is useful as a **cross-regional comparison concept**: multiple civilizations in Period 1 used religious authority to legitimize political power. The difference lies in the mechanism — Aztec emperors were gods' representatives; Islamic caliphs were successors to the Prophet; European kings claimed divine right sanctioned by the Pope. Same structural function (religion legitimizes hierarchy), different doctrinal content.

| Government Type | Source of Authority | AP Examples |
