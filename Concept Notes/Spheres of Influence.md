# Spheres of Influence

Zones within a nominally independent country where a foreign power holds exclusive trading rights and access to natural resources, often backed by military or economic coercion. Unlike formal colonies, the country retains its government, but sovereignty is severely compromised. The most prominent example is 19th-century China, where Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and Japan each carved out exclusive zones after the Opium Wars — a form of informal imperialism sometimes called "carving up the melon."

> [!exam-tip]
> Spheres of influence represent *informal* imperialism — control without annexation. The AP contrasts this with formal colonization (direct political rule). China is the key case study: nominally independent throughout the 19th century, yet economically dominated by multiple foreign powers simultaneously. Use spheres of influence to argue that Western powers had multiple tools of imperial control beyond direct conquest.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse spheres of influence with the Monroe Doctrine. The Monroe Doctrine declared a U.S. sphere of influence over the Western Hemisphere — which shows that the concept was not uniquely European but a general tool of great-power competition.
