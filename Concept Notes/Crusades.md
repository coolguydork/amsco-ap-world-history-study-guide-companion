# Crusades

## What Were the Crusades?

The Crusades were a series of European Christian military campaigns launched to retake the Holy Land — primarily Jerusalem — from Muslim control. They were fought primarily between 1095 and the late 1200s, though crusading activity continued in various forms beyond that.

**Immediate cause:** The Seljuk Turks, who had conquered much of the Middle East in the 11th century, restricted Christian pilgrim access to Jerusalem. The Byzantine Empire appealed to the Pope for military help. Pope Urban II called for a holy war in 1095.

**First Crusade (1096–1099):** Captured Jerusalem in 1099. Crusader states established in the Levant.

**Saladin retakes Jerusalem (1187):** Muslim leader Saladin (Salah ad-Din) recaptured Jerusalem. This triggered the Third Crusade.

**Fourth Crusade (1202–1204):** Never reached the Holy Land — Crusaders sacked the Christian city of Constantinople instead, weakening the Byzantine Empire and accelerating its eventual fall in 1453.

---

## AP Significance

The Crusades matter less for military outcomes (they ultimately failed to hold the Holy Land) and more for their **cultural and economic consequences:**

- **Cultural exchange:** Crusaders encountered Islamic learning, goods, and practices; introduced them to European markets and intellectual culture.
- **Knowledge transfer:** Contact with Dar al-Islam exposed Europeans to preserved Greek texts, Arabic numerals, and medical advances — contributing to the conditions for the Renaissance.
- **Economic stimulus:** Demand for Eastern luxuries (spices, textiles, dyes) expanded European trade with the Islamic world, enriching Italian city-states like Venice.
- **Byzantine weakening:** The Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople (1204) accelerated Byzantine decline, eventually contributing to the Ottoman conquest in 1453.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP cares about the *consequences* of the Crusades more than the military details. The key takeaway: failed militarily, but drove cultural and commercial exchange between Europe and the Islamic world. This exchange helped lay the groundwork for the European Renaissance.
