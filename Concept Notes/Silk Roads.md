# Silk Roads

## What It Is

The **Silk Roads** were a network of overland (and partially maritime) trade routes connecting East Asia, Central Asia, South Asia, the Middle East, East Africa, and Europe. Active from the Han Dynasty (c. 2nd century BCE) through the early modern period, they were named for Chinese silk — the most prized luxury export — but carried a vast range of goods, ideas, religions, and diseases.

The Silk Roads were not a single road but a web of routes through oases, mountain passes, and river valleys. Merchants rarely traveled the full length; goods passed through many hands across Central Asian trading cities like Samarkand.

---

## Why It Matters for the AP Exam

The Silk Roads are central to **Unit 2** (Networks of Exchange) and appear repeatedly as evidence across all four AP periods. Key AP uses:

- **Cultural diffusion**: Buddhism, Islam, and Christianity all spread along Silk Road routes
- **Technological diffusion**: gunpowder, the compass, woodblock printing spread westward from China
- **Disease transmission**: the Black Death (bubonic plague) traveled the Silk Roads in the 14th century
- **State facilitation of trade**: Tang/Song China, Mongol Pax Mongolica, Islamic caliphates all protected traders
- **Decline**: Portuguese maritime routes eventually bypassed the overland Silk Roads

> [!exam-tip]
> The Silk Roads are a classic AP **continuity and change** topic. Continuity: long-distance luxury trade persisted for centuries. Change: the Mongols revived it under the Pax Mongolica; European maritime routes eventually made overland routes less dominant.

## Topic 1.1 Connection — East Asia as Eastern Anchor (AMSCO pp. 3–8)

Song Dynasty China anchored the eastern end of the Silk Roads. Chinese exports — **silk, porcelain, and tea** — were the luxury goods that made the routes economically viable. Chinese innovations — **gunpowder, the compass, and woodblock printing** — traveled westward along these routes over centuries, eventually reshaping warfare, navigation, and literacy across Eurasia. Buddhist ideas also spread eastward into China via the Silk Roads (Xuanzang's pilgrimage to India being a famous example).
