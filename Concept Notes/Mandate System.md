# Mandate System

## Definition

The post-WWI arrangement by which Britain and France administered former Ottoman and German territories as "mandates" of the League of Nations. Article 22 of the League charter claimed that colonized peoples in Africa and Asia required "tutelage" from more "advanced" nations — racist paternalism that justified imperial expansion under a new name.

## Key Facts for the AP

The mandate system was formalized in the 1919 Treaty of Versailles and administered through the League of Nations. Former German colonies in Africa and former Ottoman territories in the Middle East were divided primarily between Britain and France. Japan received Germany's Pacific island holdings and Shandong concessions in China.

The system was explicitly hierarchical: League charter language divided mandates into three "classes" based on how close to self-governance the mandatory power deemed them. Class A mandates (mostly Middle Eastern) were theoretically close to independence; Classes B and C were further away. In practice, all remained under European control.

The system contradicted Wilson's Fourteen Points, which had raised colonial peoples' expectations of self-determination. This contradiction is one of the AP's most frequently tested ironies of the interwar period.

## AP Essay Angle

The mandate system is best understood as **continuity in imperial governance disguised as change**. The territories, the power relationships, and the racist justifications remained the same; only the administrative label changed from "colony" to "mandate." Use this as evidence in CCOT essays about imperialism in the early 20th century.
