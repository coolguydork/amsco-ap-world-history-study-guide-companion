# Opium Trade

The systematic British export of Indian-grown opium to China (c. 1760s–1917) that turned an unfavorable trade balance into a profitable imperial enterprise — and triggered two wars that forced China to open itself to Western commerce, extraterritoriality, and influence. The Opium Trade is the AP's clearest example of how economic imperialism and free-trade ideology could be weaponized to pry open resistant markets, even when the commodity was a drug that addicted millions.

## The Trade Balance Problem

For most of the 18th century, Britain and other European nations ran large trade **deficits** with China: European consumers wanted Chinese tea, silk, and porcelain, but Chinese consumers showed little interest in European manufactured goods. The Qing court insisted on payment in silver — a severe drain on European treasuries.

The British East India Company's solution: sell opium grown in Bengal to Chinese consumers. The drug was illegal in China, but Company ships smuggled it through Chinese merchants. By 1820, opium had reversed the trade balance — silver was flowing *out* of China and *into* British India.

## The Opium Wars

**First Opium War (1839–1842):** Qing Commissioner Lin Zexu confiscated and destroyed 20,000 chests of British opium at Canton. Britain responded with a naval expedition. The technologically superior British fleet devastated Chinese coastal defenses.

**Treaty of Nanjing (1842):** China ceded Hong Kong to Britain, opened five **treaty ports** (Canton, Shanghai, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Xiamen), paid a large indemnity, and granted Britain **most-favored-nation status**. The treaty system established: China could not control its own tariffs and foreigners enjoyed **extraterritoriality** (tried in their own courts, not Chinese courts).

**Second Opium War (1856–1860):** Britain and France launched a second war over treaty violations. China suffered further humiliations — the Summer Palace was looted and burned; opium was legalized in China; more treaty ports opened.

## Long-Term Consequences

- By 1900, an estimated 25–30% of the adult male population in some regions of China were opium addicts
- The "century of humiliation" — beginning with the Opium Wars — became central to Chinese nationalist memory and shaped the CCP's post-1949 rhetoric
- The treaty port system created semi-colonial zones of Western privilege that persisted until 1943
- The Qing dynasty's inability to resist the British exposed the weakness of traditional Chinese institutions, contributing to reform movements and eventually the 1911 revolution

> [!exam-tip]
> Use the Opium Trade as evidence of **economic imperialism**: Britain did not colonize China directly, but used unequal treaties, extraterritoriality, and trade leverage to extract enormous economic benefits without formal governance costs. This distinguishes economic imperialism (Topic 6.5) from direct colonial rule (Topic 6.2).
