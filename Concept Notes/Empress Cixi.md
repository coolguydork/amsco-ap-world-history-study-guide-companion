# Empress Cixi

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Empress Dowager Cixi** (1835–1908) was the de facto ruler of Qing China for nearly five decades and the most powerful political figure in late imperial China. She is the central symbol of **conservative resistance to industrialization and reform** in AP World History. Her most consequential act was the 1898 coup that ended the **Hundred Days of Reform**, setting China on a path toward revolution and collapse of the Qing dynasty in 1911.

---

## Key Facts (AMSCO pp. 320–321, 337)

- **Position**: Empress Dowager; aunt and adoptive mother of Emperor Guangxu; dominated the Qing court from 1861 until her death in 1908
- **1898 Coup**: When Emperor Guangxu launched the **Hundred Days of Reform** with reformer Kang Youwei, Cixi staged a coup d'état:
  - Placed Guangxu under **house arrest** on a lake island in the Forbidden City (he remained imprisoned until his death in 1908, one day before Cixi herself died)
  - **Repealed virtually all reform edicts**
  - Had several reform leaders executed; Kang Youwei fled into exile
- **Anti-technology stance**: Blocked the extension of **railroads and telegraph networks** into China's interior, fearing they would bring foreign influence and undermine Qing control
- **Boxer Rebellion (1900)**: Cixi initially supported the Boxer Rebellion — an anti-foreign, anti-Christian uprising — believing it could expel foreigners. When it failed catastrophically, foreign powers occupied Beijing and imposed even harsher terms on China

---

## The Irony: Late Concessions

In a bitter irony, toward the end of her rule Cixi herself acknowledged that China's civil service system was broken and supported **abolishing the civil service examination** — ending nearly **2,500 years of tradition**. But these concessions came too late for systematic reform under the Qing. (AMSCO p. 321)

> [!warning]
> Cixi is often portrayed as simply an obstacle to progress. The reality is more layered: she was protecting the Qing dynasty's political control, the interests of the Manchu nobility, and the scholar-gentry class that staffed the bureaucracy. Her conservatism was *strategic*, not merely ignorant. But its consequences for China were still devastating.

---

## Why the AP Exam Tests Her

Cixi appears on the AP exam as:
1. A **cause** of China's failure to industrialize effectively
2. A **contrast** to the Meiji reformers who embraced comprehensive change
3. An example of **conservative elites blocking modernization** (compare to Ottoman conservatives who resisted Tanzimat reforms)

> [!exam-tip]
> For comparison questions, contrast Cixi with Japan's Meiji oligarchs. Both faced similar external pressure (Western imperialism, threat of colonization). Japan's leaders sacrificed the samurai class's privileges for the sake of national modernization. Cixi protected traditional Qing structures at the cost of China's sovereignty.
