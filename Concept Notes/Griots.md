# Griots (and Griottes)

## What is a Griot?

A **griot** (also spelled *djeli* in Mande languages) was a professional oral historian, storyteller, musician, and counselor in West African societies — primarily among Mande-speaking peoples including the Mali Empire. The female equivalent is a **griottes**, who performed similar functions at social occasions such as weddings and coming-of-age ceremonies.

Griots were **specialists in memory**: they possessed encyclopedic knowledge of family genealogies, royal lineages, and the deeds of leaders. Because Sub-Saharan African societies largely operated through oral tradition rather than written records, the griot served the functions that scribes, archivists, historians, and court poets served in literate societies — combined in a single role.

> "When a griot dies, it is as though a library has burned."

---

## Social Role and Power

Griots held **enormous social power**:
- Kings and rulers sought their counsel on political matters
- Their praise or criticism of rulers shaped public reputation — "a griot could sing your success or sing your downfall"
- They were both **venerated** (as keepers of history) and **feared** (for the social power of their words)
- Griots were hereditary specialists — the role passed through family lines

Griots were also **musicians**, accompanying their oral recitations with instruments:
- The **kora** — a 12-string harp; the griot's signature instrument
- Drums
- Other percussive instruments

**Griottes** performed specifically at social transition events — weddings, funerals, initiation ceremonies. They counseled women navigating difficult family situations, providing empowerment within patriarchal societies by giving women a formal voice.

## Griots and the AP Exam

**Why this matters for AP World History:**

1. **Oral tradition as a historical source**: Griots remind students that written evidence is not the only valid form of historical record. The AP curriculum increasingly asks about the *limitations of sources* — griot oral traditions are a primary source category with specific strengths (comprehensive genealogical knowledge, continuous transmission) and weaknesses (subject to selective memory, embellishment, or distortion over time).

2. **The sourcing challenge**: The AP exam frequently includes documents from outside observers (like Ibn Battuta) about West African societies. These documents have an outsider's perspective bias. The griot tradition represents the *inside* perspective — the historical memory of the community itself — which was rarely written down and thus is underrepresented in the historical record.

3. **Cultural sophistication without writing**: When AP documents portray African societies as "less developed," griots are a specific counterexample: a highly sophisticated system of knowledge preservation that does not use writing but is no less rigorous.

> [!exam-tip]
> If an AP sourcing question asks you to identify a limitation of an external account of West Africa (e.g., Ibn Battuta's travelogue), you can note that it reflects an Islamic outsider's perspective and does not capture indigenous West African memory as preserved by griots. This demonstrates Historical Thinking Skill: Sourcing.
