# Adam Smith

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Adam Smith** (1723–1790) was a Scottish economist and philosopher whose *Wealth of Nations* (1776) provided the foundational intellectual justification for [[capitalism]] and [[laissez-faire]] economic policy. He replaced mercantilism as the dominant framework for thinking about trade and national wealth.

## AMSCO Reference (pp. 325–328)

> "In the Wealth of Nations (1776), Adam Smith, arguing that humans are naturally transactional, provided a foundational text in support of capitalism and the establishment of private entrepreneurship and shaped the economics and politics of the industrial age and the centuries to follow. Mercantilism, a system of economic protectionism, was replaced by a laissez-faire policy that promoted minimal governmental involvement in commerce."

The AMSCO text also quotes Smith directly: *"Man is an animal that makes bargains: no other animal does this — no dog exchanges bones with another."* (p. 325)

## From the Vault

**From [[Unit-5.7/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts]] (Topic 5.7):**
> Smith argued that humans are naturally transactional (self-interested) and that free markets, guided by the "invisible hand" of supply and demand, allocate resources more efficiently than governments can. Smith championed capitalism and laissez-faire and attacked mercantilism. His work is foundational to modern economics.

> [!exam-tip]
> *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) is a key text for the AP exam. Know that Smith: (1) published in 1776 — the same year as the American Declaration of Independence; (2) argued for free markets and against government interference; (3) provided the intellectual justification for industrial capitalism; and (4) critiqued mercantilism as inefficient and self-defeating.

## Historical Significance

Smith wrote during the early Industrial Revolution, before corporations and transnational companies dominated the economy. His arguments about individual entrepreneurs and free markets were extended by later thinkers to justify corporate capitalism — a context Smith himself never anticipated. Critics like [[Unit-5.8/Karl Marx & Socialism|Karl Marx]] directly engaged with and challenged Smith's framework.
