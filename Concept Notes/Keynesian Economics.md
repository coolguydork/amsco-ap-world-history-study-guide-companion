# Keynesian Economics

The economic theory developed by British economist **John Maynard Keynes** (1883–1946), arguing that free markets do not automatically self-correct during recessions or depressions. Governments must actively manage aggregate demand through fiscal policy — especially deficit spending and public works — to restore employment and growth. Became the dominant framework for Western economic policy from the New Deal (1930s) through the 1970s.

## Core Argument

Before Keynes, mainstream economics held that markets are self-regulating: unemployment causes wages to fall until employers hire again; recessions are temporary. Keynes rejected this view. During a depression, individuals and businesses cut spending simultaneously (the "paradox of thrift"), creating a downward spiral that markets cannot escape on their own. The solution: government must step in as the "spender of last resort," injecting demand through public works and social programs even if it means borrowing (deficit spending).

## Key Ideas

- **Aggregate demand:** Total spending in the economy; Keynes argued governments should manage it actively
- **Deficit spending:** Government borrows and spends during downturns; acceptable if it restores demand
- **Multiplier effect:** Each dollar of government spending circulates through the economy, creating more than one dollar of total activity
- **Counter-cyclical policy:** Spend during recessions; pay down debt during booms (though governments proved better at the first part)

## Historical Application: The New Deal

FDR's New Deal (1933–1939) was the first large-scale application of Keynesian ideas. Key programs: CCC (young men in conservation jobs), WPA (arts, infrastructure, writers' projects), TVA (regional electricity), and Social Security. The New Deal stabilized the economy and preserved democracy — but WWII rearmament spending was what truly ended Depression-era unemployment, vindicating Keynes's theory at scale.

## Contrast: Adam Smith vs. Keynes

| | Adam Smith (laissez-faire) | John Maynard Keynes |
|--|--------------------------|---------------------|
| Markets | Self-regulating; best left alone | Prone to failure; need government intervention |
| Government role | Minimal; protect property and contracts | Active; manage aggregate demand |
| Deficit spending | Irresponsible; governments should balance budgets | Necessary and beneficial during downturns |
| Depression response | Wait for market to self-correct | Stimulus spending immediately |
