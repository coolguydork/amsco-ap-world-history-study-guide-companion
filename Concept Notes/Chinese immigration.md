# Chinese Immigration

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Chinese immigration** in the 19th century refers primarily to the large-scale movement of Chinese laborers — overwhelmingly young men from Guangdong Province — to the Americas, Australia, and Southeast Asia in search of work. In the AP World History context, it appears most prominently as a labor story tied to the construction of the **Transcontinental Railroad** (United States, completed 1869) and as part of the broader pattern of global labor migration produced by industrialization.

## Context: Why Chinese Laborers?

Chinese immigration to the United States surged after the **California Gold Rush (1848–1855)** and accelerated with railroad construction in the 1860s. The Central Pacific Railroad, building eastward from California, recruited Chinese workers aggressively because:
- White American laborers demanded higher wages and were more willing to strike
- Chinese workers, often desperate and facing discrimination that limited other opportunities, accepted lower wages and worked in the most dangerous conditions — blasting through the Sierra Nevada mountains with nitroglycerin
- Contractors found them reliable and organized

Approximately **10,000–20,000 Chinese workers** built the western half of the Transcontinental Railroad, yet their names appear on no commemorative plaque at the 1869 completion ceremony at Promontory Summit, Utah.

## Broader Pattern: The "Coolie" Trade

Chinese labor migration was part of a global pattern that emerged after the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade. When enslaved African labor became legally unavailable, plantation owners and infrastructure builders in the Americas, Caribbean, and Southeast Asia turned to **indentured Chinese and Indian laborers** — often called "coolies" (a derogatory term). Conditions were frequently brutal and contracts were difficult to escape.

Destinations for Chinese laborers in this era included: the United States and Canada (railroads, mines), Peru and Cuba (plantations), Australia (gold fields), and Southeast Asia (tin mines, plantations in British Malaya and Dutch Indonesia).

## Backlash: Restriction and Exclusion

Chinese immigration produced significant nativist backlash. The United States passed the **Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** — the first major federal law restricting immigration by nationality and race — barring Chinese laborers from entering the country. Canada, Australia, and other destinations enacted similar restrictions.

> [!exam-tip]
> Chinese immigration appears on the AP exam as evidence for **global migration patterns** driven by industrialization (Unit 5), and as a case of **labor exploitation and racial hierarchy** (Units 5–6). Two strong arguments: (1) Chinese labor was essential to industrial infrastructure (railroads) in the Americas — industrialization depended on migrant workers, not just machines. (2) The Chinese Exclusion Act shows how racial discrimination structured global labor migration even as industrial capitalism needed that labor.
