# Atlantic Slave Trade

## Definition

The forced transportation of millions of Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas as enslaved laborers, c. 1500–1850. The largest forced migration in human history. Distinct from earlier forms of slavery (which existed in Africa, the Islamic world, and Europe) by its scale, its explicitly racial basis, and the hereditary chattel system it produced.

## Origins and Causes

The Atlantic slave trade emerged from the intersection of three forces (Topics 4.3–4.4):

1. **Columbian Exchange disease** — European contact killed up to 90% of indigenous American populations in some regions, collapsing the labor supply that encomienda and mit'a depended on.
2. **Plantation economy demand** — Sugar, tobacco, and rice cultivation required intensive, year-round labor that indentured servants (who worked only 5–7 years) could not reliably supply.
3. **African captive supply networks** — West African coastal kingdoms (Dahomey, Oyo, Kongo) raided inland neighbors and sold captives to European merchants in exchange for firearms — which enabled further raiding.

## The Middle Passage

The ocean crossing from West Africa to the Americas. Captive Africans were packed into cargo holds with minimal food, water, or space. Voyages lasted approximately six weeks. Overall, 10–15% of all transported Africans died during the crossing; some voyages lost up to half of captives. Rebellions were frequent but almost always suppressed. *(AMSCO p. 218–228)*

## Scale and Destinations

Approximately 12–13 million Africans were transported across the Atlantic between c. 1500 and 1850. Destinations by share: Portuguese colonies/Brazil (~39%), British West Indies (~18%), Spanish colonies (~18%), French colonies (~14%), British mainland North America (~6%).

## Effects on African Societies

*(AMSCO pp. 235–236 — [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview\|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]] (pp. 232–241))*

- **Population loss** — century-long declines in coastal West Africa; raids devastated inland communities
- **Political disruption** — kingdoms like Kongo were weakened as slave raiding bypassed royal authority
- **Firearms dependency** — raiding kingdoms (Dahomey, Oyo) became economically dependent on European goods, locking them into an escalating cycle of violence
- **Gender imbalance** — more than two-thirds of those enslaved were male; polygyny became more common in affected societies; women took on traditionally male economic roles
- **Long-term** — economic dependency on European trade goods set the stage for 19th-century imperialism ([[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview\|State Expansion Overview]] (pp. 375–387))

> [!warning]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to compare the Atlantic slave trade with the Indian Ocean slave trade. Key differences: the Atlantic trade was racially defined, hereditary, and uniquely large in scale. The Indian Ocean trade was older, involved both men and women, and enslaved people in Islamic communities had limited legal protections (e.g., the right to marry).

## Chattel Slavery

The legal system that made the Atlantic trade function: enslaved people defined as property to be bought, sold, and inherited. **Hereditary** — children of enslaved mothers were born enslaved regardless of father's status. Racially coded — created a system where African descent itself became the marker of enslavement. *(AMSCO p. 4.4 Key Terms)*

## AP Exam Threads

**Causation:** Disease → labor vacuum → chattel slavery is one of the most important causal chains in the AP curriculum. Do not confuse cause and effect — slavery did not cause indigenous death; indigenous death caused the expansion of slavery.

**Comparison:** Atlantic vs. Indian Ocean slave trade; Atlantic slave trade vs. earlier African forms of slavery; chattel slavery vs. encomienda vs. indentured servitude.

**CCOT:** What continued from pre-1450 slavery? (Slave trade in Africa and the Islamic world, coercive labor generally.) What was new? (Scale, racial hereditary system, Middle Passage, plantation economy.)

**Continuity into Unit 5+:** The racial hierarchies of the colonial period (casta system) and the disruption of West African societies both feed directly into 19th-century imperialism ([[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview\|State Expansion Overview]] (pp. 375–387)) and the abolition movement (Topics 5.1, 5.2).
