# Human Sacrifice

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

Human sacrifice — the ritual killing of human beings as an offering to deities — was practiced across multiple civilizations in the AP curriculum, most prominently in Mesoamerica and the Andes. It raises important questions about religious meaning, political legitimation, and the limits of European sources.

---

## The Americas

**From [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|Topic 1.4 Overview]]:**

The Maya, Aztec, and Inca all practiced human sacrifice, but to different degrees and with different theological rationales.

**Aztec (Mexica):** Human sacrifice was central and extensive. The theological basis was that the gods had sacrificed themselves to create the world — humans owed a cosmic debt repaid through sacrifice and bloodletting. Sacrifice also served a political function: publicly demonstrating the Aztec Empire's power and the gods' favor. Victims were typically war captives, which incentivized constant military expansion.

**Inca:** Human sacrifice occurred but less frequently. Priests determined the need through divination. Major events — famines, plagues, battles, royal deaths — could call for sacrifice. The Inca did not have the same cosmological framework of cosmic debt repayment as the Aztecs.

**Maya:** War captives were the primary sacrifice victims. Sacrifice was linked to religious calendars and rituals honoring deities of sun, rain, and corn.

## Sourcing Caution — AP Critical Thinking

> [!warning]
> Most detailed descriptions of Aztec human sacrifice come from **Spanish colonial sources** — conquistadors and priests who wrote after the conquest. These sources had powerful political and religious motivations to portray Aztec practices as extreme and barbaric, thereby justifying Spanish conquest and forced conversion. On an AP DBQ, this is a textbook HAPP analysis opportunity:
> - **Historical situation**: Post-conquest colonial period
> - **Audience**: European readers, Spanish Crown, Catholic Church
> - **Purpose**: Legitimate the conquest morally and religiously
> - **Perspective**: Conquistadors and missionaries with clear bias
>
> The actual scale of Aztec human sacrifice may have been exaggerated. The Florentine Codex (created by Spanish friar Sahagún with indigenous informants) is a more nuanced primary source but still post-conquest.

## AP Exam Significance

Human sacrifice appears most often on the AP exam as:
1. **Evidence of Aztec political-religious structure** (theocracy, emperor as divine representative)
2. **A sourcing challenge in DBQs** (Spanish colonial sources require HAPP analysis)
3. **A comparison point** between Aztec (extensive) and Inca (limited) religious practice
4. **Context for the Spanish conquest** — Spanish used Aztec religious practices to justify colonization to European audiences
