# Transnational Companies

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Transnational companies** are [[corporation|corporations]] that operate across national boundaries — producing, sourcing, selling, and investing in multiple countries. They emerged as a defining feature of the late Industrial Revolution and represent the intersection of industrial [[capitalism]] and imperial expansion.

## AMSCO Reference (pp. 325–328)

> "De Beers was one of many transnational companies — those that operated across national boundaries — that emerged in the 19th century. For example, the Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, a British-owned bank opened in its colony of Hong Kong in 1865, focused on finance, corporate investments, and global banking. The Unilever Corporation, a British and Dutch venture, focused on household goods — most famously, soap. By 1890 it had soap factories in Australia, Switzerland, the United States, and beyond. Unilever sourced the palm oil for its soaps first from British West Africa and later the Belgian Congo, where it operated huge plantations. Because these companies were transnational, they gained wealth and influence on a scale rarely approached before."

> "The overwhelming majority of railway workers in Africa were natives who were paid far lower wages than their European counterparts. Thus, railroad technology was a means of extracting as many resources as possible from subject lands while paying colonial laborers as little as possible."

> Transnational companies are a key example of how economic imperialism worked. De Beers, HSBC, and Unilever extracted resources and labor from colonized regions (Belgian Congo, British West Africa) and shipped profits back to Europe.

**From [[Unit-5.7/Themes & Historical Reasoning|Themes & Historical Reasoning]] (Topic 5.7):**
> Corporations needed raw materials (rubber, palm oil, cotton, diamonds) and markets. They lobbied governments for colonial protection of their operations. They paid colonial workers far less than European workers. They reinvested profits in Europe, not in colonies. In this way, corporate expansion and imperial expansion were inseparable.

## Why This Matters for AP

Transnational companies are the economic mechanism connecting Period 3 (industrialization) to Period 4 (New Imperialism). They explain *why* European powers colonized Africa and Asia — not just for national glory, but because corporations needed raw materials, cheap labor, and protected markets. This pattern — extracting colonial resources, paying minimal wages, repatriating profits to Europe — is the definition of economic imperialism.

> [!exam-tip]
> On the AP exam, use transnational companies as evidence in essays about New Imperialism (Unit 6) and economic change (Unit 5). The key argument: **the corporation's structure made economic imperialism possible at global scale** — no individual merchant or government alone could have built a diamond empire across southern Africa or a soap supply chain connecting Belgium, West Africa, and Australia.
