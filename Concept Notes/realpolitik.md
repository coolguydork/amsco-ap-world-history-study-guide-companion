# Realpolitik

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Realpolitik** (German: "politics of reality") is a pragmatic approach to politics and governance that prioritizes practical power and national interest over ideology, morality, or legal norms. The term is most associated with 19th-century European statecraft, particularly with **Count Cavour** (Italian unification) and **Otto von Bismarck** (German unification). In AP World History, it also applies to the strategic logic behind Japan's Meiji reforms.

## AMSCO Reference (Topic 5.2)

> "He [Cavour] also believed in the practical politics of reality, which came to be called realpolitik. Thus, he did not hesitate to advance the cause of Italian unity through manipulation. In 1858, he maneuvered Napoleon III of France into a war with Austria, hoping to weaken Austrian influence on the Italian Peninsula."

> "Prussian leader Otto von Bismarck, who like Cavour favored realpolitik, used nationalist feelings to engineer three wars to bring about German unification. Bismarck manipulated Austria into participating in two wars, the first with Prussia against Denmark (1864) and the second between Prussia and Austria (Seven Weeks' War of 1866). After winning both wars, Bismarck manipulated France into declaring war against Prussia. His armies beat the French soundly in the Franco-Prussian War (1870)."

## How Realpolitik Worked

Realpolitik practitioners:
- **Used nationalism as a tool** — not because they believed in romantic nationalist ideals, but because nationalism was politically useful for mobilizing populations and justifying wars
- **Manufactured conflicts** — engineered wars to weaken rivals and consolidate territory
- **Ignored ideology when inconvenient** — Bismarck, a conservative, allied with liberals and even socialists when it served Prussian interests
- **Measured success by outcomes, not principles** — the only question was whether a policy advanced the state's power

## Key Practitioners

| Figure | State | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Count Cavour | Piedmont-Sardinia (Italy) | Maneuvered France into war with Austria; allied with Mazzini's nationalists and Garibaldi's Red Shirts |
| Otto von Bismarck | Prussia (Germany) | Engineered three wars (1864, 1866, 1870) to unify Germany under Prussian dominance |
| Meiji oligarchs | Japan | Adopted Western institutions not from ideological belief but as strategic necessity to prevent colonization |

> [!exam-tip]
> Realpolitik is a strong **comparison and causation** term on the AP exam. Use it to explain *why* Bismarck and Cavour succeeded where idealistic nationalists like Mazzini failed — pragmatic manipulation of power beats romantic idealism. Also use it to frame Japan's Meiji Restoration: Japan "chose" Western institutions for strategic reasons, not cultural admiration.

> [!warning]
> Bismarck and Cavour are often described as "nationalist" leaders, but this is imprecise. Both were conservatives who *used* nationalism instrumentally. Bismarck in particular was deeply skeptical of mass politics and popular nationalism — he used nationalist sentiment as a tool to consolidate Prussian aristocratic power.
