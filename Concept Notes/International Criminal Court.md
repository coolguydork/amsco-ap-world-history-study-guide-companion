# International Criminal Court

Permanent international tribunal established by the Rome Statute (2002) to prosecute individuals for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide when national courts cannot or will not act. Based in The Hague, Netherlands.

## Origins

The ICC is the institutional culmination of the Nuremberg precedent: the 1945–1946 Nuremberg Trials established that individuals — including heads of state — can be held criminally accountable for atrocities. Post-WWII ad hoc tribunals (International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda) further established the precedent before the Rome Statute created a permanent court.

## AP Significance

- **Darfur:** ICC charged Sudan's President **Omar al-Bashir** with war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide — the first sitting head of state so indicted (2009). He remained in power for years, exposing limits of enforcement.
- **Yugoslavia:** The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (predecessor body) indicted **Slobodan Milošević**; he died in custody before verdict.
- These cases demonstrate that the "Never again" commitment has legal teeth but limited enforcement capacity when powerful states protect perpetrators.

## Limits

ICC enforcement depends on state cooperation; the U.S., China, and Russia have not ratified the Rome Statute. Geopolitical interests repeatedly shield perpetrators. The ICC's record illustrates the ongoing gap between international human rights norms and enforcement.
