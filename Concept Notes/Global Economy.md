# Global Economy

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

The **global economy** of the late 19th century was a qualitatively new phenomenon — the first time in history that producers, consumers, and investors across distant continents were linked together in near-real time through a combination of industrial transportation (railroads and steamships) and communication (telegraph, telephone, radio). This interconnection was both the product of the [[Concept Notes/Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and the mechanism through which [[Concept Notes/New Imperialism|New Imperialism]] operated.

## AMSCO Reference (pp. 313)

> "Railroads, steamships, and a new invention called the telegraph made exploration, development, and communication possible. The telegraph allowed immediate communication... Products of industrialization, such as the railroad, steamship, and the telegraph, directly linked farmers, miners, manufacturers, customers, and investors globally for the first time in history."

> "The desire for capital, money available to invest in a business, was a driving force domestically and abroad... With the development of the railroad and steamships, such countries as Great Britain, Germany, and the United States intensified industrialization, increasing the need for resources. Industrialized countries sought to protect their access to resources and markets by establishing colonies."

## From the Vault

**From [[Unit-5.5/The Two Industrial Revolutions]] (Topic 5.5):**
> "Before [railroads, steamships, and telegraph]: trade was slow, expensive, and limited largely to coastal zones and navigable rivers. Communication took weeks or months across long distances. Markets were regional, not global."

> "After: A cotton farmer in Egypt, a textile mill in Manchester, a banker in London, and a consumer in Boston were all connected economically. Price signals moved globally via telegraph — commodity prices became global, not local. Industrial nations could extract resources from distant colonies efficiently, then sell finished goods back — the technological foundation of [[Concept Notes/New Imperialism|New Imperialism]]."

## What Made It Possible

| Technology | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Railroads** | Opened continental interiors to resource extraction; lowered overland transport costs dramatically |
| **Steamships** | Reliable, scheduled ocean transport; ended wind dependency; enabled coaling-station networks |
| **Telegraph** | Near-real-time price signals, market coordination, military orders across oceans |
| **Telephone / Radio** | Extended communication to voice and wireless, deepening global integration |

## Structural Consequence: Core and Periphery

The global economy of this era was structured asymmetrically. Industrialized nations (the "core" — Britain, Germany, United States, France) supplied finished manufactured goods. Colonized and semi-colonized regions (the "periphery" — India, Egypt, sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America) supplied raw materials. [[Concept Notes/colonialism|Colonialism]] enforced this division by using tariffs, legal restrictions, and military force to prevent industrialization in colonized regions.

> [!exam-tip]
> On AP essays, "global economy" by itself is vague. Make the argument specific: explain the *mechanism* (telegraph + railroad + steamship → near-real-time price signals → commodity markets became global → colonial resource extraction became profitable at scale → New Imperialism). A strong essay traces causation, not just correlation.
