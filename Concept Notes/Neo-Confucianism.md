# Neo-Confucianism

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

## Definition

**Neo-Confucianism** is a syncretic philosophical and ethical system that evolved in China between approximately 770 and 840 CE. It combined the rational ethical framework of classical [[Confucianism]] with the more abstract spiritual and metaphysical ideas of [[Daoism]] and [[Buddhism]]. Unlike earlier Confucianism (which focused on proper social relationships and governance), Neo-Confucianism engaged more deeply with questions of cosmology and moral cultivation — while still emphasizing ethics over theology or mysticism.

## Origin and Spread

Neo-Confucianism emerged during the Tang-Song transition and became the dominant intellectual framework of the [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]] (960–1279). It spread outward via the [[tributary system]] and cultural influence:

- **Korea** — adopted by the educated elite; structured government and family life
- **Japan** — influential among the samurai class and in governance
- **Vietnam** — adopted by scholar-officials, though adapted to local political culture

See [[Unit-1.1/Japan Korea Vietnam|Japan, Korea & Vietnam]] for regional comparisons.

## Key Features

- **Syncretic**: fused Confucian ethics with Buddhist introspection and Daoist naturalism
- **Ethics-centered**: emphasized moral cultivation and proper social relationships
- **Reinforced hierarchy**: supported filial piety, deference to rulers, and the patriarchal family structure

## Why It Matters for the AP Exam

Neo-Confucianism illustrates: **syncretism**, **cultural diffusion** from China outward, **continuity with change** in Chinese intellectual tradition, and **social structure** reinforcement across East Asia.

> "Neo-Confucianism evolved in China between 770 and 840. It was a syncretic system, combining rational thought with the more abstract ideas of Daoism and Buddhism... It became immensely popular in the countries in China's orbit, including Japan, Korea, and Vietnam." — AMSCO p. 8
