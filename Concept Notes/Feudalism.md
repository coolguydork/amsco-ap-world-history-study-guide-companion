## What It Is

**Feudalism** is a decentralized political and social system in which local lords control land and the people who work it, in exchange for military service and loyalty to a higher authority. It typically features hereditary hierarchies with very little social mobility — people are born into their roles as rulers, warriors, or peasants.

Feudalism arose in multiple regions independently when central authority broke down and local strongmen filled the power vacuum. The AP exam covers two main feudal systems: **Japanese** and **European**.

---

## Why It Matters for the AP Exam

Feudalism is a key comparison topic. The AP frequently asks students to compare Japanese and European feudalism, or to contrast both with centralized systems like Song China's meritocratic bureaucracy.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves to test: (1) similarities between Japanese and European feudalism, (2) the key difference (daimyo were more powerful relative to the political center than European nobles were), and (3) the contrast between both feudal systems and Song China's merit-based bureaucracy.

## Japanese Feudalism — [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview\|East Asia Overview]] (pp. 3–14) (AMSCO pp. 9–10)

Japanese feudalism developed as central imperial authority declined, with powerful landowning aristocrats (**daimyo**) competing for control. Key features:

| Role | Japanese Term | Function |
|------|--------------|----------|
| Military ruler | Shogun | Real political power; emperor is figurehead |
| Lords | Daimyo | Control vast land; more powerful than the shogun in practice |
| Warriors | Samurai | Serve daimyo; follow bushido code |
| Peasants | Serfs | Hereditary agricultural laborers; no social mobility |

**Bushido** — the warrior code of the samurai — stressed frugality, loyalty, martial arts, and honor unto death.

The **Kamakura Shogunate** (1192–1333) established Japan's first feudal government under a shogun. The daimyo in Japan wielded *more* effective power relative to the political center than European nobles did — the emperor and even the shogun were often subordinate in practice to the strongest daimyo.

## European Feudalism

European feudalism arose from the collapse of the Western Roman Empire and the inability of weak monarchies to provide security. Key features mirrored Japan's but with important differences:

| Role | European Term | Function |
|------|--------------|----------|
| Political center | Monarch | Above nobles in theory; real power varied |
| Lords | Nobles / knights | Hold land as fiefs from the monarch |
| Warriors | Knights | Follow chivalry code |
| Peasants | Serfs | Hereditary agricultural laborers |

**Chivalry** — the European warrior code — emphasized duty to God, duty to women (courtly love), and duty to countrymen.

*See also [[Unit-1.6/Medieval Europe Overview\|Medieval Europe Overview]] (pp. 53–64) for full treatment of European feudalism.*

## Key Comparison: Japan vs. Europe vs. Song China

| Feature | Japanese Feudalism | European Feudalism | Song China |
|---------|-------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Political structure | Decentralized; shogun/daimyo | Decentralized; monarch/nobles | Centralized imperial bureaucracy |
| Social mobility | Hereditary; very limited | Hereditary; very limited | Limited but possible via civil service exam |
| Warrior class | Samurai (bushido) | Knights (chivalry) | Scholar-gentry (not warriors) |
| Lords' power | Daimyo > political center | Nobles < monarch (in theory) | No hereditary lords; officials appointed |
| Economy | Subsistence; land-based | Subsistence; land-based | Commercialized; market-oriented |

> [!tip]
> Song China is the **contrast** to both feudal systems. While Japan and Europe were decentralized, hereditary, and subsistence-based, Song China had a meritocratic bureaucracy, an urban commercial economy, and a literate scholar class. This makes Song China and medieval Europe the sharpest comparison in Unit 1.
