# Feudalism

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Feudalism** is a decentralized political and economic system in which a ruling class of landowners (lords, nobles, daimyo) controls territory and extracts labor or military service from those below them in exchange for protection. No strong central government exists; power is fragmented among competing regional elites.

Feudalism appeared independently in **Japan** and **Western Europe** during the medieval period — one of AP World History's most productive **cross-regional comparisons**.

---

## Japanese Feudalism (Topic 1.1)

Japan's feudal system emerged from centuries of weak imperial authority. By 1192, the **Minamoto clan** installed a **shogun** (military ruler), formalizing a system in which:

- **Daimyo** (landowning aristocrats) held real territorial power — often more power than the shogun
- **Samurai** served the daimyo as a warrior class, bound by **[[Concept Notes/Bushido|Bushido]]**
- The majority of people were **rice farmers** (de facto serfs)
- The **emperor** was a ceremonial figurehead with little real authority

> [!warning]
> In Japan, the **daimyo were more powerful than the shogun** in practice — the inverse of what Confucian hierarchy would suggest. This is a common AP exam trap.

---

## European Feudalism (Topic 1.6)

Western Europe's feudal system emerged after the collapse of the Roman Empire and Charlemagne's empire. Key features:

- **Monarchs** nominally topped the hierarchy but depended on nobles for military support
- **Nobles/lords** held land (fiefs) granted by the monarch in exchange for loyalty and troops
- **Knights** formed the warrior class, governed by **chivalry**
- **Serfs** were bound to the land and provided agricultural labor

---

## Comparison: Japanese vs. European Feudalism

| Feature | Japanese Feudalism | European Feudalism |
|---|---|---|
| Warrior class | **Samurai** | **Knights** |
| Landowners | **Daimyo** | **Nobles/Lords** |
| Laborers | **Serfs (rice farmers)** | **Serfs** |
| Top figure | **Emperor** (ceremonial) + **Shogun** (real power) | **Monarch** (real power) |
| Who held most power | **Daimyo** — often more powerful than the shogun | **Monarch** — above nobility, though power varied |
| Warrior code | **[[Concept Notes/Bushido|Bushido]]** — frugality, loyalty, martial arts, honor unto death | **Chivalry** — duty to God, countrymen, and women |
| Religion's role | [[Concept Notes/Shinto|Shinto]] and Zen Buddhism shaped Bushido | Christianity underpinned chivalry and kingship |
| Cause of decentralization | Weak imperial tradition; daimyo competition | Collapse of Rome; no strong successor state |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves this comparison. The key *similarity* is both are decentralized systems with a landowning military elite and a subordinated laboring class. The key *difference* is who holds real power at the top — daimyo in Japan, monarchs (theoretically) in Europe.

## AP Themes

**Governance:** Feudalism is the *absence* of centralized governance. Both Japan and Europe show how political fragmentation can persist for centuries, and how strong central states eventually emerged to replace feudal systems (the shogunate eventually unified under Tokugawa in 1603; European monarchies consolidated in the early modern period).

**Social Structures:** Feudalism creates rigid, hereditary hierarchies. Social mobility within a feudal system is extremely limited — birth determines status. Contrast with Song China's civil service exam (meritocracy) or the Islamic world's more fluid social structures.
