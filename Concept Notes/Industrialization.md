# Industrialization

The transformation from agrarian/artisan economies to factory-based, machine-powered manufacturing; reshaped class structures, urbanization patterns, global trade, and the relationship between states and their citizens. Began in Britain c. 1760–1840 (coal, iron, steam engine, textile mills), then spread to Western Europe, the U.S., and Japan by the late 19th century. By 1900, industrialized nations used their economic and military superiority to dominate non-industrialized regions through imperialism.

**Why Britain first?** Historians point to: abundant coal and iron deposits near navigable rivers, a secure island location reducing military spending, a colonial empire supplying raw materials and markets, an agricultural revolution freeing up labor, and institutions (patent law, Bank of England, Parliament) that protected property and encouraged investment.

**Social consequences:** Created the industrial working class (proletariat), urban slums and public health crises, child and female labor in factories, and eventually labor unions, socialism, and government reform movements as responses.

> [!exam-tip]
> Industrialization is the single most consequential development in Unit 5 and drives much of Units 6–8. For any LEQ about causes or effects, think in three layers: *economic* (factory system, global trade), *social* (new class structures, urbanization, gender changes), and *political* (reform movements, nationalism, imperialism). The AP frequently asks you to compare industrialization across regions — Britain vs. Japan vs. Russia vs. Egypt is a highly testable comparison.

> [!tip]
> The key AP contrast is between states that industrialized (Britain, France, U.S., Germany, Japan) and those that didn't (most of Africa, Latin America, parts of Asia). Non-industrialization made regions *targets* of imperialism rather than practitioners of it — this is the structural explanation for why the Scramble for Africa happened when it did.
