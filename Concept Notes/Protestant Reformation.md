# Protestant Reformation

## Definition

The 16th-century fracture of Western Christianity that produced Lutheranism, Calvinism, Anglicanism, and dozens of other Protestant denominations, permanently ending the Catholic Church's monopoly on Christian religious authority in Europe. Dates conventionally from Martin Luther's *Ninety-Five Theses* (1517) through the Peace of Westphalia (1648).

## Causes

*(Topics 4.5, 4.6)*

- **Gutenberg printing press (c. 1450s)** — made vernacular religious texts, including Luther's *Theses*, cheap and widely reproducible; accelerated the spread of reform ideas beyond the Church's control
- **Corruption and indulgences** — the Church sold indulgences (remissions of sin) to fund St. Peter's Basilica; Luther's *Theses* attacked this as theological fraud
- **Humanist scholarship** — Renaissance humanists had already encouraged critical reading of ancient texts, including scripture; Erasmus's Greek New Testament (1516) exposed errors in the Latin Vulgate
- **Political fractures** — German princes used Luther's movement to assert independence from both the Pope and Holy Roman Emperor Charles V; the Reformation gave political cover to break from Rome

## Major Branches

| Branch | Founder | Key Beliefs | Political Context |
|--------|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| Lutheranism | Martin Luther | Salvation by faith alone; scripture over tradition | German princes; Peace of Augsburg (1555) |
| Calvinism | John Calvin | Predestination; strict moral discipline | Geneva; Netherlands; Scotland (Presbyterians) |
| Anglicanism | Henry VIII / Elizabeth I | King as head of church; doctrinally moderate | England; split from Rome over Henry's divorce |
| Anabaptism | Various | Adult baptism; radical separation of church/state | Persecuted by both Catholics and Protestants |

## Catholic Counter-Reformation

The Church's internal response: the Council of Trent (1545–1563) clarified doctrine, eliminated the worst abuses, and launched the **Jesuit order** (founded by Ignatius of Loyola, 1540) as a missionary and educational force. Jesuits became major actors in global conversion — including in Asia (Japan, China) and the Americas.

## Political Consequences

*(Topics 4.5, 4.6)*

- **Religious wars** — the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) devastated central Europe; the French Wars of Religion produced the St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre (1572)
- **Peace of Westphalia (1648)** — ended the Thirty Years' War; established the principle that rulers determine their territory's religion (*cuius regio, eius religio*); a foundational moment in the development of the modern nation-state system
- **English Civil War / Glorious Revolution** — Protestant–Catholic tensions (and Anglican-Puritan divisions) drove English constitutional crisis through the 17th century; the Glorious Revolution (1688) established constitutional monarchy and limited royal power, directly inspiring Enlightenment thinkers
- **Colonial settlement** — Protestant sects seeking religious freedom (Puritans, Quakers, Huguenots) became major drivers of British and French colonial settlement in North America

## Connection to the Enlightenment

*(Topic 5.1)*

The Reformation's core move — challenging established authority on principled grounds — was a prerequisite for Enlightenment thought. Hobbes wrote *Leviathan* (1651) against the backdrop of English religious war; Locke's *Two Treatises* (1689) drew on the Glorious Revolution as proof that governments can be legitimately overthrown. The Reformation demonstrated that intellectual authority could be challenged; the Enlightenment applied the same logic to political authority.

> [!tip]
> **AP comparison thread:** Protestant Reformation (challenge to Catholic religious authority in Europe) ↔ Sunni–Shia split (challenge to Islamic political-religious authority, Topic 3.1). Both are internal religious fractures used by political actors as weapons. Both ended in partial negotiated settlements. Key difference: the Shia–Sunni split was about political succession as much as theology; the Reformation involved new doctrinal claims about salvation.

## AP Exam Threads

**Causation:** Printing press → rapid spread of Lutheran ideas = classic technology-driving-change argument.

**Continuity and Change:** Catholic Church had dominated European religious life since the fall of Rome; the Reformation is the sharpest break in that continuity.

**Comparison:** Protestant Reformation vs. Sunni–Shia split; Catholic Counter-Reformation vs. Islamic reform movements.

**Connection to Revolution:** Glorious Revolution (4.6) → Locke's political theory (5.1) → American and French Revolutions (5.2). The Reformation is the starting point of this chain.
