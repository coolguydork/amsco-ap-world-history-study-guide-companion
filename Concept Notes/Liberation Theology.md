# Liberation Theology

**Origin:** Latin America, 1950s–1960s
**Character:** Catholic theological movement combining Christian ethics with socialist economics
**Core argument:** The Church has a moral obligation to help the poor achieve social justice; Jesus's teachings mandate liberation from political and economic oppression

Liberation theology emerged in the context of extreme inequality in Latin America — military dictatorships, landlessness, and poverty — and offered a religious framework for demanding reform. It called for wealth redistribution and challenged the Catholic Church's historical alliance with ruling elites.

**Key figures and events:**
- **Oscar Romero** — Archbishop of El Salvador, martyred in 1980 while celebrating mass after publicly denouncing military violence against the poor
- **Nicaragua** — Liberation theologians helped the Sandinista movement topple the Somoza dictatorship (1979) and institute a socialist government
- **Hugo Chavez (Venezuela)** — deeply influenced by liberation theology in his political philosophy
- **Pope Francis (2013)** — first Latin American pope; reversed the Vatican's opposition to liberation theology, giving the movement institutional legitimacy after decades of suppression

**State responses:** Military dictatorships in Brazil, Argentina, Chile, El Salvador, and Guatemala persecuted, imprisoned, and killed priests and nuns who embraced liberation theology. The Vatican under Pope John Paul II also distanced itself from the movement in the 1980s, viewing its socialist elements as incompatible with Catholic teaching.

**AP Significance:** Liberation theology is an AP-relevant example of: (1) religious reform movements challenging political authority; (2) the intersection of CDI (religious ideas) and GOV (state repression/reform); (3) a distinctly Global South adaptation of a religion introduced through colonialism (Catholicism) to serve anti-colonial and anti-authoritarian ends.
