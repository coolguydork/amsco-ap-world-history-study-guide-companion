# Utilitarianism

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Utilitarianism** is a philosophical and political framework associated most prominently in the AP World History context with **John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873). Its central principle: the right policy is whatever produces **"the greatest good for the greatest number."**

---

## Core Ideas

Rather than deriving ethics from religion, tradition, or abstract principles, utilitarianism evaluates actions and policies by their **consequences** — specifically, whether they maximize well-being and minimize suffering across society. It is a fundamentally secular, empirical approach to morality and governance.

**Applied to industrialization**, utilitarianism led Mill to argue:
- Labor unions should be legalized (they produce better outcomes for workers)
- Child labor should be restricted (children's welfare is part of "the greatest good")
- Factory conditions should be regulated by law (unsafe conditions produce unnecessary suffering)

---

## Utilitarianism vs. Alternatives

| Framework | Core Idea | Response to Industrial Problems |
|---|---|---|
| **Laissez-faire capitalism** | Markets self-regulate; government should not interfere | Accept industrial conditions as the natural result of free markets |
| **Utilitarianism (Mill)** | Reform capitalism's worst abuses; judge policy by outcomes | Regulate markets to protect workers; keep capitalism but add guardrails |
| **Marxism** | Capitalism is structurally exploitative; replace it | Overthrow capitalism; worker control of production |

> [!tip]
> Think of utilitarianism as the **moderate reform** position — between laissez-faire defenders on the right and socialists on the left. Mill represents the tradition that eventually produced the modern welfare state.

---

## Utilitarianism's Founders

The philosophical foundation of utilitarianism was laid by **Jeremy Bentham** (1748–1832), who proposed a "felicific calculus" — a way of measuring pleasure and pain to guide policy. Mill inherited and humanized Bentham's framework, arguing that some forms of pleasure (intellectual, moral) are higher than others — not all satisfactions are equal.

---

## Legacy

Mill's specific proposals — unions, child labor limits, factory safety — became mainstream legislation in Britain by the late 19th and early 20th centuries. His broader framework influenced liberal political philosophy across the industrialized world.

Mill was also a pioneering advocate for **women's rights** — his essay *The Subjection of Women* (1869) argued for equal legal and political status. This places him in sharp contrast with the Tanzimat reforms, which largely excluded women.
