# Primogeniture

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

## Quick Summary

**Primogeniture** is the legal principle that the entire estate of a deceased person passes to their eldest son (or, in some systems, eldest child). In the context of AP World History, it appears as one of the **push factors** that drove European emigration and exploration in the period 1450–1750.

## As a Cause of Exploration

From [[Unit-4.1/Technological Innovations Overview]]:
> "Not all sons of the wealthy could own land because primogeniture laws gave all of each estate to the eldest son. In the early 17th century, religious minorities searched for a place to settle where people were tolerant of their dissent."

Primogeniture created a class of younger sons from wealthy families who had social standing but no land or inheritance. These men had:
- Education and connections (from wealthy families)
- Ambition and need for wealth (no inheritance coming)
- Motivation to seek fortune elsewhere — through military service, trade, the church, or overseas exploration

This made them a ready supply of men willing to risk dangerous ocean voyages in search of land, wealth, and status.

## Broader Context

Primogeniture was part of a larger pattern of **demographic pressure** pushing Europeans into exploration:
- Population growth meant not all workers could find employment
- Religious minorities were persecuted and sought tolerant settlements
- Younger sons of the wealthy needed alternative paths to status
- Those seeking adventure and glory were drawn to exploration

All of these groups contributed to a global demographic shift as emigration intensified.
