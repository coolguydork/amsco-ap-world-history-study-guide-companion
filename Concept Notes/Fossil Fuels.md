# Fossil Fuels

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Fossil fuels** are energy sources derived from the decomposed remains of ancient plants and animals — primarily **coal** and **petroleum** (oil). They powered both waves of the [[Concept Notes/Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]] and remain the energy foundation of the modern global economy. Their exploitation marked one of the most consequential shifts in human history: from organic energy sources (wood, water, animal muscle) to stored geological energy that could be burned on demand, anywhere.

## AMSCO Reference (pp. 311–312)

> "Coal was vital to industrialization because when burned it could power the steam engine. The burning of this fossil fuel, an energy source derived from plant and animal remains, was also essential in the process of separating iron from its ore."

> "Oil: In the mid-1800s, the first commercial oil wells were drilled, tapping into a vast new resource of energy. Petroleum, like coal, is a fossil fuel, an energy source derived from plant and animal remains. At first, the most important product from petroleum was kerosene, which was used for lighting and heaters. In 1847, inventors developed chemical techniques to extract kerosene from petroleum."

> "These techniques led to other developments, such as precision machinery and the internal combustion engine, which in turn led to automobile and airplane technologies. When automobiles were introduced in the early 1900s, gasoline as fuel became a more important product from petroleum than kerosene."

## Two Fossil Fuels, Two Revolutions

| Fuel | Era | Primary Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Coal** | First Industrial Revolution (c. 1760–1850) | Steam engines, iron smelting, railroads, steamships |
| **Petroleum** | Second Industrial Revolution (c. 1850–1914) | Kerosene lighting → internal combustion engine → automobiles, airplanes; gasoline as primary product by 1900s |

## Strategic Consequences

Fossil fuel dependence created new **strategic geographies**:
- Nations with coal deposits (Britain, Germany, the United States) had natural industrial advantages
- [[Concept Notes/Coaling stations|Coaling stations]] became the strategic infrastructure of naval power — whoever controlled refueling points controlled sea lanes
- Petroleum created new strategic prizes in the Middle East in the 20th century (beyond this unit's scope)
