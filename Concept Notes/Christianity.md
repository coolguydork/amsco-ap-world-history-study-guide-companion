# Christianity

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Christianity** is a monotheistic religion founded in the 1st century CE in the eastern Roman Empire, based on the life, teachings, death, and resurrection of **Jesus of Nazareth**, whom Christians believe to be the Son of God and the messiah prophesied in Jewish scripture. By Period 1 (c. 1200–c. 1450), Christianity was the dominant religion of Europe and a major civilizational force shaping governance, culture, and social life.

---

## Core Beliefs

- **Monotheism**: belief in one God in three persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (the Trinity)
- **Jesus Christ**: believed to be fully divine and fully human; his death atones for human sin; his resurrection offers believers eternal life
- **Bible**: the sacred scripture (Old Testament / Hebrew Bible + New Testament)
- **Salvation**: liberation from sin through faith in Christ; eternal life with God
- **Church**: the institutional body of believers; mediates between God and humanity through sacraments, clergy, and scripture

---

## Major Divisions by Period 1

By c. 1200, Christianity had split into two major branches:

| Branch | Center | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Roman Catholic Church** | Rome (Pope) | Western Europe; Latin liturgy; papal authority over secular rulers; celibate clergy |
| **Eastern Orthodox Church** | Constantinople (Patriarch) | Byzantine Empire and Eastern Europe; Greek liturgy; closer church-state alignment; married clergy permitted |

> [!exam-tip]
> The **Great Schism (1054)** divided Catholic and Orthodox Christianity. This matters for AP because it explains why Western and Eastern Europe developed differently — and why Russia and the Balkans followed Byzantine rather than Roman cultural patterns.

---

## Christianity and Governance in Period 1

In medieval Western Europe, the **Catholic Church** was inseparable from political life:

- The Pope claimed authority over secular rulers (the power to crown or excommunicate kings)
- Church law (**canon law**) governed marriage, inheritance, and education alongside secular law
- Monasteries served as centers of literacy, scholarship, and agricultural development
- The **Crusades** (1095–1291) were religiously sanctioned military campaigns — demonstrating Christianity's power to mobilize political and military action

In the **Byzantine Empire**, the Orthodox Church reinforced imperial authority rather than competing with it. The emperor was seen as God's representative on earth — a fusion of religious and political power.

---

## Christianity and Social Structure

- **Clergy** formed a privileged class exempt from certain taxes and secular jurisdiction
- **Women** in Christian Europe had fewer legal rights than women in Islamic law — they could not inherit property equally, had limited divorce rights, and were excluded from clergy
- **Serfs** were bound to the land in the feudal system; the Church reinforced social hierarchy as divinely ordained
- **Merchants** occupied a morally ambiguous position — the Church condemned usury (lending at interest), complicating commercial development

> [!exam-tip]
> On AP comparison questions about women's legal status: **Islamic law formally gave women more rights** (property ownership, some divorce rights) than Christian law in the same period. However, both societies restricted women's public roles in practice. Nuance matters.

---

## Christianity and Cultural Developments

- **Scholasticism**: the intellectual tradition of reconciling Christian theology with classical (especially Aristotelian) philosophy; centered in cathedral schools and early universities (Paris, Oxford, Bologna)
- **Gothic cathedrals**: massive religious architecture expressing Christian cosmology and civic pride
- **Illuminated manuscripts**: hand-copied texts produced in monasteries; preserved classical learning through the early medieval period
- **Icons and religious art**: in the Orthodox tradition, icons (sacred images) were central to devotional practice

---

## Christianity and the Crusades

The **Crusades** (1095–1291) were a series of Catholic military campaigns to retake Jerusalem and the Holy Land from Muslim control. AP significance:
- Increased contact between Christian Europe and the Islamic world → cultural and commercial exchange
- Introduced Europeans to goods, ideas, and trade routes they had been cut off from
- Contributed to the eventual rise of Italian merchant cities (Venice, Genoa) as intermediaries in East-West trade
- Deepened the Catholic-Orthodox rift when Crusaders sacked Constantinople in 1204

---

## Christianity in Period 1: The Big Picture

By 1200, Christianity dominated Europe but had lost the Crusades and was increasingly pressured by a wealthier, more intellectually dynamic Islamic world. The Church's political power peaked in the 13th century and began to face internal challenges (corruption, calls for reform) that would eventually produce the [[Concept Notes/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]] in the 16th century.
