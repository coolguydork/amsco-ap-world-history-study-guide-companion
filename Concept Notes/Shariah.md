# Shariah

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

## Definition

**Shariah** is Islamic law derived from the Quran (the holy book of Islam) and the *hadith* (recorded sayings and actions of the Prophet Muhammad). It governs personal conduct, family law, commercial transactions, and criminal justice — essentially a comprehensive legal and moral framework for Muslim life.

## Role in Dar al-Islam

Shariah served as the most powerful force of **cultural and legal unity** across the politically fragmented Islamic world. Even as the Abbasid Caliphate declined and Turkic successor states (Mamluks, Seljuks, Delhi Sultanate) replaced Arab-led governance, shariah provided a **common legal system** that connected Muslims from Spain to Central Asia.

> "The common use of shariah created similar legal systems. Great universities in Baghdad, Iraq; Cordoba, Spain; Cairo, Egypt; and Bukhara in Central Asia created centers for sharing intellectual innovations." — AMSCO p. 16

## Shariah and Women's Rights

Shariah's treatment of women was notably more generous than comparable systems in the medieval Christian or Jewish worlds:
- Women could **inherit property** and retain it after marriage
- Women could **initiate divorce** under certain conditions
- Divorced women received a **cash settlement**
- Women could **remarry** if widowed
- Women were **protected from retaliation** for testifying in court (though their testimony was worth half a man's)

These protections gave Islamic women a formal legal standing unusual for the era. See [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]] for the full context.

## Why It Matters for the AP Exam

Shariah is central to understanding:
- What held **Dar al-Islam together** politically and culturally despite fragmentation
- The **status of women** in Islamic society (often a comparison question)
- How **governance and religion intertwined** in the Islamic world
- The later **Ottoman millet system** (Topic 3.1), which coexisted shariah with non-Muslim legal traditions
