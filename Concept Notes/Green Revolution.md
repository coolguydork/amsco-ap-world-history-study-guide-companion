# Green Revolution

An agricultural transformation beginning in the 1960s that introduced high-yield crop varieties (especially wheat and rice), expanded irrigation, and increased use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides, dramatically increasing global food production. Associated with agronomist Norman Borlaug, who won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. The Green Revolution reduced the risk of famine in South Asia and Latin America but also displaced small farmers and caused significant environmental damage.

---

## Key Mechanisms

**Crossbreeding:** Borlaug and his colleagues produced new wheat strains by hybridizing existing varieties to combine high-yield and disease-resistance traits. These were first deployed in Mexico, then India and Pakistan in the 1960s.

**Genetic engineering:** A later phase (1980s–present) in which scientists directly manipulated crop DNA to create genetically modified organisms (GMOs) with even greater pest resistance, drought tolerance, or nutritional content.

**Agricultural inputs:** New crop varieties worked best with chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and expanded irrigation — inputs that were expensive and not universally accessible.

---

## Benefits

- Wheat production in India roughly tripled between 1965 and 1980; rice production surged across Asia
- Averted feared mass famines in South Asia despite rapid population growth
- Reduced the percentage of the population engaged in agriculture in many countries, freeing labor for industrial and service sectors

---

## Costs and Trade-offs

- Small farmers unable to afford new inputs were forced to sell to large landowners → increased land concentration
- Heavy chemical use degraded soil quality and contaminated waterways
- Mechanized techniques reduced agricultural employment
- Loss of traditional seed varieties as new engineered plants were adopted
- Borlaug himself warned in his 1970 Nobel lecture that the Green Revolution bought only "a breathing space" — population growth could again outstrip food supply

> [!warning]
> The AP exam consistently rewards nuanced treatment of the Green Revolution. Never describe it as an unqualified success. Always pair the benefit (increased yields → reduced famine) with the cost (inequality, environment) and the limit (Borlaug's own warning about population growth).
