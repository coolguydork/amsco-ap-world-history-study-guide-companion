# Colonialism

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Colonialism** is the policy and practice by which one state extends political and economic control over another territory and its people, typically for economic exploitation. In AP World History, colonialism appears across multiple periods, but its industrial-age form (c. 1750–1914) is particularly important for understanding how global inequality was structured.

## Colonialism as Deindustrialization — The India Case

The AP exam's favorite example of how colonialism actively *prevented* development is India under British rule.

**From [[Unit-5.4/Global Spread of Industry]] (Topic 5.4, AMSCO pp. 305–307):**
> "Just as it stifled the production of ships and iron, British colonization also affected textile production in India. As the textile industry flourished in India, it undermined the British textile mills in Britain, specifically in Lancaster. The owners of the Lancaster textile mills pressured the British government in India to impose an 'equalizing' five percent tax on all textiles produced at the more than 80 mills operating in Bombay, thus undermining their profitability."

> "During the period of company rule — British East India Company control over parts of the Indian subcontinent from 1757 to 1858 — steep British tariffs led to the decline of India's ability to mine and work metals. The British also began to close mines completely, especially after the Rebellion of 1857."

> "The ongoing fear of another uprising led to the Arms Act of 1878, which restricted not only access to minerals, but also to the subsequent production of firearms. British colonizers limited India's ability to mine and work metals."

**From [[Unit-5.4/Themes & Historical Reasoning]] (Topic 5.4):**
> "The proximate cause of Indian textile decline was the 5% equalizing tax; the underlying cause was colonialism and loss of political sovereignty."

> [!warning]
> India is one of the AP's favorite examples of how colonialism *actively prevented* development in colonized regions. This is NOT simply a story of India "falling behind" — it's a story of deliberate suppression. British policy actively dismantled Indian shipbuilding, textile production, and mining. Keep this distinction sharp in essays.

## Key Mechanisms of Colonial Economic Control

1. **Tariffs** — impose taxes on locally produced goods to make them uncompetitive
2. **Raw material extraction** — force colonies to export raw materials cheaply and import finished goods at higher prices
3. **Legal restrictions** — restrict weapons, mining, and manufacturing to prevent competition and local military capacity
4. **Monopoly trading companies** — British East India Company controlled Indian Ocean trade routes and Indian economic policy

## Colonialism vs. Sovereignty — The Core AP Argument

**From [[Unit-5.6/Practice & Review]] (Topic 5.6):**
> "Japan was able to industrialize more fully because it maintained political sovereignty throughout the reform period... Egypt, by contrast, faced ongoing Ottoman nominal control and ultimately fell under British imperial domination (1882), which redirected Egyptian resources to serve British interests rather than Egyptian development."

The contrast is the AP's central argument: **sovereignty enables industrialization; colonialism prevents it.** Japan's success and India's failure are two sides of the same coin.

## From the Vault

**From [[Unit-5.4/Global Spread of Industry]]:**
> Colonialism is described as "the mechanism of Indian deindustrialization" — the direct cause of India's declining share in global manufacturing, shipbuilding, and textiles.

> [!exam-tip]
> When writing about colonialism and economic development, make the **causation explicit**: colonial tariff policy → Indian textile producers uncompetitive → mills close → deindustrialization. Then contrast with Japan (no colonialism → sovereignty → state-directed industrialization → industrial power). This is the structural argument that earns complexity points.
