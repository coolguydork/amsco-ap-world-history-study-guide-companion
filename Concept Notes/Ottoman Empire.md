# Ottoman Empire

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

The **Ottoman Empire** was one of the most powerful and longest-lasting Islamic empires in world history, ruling from the early 14th century until its dismantlement after World War I (formally 1922). At its height it controlled Anatolia, the Balkans, the Arab Middle East, and North Africa. Its decline in the 19th century — earning it the nickname "Sick Man of Europe" — is a major AP World History theme connecting governance, imperialism, and industrialization.

---

## Key Periods for AP World History

### Rise and Peak (c. 1300–1600s)
- Founded in Anatolia; expanded rapidly using **Janissaries** (elite slave soldiers, [[Devshirme]] system) and gunpowder weapons
- Sultan **Suleiman the Magnificent** (r. 1520–1566) brought the empire to its territorial peak — reaching the gates of Vienna (1529) and controlling major trade routes
- A model **multi-religious empire**: administered via the **millet system** — separate legal courts for different religious communities (Christians, Jews, Muslims)
- Istanbul (formerly Constantinople, conquered 1453) was one of the world's great cultural and commercial centers

### Consolidation and Stagnation (1600s–1700s)
- Failed second siege of Vienna (1683) marks the beginning of territorial retreat
- Increasing competition from European maritime powers redirected trade routes away from Ottoman-controlled corridors
- Internal corruption and weakening of central authority; Janissaries became a conservative political force resisting reform

### Decline and Reform (1800s)
By the 19th century the empire was in serious trouble:
- **Corruption** drained resources and undermined effective governance
- **Ethnic nationalism** among subject peoples (Greeks, Serbs, Bulgarians, Armenians, Arabs) created constant unrest and separatist movements
- **Military weakness** relative to European industrial powers
- **Economic dependency** on European finance and investment grew

The phrase **"Sick Man of Europe"** became the dominant European characterization of Ottoman weakness. (AMSCO p. 317)

#### Reform Attempts
- **Sultan Mahmud II** (r. 1808–1839): Abolished the Janissaries (1826), built a new European-trained military, created a postal service, and established European-style ministries
- **Tanzimat Reforms (1839–1876)**: "Reorganization" — including a commercial code (1850), a penal code (1858), and the **Hatt-i Humayun** (1856) declaring equality in education and government regardless of religion or ethnicity; created secular primary schools
- **Sultan Abdulhamid** (r. 1876–1909): Initially supported reform but became increasingly authoritarian; drove the "Young Turks" into exile; whipped up anger against minorities. The **Hamidian Massacres (1894–1896)** killed between 100,000–250,000 Armenians — earning him the nickname "the Red Sultan"

> [!warning]
> The Tanzimat reforms had significant limitations. They benefited men far more than women — many reforms specifically excluded women from their scope. The 1839 Tanzimat reforms did not even mention women. (AMSCO p. 335)

### Dismantlement (Post-WWI)
- The Ottoman Empire entered WWI on the side of Germany and was defeated
- The empire was formally dismantled by the Allied powers; the **Republic of Turkey** emerged under **Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk)**, along with several new independent Arab states

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Janissaries | Elite Ottoman infantry composed of enslaved Christian boys ([[Devshirme]]); abolished 1826 |
| Millet system | Separate legal courts for different religious communities within the empire |
| Tanzimat | "Reorganization" — 19th-century Ottoman reform period (1839–1876) |
| Hatt-i Humayun | 1856 Ottoman Reform Edict; declared equality in education and justice for all men regardless of religion |
| Young Turks | Reform movement in the late 19th/early 20th century pushing for constitutional government |
| Sick Man of Europe | 19th-century nickname reflecting Ottoman political and economic decline |

## AP Exam Connections

> [!exam-tip]
> The Ottoman Empire appears across **multiple AP World History topics**:
> - **Units 1–2**: Islamic learning, trade networks, gunpowder empire
> - **Unit 3**: Ottoman power at its peak; connections to the [[Mongols]] and post-Mongol Islamic world
> - **Unit 5.6**: Ottoman decline and failed response to industrialization; Muhammad Ali of Egypt within the Ottoman system
> - **Unit 6**: Ottoman territories as targets of European imperialism; Armenian Genocide
> - **Unit 7**: WWI and Ottoman dismantlement

The empire is a strong example for **CCOT** arguments: from dominant 16th-century power to "Sick Man" by the 19th century, through a combination of internal corruption, ethnic nationalism, and failure to industrialize at the pace of European rivals.
