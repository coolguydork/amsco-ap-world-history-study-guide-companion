# Túpac Amaru II

**José Gabriel Condorcanqui (c. 1742–1781)**, known by his adopted name **Túpac Amaru II**, was a mestizo curaca (indigenous chief) in the Viceroyalty of Peru who led the largest and most violent indigenous rebellion against Spanish colonial rule in the Americas — the **Great Andean Rebellion (1780–1781)**. He claimed direct descent from the last Inca emperor, Túpac Amaru I (executed by the Spanish in 1572), and used this lineage to present himself as a legitimate ruler and restorer of Inca sovereignty.

## The Rebellion (1780–1781)

**Causes:** The Spanish Bourbon Reforms of the 18th century intensified colonial extraction: forced labor (the **mita** system for silver mines), increased taxes, the repartimiento de mercancías (forced purchase of Spanish goods), and the exclusion of criollos and indigenous elites from local governance.

**Outbreak:** In November 1780, Condorcanqui captured and publicly executed the local colonial corregidor (Spanish official) Antonio de Arriaga. He then issued proclamations in Quechua and Spanish calling for the abolition of the mita, repartimiento, and other colonial abuses.

**Scale:** The rebellion spread rapidly through the southern Andes, attracting tens of thousands of indigenous, mestizo, and even some criollo followers. At its peak, rebel forces threatened Cusco — the former Inca capital. The rebellion also inspired a simultaneous uprising in Upper Peru (Bolivia) led by **Tupaj Katari**, who besieged La Paz for months.

**Suppression:** Spanish authorities mobilized 17,000 troops. The rebellion was crushed in April 1781. Condorcanqui, his wife Micaela Bastidas (who had commanded logistics and written much of the rebel correspondence), and his family were captured. He was forced to watch family members executed before being drawn and quartered in the main plaza of Cusco.

## Legacy

Despite its defeat, the rebellion had lasting consequences:
- Spain abolished the repartimiento system and reformed the mita in the rebellion's aftermath, showing that violent resistance could extract concessions
- The rebellion terrified the criollo elite — ironically pushing them *away* from indigenous alliance and *toward* accommodation with Spain in the short term
- Túpac Amaru II became a powerful symbol of indigenous resistance; his name and image were invoked by Latin American independence movements in the 1810s–1820s and by 20th-century leftist movements (the Peruvian guerrilla organization MRTA took its name from him)

## AP Significance

The Great Andean Rebellion is the AP's **primary example of indigenous resistance to Bourbon colonial reform** in the Americas. It belongs to a pattern of late 18th-century resistance — alongside the Haitian Revolution and North American colonial protests — that reflected Enlightenment ideas, colonial economic grievances, and the limits of imperial authority. The rebellion's failure also illustrates the difficulty of maintaining indigenous-criollo coalitions against Spanish power.

> [!exam-tip]
> Compare Túpac Amaru II's rebellion to the Haitian Revolution: both were responses to colonial exploitation, both initially targeted the colonial system rather than European identity, and both ended with very different outcomes (failure in Peru vs. success in Haiti). The difference? Haiti's free Black population and enslaved majority overwhelmed French forces weakened by yellow fever. Peru's indigenous majority faced a united Spanish-criollo response.
