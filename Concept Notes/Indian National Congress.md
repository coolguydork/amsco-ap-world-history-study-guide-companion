# Indian National Congress

The Indian National Congress (INC, founded 1885) was India's primary political organization for independence from British rule. Initially a forum for educated Indian elites seeking modest reforms within the British system, it evolved — under Mohandas Gandhi's leadership — into a mass movement using civil disobedience, noncooperation, and nonviolent resistance to demand and ultimately achieve Indian independence in 1947.

## Founding and Early Phase (1885–1919)

The INC was founded in Bombay with British encouragement — the colonial administration initially saw it as a useful channel for Indian political expression that could prevent violent unrest. Early leaders (Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale) sought greater Indian representation in the colonial government, not independence. They used petitions, resolutions, and constitutional arguments.

The **Partition of Bengal (1905):** When the British divided Bengal along religious lines (dividing Hindus and Muslims), a mass boycott of British goods (**Swadeshi movement**) erupted. This first mass mobilization shifted the INC toward a more confrontational posture and proved that ordinary Indians could be brought into political action.

## Gandhi Era (1919–1942)

The **Amritsar Massacre (April 1919):** British troops fired on an unarmed crowd in Jallianwala Bagh, killing at least 379 people. The massacre radicalized Indian opinion and transformed Gandhi from a loyalist into a leader of mass resistance. Gandhi took control of the INC and restructured it as a genuinely national organization with branches across India.

**Key campaigns:**
- **Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922):** Boycott of British goods, courts, schools, and elections. Suspended after violence at Chauri Chaura.
- **Salt March (1930):** Gandhi led a 240-mile march to the sea to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax — a tactically brilliant act of civil disobedience that attracted global media attention and mass participation.
- **Quit India Movement (1942):** Launched during WWII; the British arrested the entire INC leadership, but underground resistance continued.

## Independence and Partition (1947)

Britain, exhausted by WWII and facing mass unrest, granted Indian independence in August 1947. The INC's failure to resolve tensions with the **Muslim League** (led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah) led to the **Partition of India** — the simultaneous creation of independent India and Pakistan along religious lines. Partition triggered one of the largest mass migrations in history (~15 million people displaced) and horrific communal violence killing hundreds of thousands.

Jawaharlal Nehru (INC) became India's first prime minister. Gandhi, who had opposed partition, was assassinated in January 1948 by a Hindu nationalist who blamed him for being too conciliatory to Muslims.

## AP Significance

The INC is the AP's **primary example of anti-colonial nationalism in South Asia** and the most thoroughly documented case of nonviolent resistance as a political strategy. It appears across multiple AMSCO topics: as a colonial-era response movement, as a post-WWI expression of self-determination, and as the vehicle for decolonization.

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the three phases of INC strategy: early petitionism (elite-driven, moderate), Gandhian mass nonviolence (1919–1947), and post-independence governance. AP essays often ask about the effectiveness of resistance strategies — the INC gives you a nuanced answer: nonviolence worked but required decades, a world war, and ultimately accepted the painful compromise of partition.
