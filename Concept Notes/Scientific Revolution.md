# Scientific Revolution

## What Was the Scientific Revolution?

The Scientific Revolution (c. 1543–c. 1700) was a fundamental shift in the way European thinkers understood the natural world. Instead of relying on ancient authorities (Aristotle, the Church) or scripture, thinkers like Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton insisted that truth about nature must come from **observation, experimentation, and mathematical reasoning**. This method — **empiricism** — became the intellectual foundation for the Enlightenment that followed.

## Key Figures

| Thinker | Contribution | Significance |
|---------|--------------|--------------|
| Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543) | *On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres* — heliocentric model | Displaced Earth (and humanity) from the center of the universe |
| Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) | Telescope observations; laws of motion | Confirmed Copernicus; challenged Church authority; tried by the Inquisition |
| Francis Bacon (1561–1626) | Inductive reasoning; empiricism | Knowledge must come from observed evidence, not inherited tradition |
| René Descartes (1596–1650) | Deductive reasoning; "I think, therefore I am" | Mathematical rationalism as a path to truth |
| Isaac Newton (1642–1727) | Laws of motion; universal gravitation; *Principia Mathematica* (1687) | Universe operates by discoverable natural laws — reason can unlock nature |

## Connection to the Enlightenment

The Scientific Revolution directly planted the seeds of the Enlightenment. If Newton could discover the laws governing planets through reason and observation, why couldn't reason also reveal the natural laws governing human society? Enlightenment thinkers applied the scientific method to politics, economics, and morality — producing the ideas about **natural rights**, **social contracts**, and **laissez-faire economics** that drove the revolutions of Period 3.

From the Unit-5.1 overview:
> "The Enlightenment was an 18th-century intellectual movement that prioritized reason, individualism, and natural law over tradition and religious authority. Growing out of the Scientific Revolution and Renaissance humanism, Enlightenment thinkers believed that applying reason to society would produce progress." — [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview]]
