# Transatlantic Slave Trade

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

## Quick Summary

The transatlantic slave trade was the forced transportation of approximately **10–15 million Africans** to the Americas between the early 1500s and the mid-19th century. Driven by demand for labor on sugar, tobacco, and later cotton plantations, it was the largest forced human migration in history and permanently shaped the demographics and cultures of three continents.

## Origins and Causes

From [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Overview]]:
> "Several factors converged to make Africa a target for slave raids by Europeans after 1450. Slavery in Latin America and the Caribbean began toward the end of the 16th century, when European conquistadores sought fortunes in gold, silver, and sugar. Land was plentiful, but labor to make the land profitable was scarce."

Key causal factors:
- Disease devastated indigenous American populations, eliminating them as a labor force
- Sugar cultivation required enormous, year-round intensive labor
- Enslaved Africans were unfamiliar with American territory and could not easily escape
- Some African states (Dahomey, Oyo, Kongo) participated in slave raiding for profit and weapons
- European commercial and legal systems made chattel slavery economically rational

## Scale and Destination

From [[Unit-4.3/Columbian Exchange Overview]]:
> "From the early 1500s to the mid-19th century, 10 to 15 million Africans were transported to the Americas. More than 90 percent went to the Caribbean and South America (primarily for sugar cultivation). Only about 6 percent went to British North America. Until the mid-1800s, more Africans than Europeans went to the Americas."

**Destination breakdown**:
- Portuguese colonies (Brazil): ~40% of the total
- British West Indian colonies (Jamaica, Barbados, etc.): ~20%
- Spanish colonies: ~15%
- Dutch West Indian colonies: ~5%
- British North America (later the United States): ~6%

## The Middle Passage

From [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Overview]]:
> "Captive Africans, swept away from their families, were taken to holding pens in West Africa known as barracoons, or 'slave castles.' From these holding pens, slave traders next crammed their captives into the dank cargo section of a ship, providing them little water, food, or even room for movement. The grueling journey across the Atlantic was known as the Middle Passage... During the journey to the Americas, which usually took about six weeks, up to half of a ship's captives might die. Over the hundreds of years of the Atlantic slave trade, 10 to 15 percent of all African captives perished before reaching the Americas."

## Effects on Africa

From [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview]]:
> "The Atlantic slave trade greatly weakened several West African kingdoms, such as Kongo. The loss of so many people slowed population growth. Trade competition led to violence among their societies, but also made African slave-raiding kingdoms economically dependent on goods from Europe. Such societies were slow to develop more complex economies."

**Gender impact**: More than two-thirds of those taken were males, creating severe gender imbalances. Polygyny increased; women assumed traditionally male roles.

**Paradox**: Africa's population actually grew during the 16th–17th centuries because American crops (maize, manioc, peanuts) introduced through the Columbian Exchange improved African diets enough to offset the slave trade's demographic drain.

## Effects on the Americas

From [[Unit-4.3/Columbian Exchange Overview]]:
> "African cultures were not completely lost once captives arrived in the Americas. In fact, during the African Diaspora (dispersion of Africans out of Africa), enslaved Africans retained some aspects of their cultures."

Cultural contributions of the African Diaspora:
- **Language**: Creole languages (Haitian Creole, Gullah)
- **Music**: Syncopated rhythms; contributed to gospel, blues, jazz, rock and roll, hip-hop, samba, reggae
- **Food**: Okra, rice, knowledge of their preparation; dishes like gumbo
- **Religion**: Syncretic traditions — Santería, Vodun, Candomblé

From [[Unit-4.7/Social Hierarchies Overview]]:
> "The encounter of Europeans, Africans, and indigenous Americans in the colonial Americas created entirely new social structures. Race became the primary organizing principle — a formal, legal system with profound consequences."

## The Indian Ocean Slave Trade (Parallel System)

From [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Overview]]:
> "By routes overland or in the Indian Ocean, slaves from eastern Africa were sold to buyers in northern Africa, the Middle East, and India... Slaves taken in the Indian Ocean trade suffered different fates from those taken across the Atlantic. Slaves in the Indian Ocean were more likely to work in seaports as laborers in the shipping industry and as household servants. Some worked as sailors or even soldiers... Slaves who ended up in Islamic communities had certain rights, such as the right to marry."
