# Laissez-Faire

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Laissez-faire** (French: "let it be") is the economic doctrine that governments should not interfere in market activity — no price controls, no production quotas, minimal regulation. It was the dominant economic policy philosophy of the industrial era, associated with [[Adam Smith]]'s *Wealth of Nations* (1776).

## AMSCO Reference (pp. 325–328)

> "Mercantilism, a system of economic protectionism, was replaced by a laissez-faire ('leave alone' in French) policy that promoted minimal governmental involvement in commerce and encouraged countries to reduce tariffs on trade."

> "As trade and production became increasingly global, the ideas of early economists such as Adam Smith were taken in new directions. While Smith wrote in an age of individual entrepreneurs and small businesses, people of the 19th century witnessed the rise of large-scale transnational businesses." (p. 333)

## The Core Argument

[[Adam Smith]] argued that the "invisible hand" of supply and demand guides markets to efficient outcomes more effectively than any government could. Self-interest, channeled through free competition, benefits society overall. Governments should therefore stay out of economic decisions about wages, prices, and production.

> [!warning]
> Laissez-faire did **not** mean governments disappeared from economic life. Governments still chartered [[corporation|corporations]], built railroads, imposed tariffs, and fought imperial wars on behalf of corporate interests. Laissez-faire meant governments stayed out of regulating *wages and working conditions* — not that they were absent altogether.

## AP Exam Significance

Laissez-faire appears on the AP exam as: (1) the replacement for mercantilism, (2) the ideology that shaped government non-intervention in industrial working conditions, and (3) the target of critics like [[Unit-5.8/Karl Marx & Socialism|Karl Marx]] and utilitarian reformers like John Stuart Mill.
