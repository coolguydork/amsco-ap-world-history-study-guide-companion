# Holodomor

The Holodomor ("death by hunger" in Ukrainian) was the devastating famine of 1932–1933 in Soviet Ukraine that killed an estimated 3.5–7.5 million people — roughly 13–15% of Ukraine's population. It resulted directly from Stalin's forced collectivization policies and the Soviet state's systematic confiscation of grain from Ukrainian villages. Ukraine, many nations, and a growing number of scholars classify the Holodomor as a genocide; Russia disputes this classification.

## Causes

Stalin's **First Five-Year Plan (1928–1932)** sought to rapidly industrialize the USSR by extracting agricultural surplus to fund industrial investment. This required **collectivization** — replacing private peasant farms with state-controlled collective farms (kolkhozy). Ukrainian peasants, many of whom were relatively prosperous small farmers ("kulaks"), resisted collectivization by slaughtering livestock rather than surrendering it and by hiding grain.

The Soviet response was brutal:
- Grain quotas were set at impossibly high levels and enforced even when villages had no surplus left
- The **Blacklist Decree (December 1932):** Villages that failed to meet quotas were cut off from receiving any food supplies from outside
- Internal passports were introduced to prevent Ukrainian peasants from traveling to cities or other regions to find food
- Communist Party activists searched homes and confiscated any hidden food, including seed grain needed for the next planting

## Scale and Evidence

By the winter of 1932–33, mass starvation spread across Ukraine, Kazakhstan, and parts of Russia — but Ukraine was disproportionately targeted. Mortality rates in some Ukrainian villages exceeded 25% in a single year. Soviet authorities concealed the famine from foreign observers; Western journalists who reported it (notably Gareth Jones) were dismissed or attacked by colleagues sympathetic to the USSR.

## Genocide Debate

**Those who classify it as genocide** (Ukraine, Canada, the EU, and ~30 other countries) argue that the policies — especially the internal passport system and the Blacklist Decree — were deliberately targeted at Ukrainians as an ethnic and national group to crush Ukrainian nationalism and resistance to Soviet rule.

**Those who dispute genocide** (Russia and some historians) argue it was a criminal policy of mass terror driven by class ideology (anti-kulak) rather than ethnic targeting, and that other Soviet regions also suffered famine.

## AP Significance

The Holodomor is the AP's clearest case study of **state-directed mass death as a tool of political control** during the interwar period. It appears in AMSCO as part of Stalin's broader program of collectivization and terror. For AP essays, it is powerful evidence of the extreme measures authoritarian states took to achieve industrialization and suppress resistance — alongside the Holocaust and Mao's Great Leap Forward as comparators.

> [!exam-tip]
> If an AP LEQ asks you to evaluate the methods by which 20th-century authoritarian states consolidated power or pursued economic development, the Holodomor is a top-tier example: it shows that famine could be a weapon, not just a failure.
