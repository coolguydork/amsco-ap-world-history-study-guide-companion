# Black Death

The Black Death (bubonic plague, caused by *Yersinia pestis*) was the most devastating pandemic in human history, killing an estimated 75–200 million people across Eurasia between the 1330s and 1350s. It is the AP exam's premier example of **disease as an unintended consequence of trade network connectivity**.

---

## Origins & Transmission

The plague originated in **Central/southern China** and spread outward along the very corridors that the Mongols had made safe for commerce — a grim irony of the Pax Mongolica. Mongol conquests helped transmit the **fleas** carrying the bacterium from southern China to Central Asia, and from there to Southeast Asia and Europe. The **caravanserai** system — rest stops that housed travelers and animals together — likely accelerated spread, since animals carried infected fleas.

The plague followed the **Silk Roads** and sea routes westward, arriving in Constantinople in 1346–1349 and in Western Europe by 1347.

> [!tip]
> The AP exam loves to test the Mongols as a *vector* of both trade AND disease. The same roads that carried silk and spices carried plague. Frame it as: **Pax Mongolica = connectivity with deadly side effects.**

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| 1332–1347 | ~25 million killed in China and Central Asia |
| 1346–1349 | Bubonic plague kills roughly half the population of Constantinople |
| 1347 | Plague arrives in Western Europe via Silk Roads and Mediterranean sea routes |
| 1347–1351 | Peak European epidemic; killed ~1/3 of Europe's population (75–200 million total across Eurasia) |
| Post-1351 | Additional outbreaks recur for decades |

*(AMSCO pp. 121–123)*

## Geographic Impact — Who Was Hit, Who Was Spared

The plague devastated **Eurasia and North Africa** but largely spared **South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa**, because those regions had fewer major trading ports connecting them to the Silk Roads corridor at the time.

| Region | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| Europe | ~1/3 of population dead (1347–1351) |
| China & Central Asia | ~25 million dead (1332–1347) |
| Constantinople | ~half the population dead (1346, 1349) |
| North Africa | Severe losses |
| South Asia | Largely spared |
| Sub-Saharan Africa | Largely spared |

## Consequences

### Economic
With fewer workers, agricultural production declined sharply. Labor scarcity made each surviving worker more valuable — peasants and serfs could **demand higher wages**. This shift helped **weaken feudalism** and laid groundwork for the economic transformations of the late medieval and early modern period. *(AMSCO p. 123)*

### Social & Religious
The Church failed to explain or stop the plague, severely damaging its **moral authority**. This crisis of faith contributed — over the long run — to the conditions that enabled the **Protestant Reformation** in the 1500s. *(AMSCO ref_3_3)*

The psychological trauma of mass death accelerated a general **turn inward** and questioning of medieval institutions that had previously seemed stable.

### Demographic
Population collapse across Eurasia took over a century to recover in many regions. The reduced population paradoxically improved living standards for survivors in the short term (more land per person, higher wages) even as it devastated economic output overall.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate cause and effect: the plague **accelerated** the decline of feudalism, but feudalism was already under stress from agricultural crises, peasant revolts, and the Little Ice Age. The Black Death was the shock that pushed a weakening system over the edge — not the sole cause of its collapse.

---

## AP Exam Angles

> [!exam-tip]
> The Black Death appears most often in these exam contexts:
> 1. **Cause & Effect** — Mongol connectivity as the cause; demographic collapse and feudal decline as effects.
> 2. **Comparison** — Black Death vs. **Columbian Exchange** (Topic 4.3): both involve disease spread through newly connected networks; both caused massive demographic collapse; both accelerated political and economic change.
> 3. **Continuity & Change** — What changed (labor relations, Church authority, population) vs. what continued (trade routes still operated, religious practice continued, agricultural systems rebuilt).
> 4. **Causation** — The plague is a strong supporting piece of evidence for any argument about *why* feudalism declined or *why* the Church's authority weakened before the Reformation.

## Key People & Sources

**Giovanni Boccaccio** (1313–1375) — Italian writer who lived through the Black Death in Florence. His *Decameron* was directly inspired by the plague and is the most famous literary primary source on the experience of the epidemic. AMSCO quotes him: *"Dead bodies filled every corner."*
