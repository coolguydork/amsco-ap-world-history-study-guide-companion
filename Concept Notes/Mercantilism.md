# Mercantilism

## Definition

The dominant European economic doctrine c. 1450–1750: national wealth equals gold and silver reserves; states should maximize exports, minimize imports, and use colonies as sources of raw materials and captive markets for finished goods. Government-directed rather than market-directed. *(AMSCO p. 232–233)*

> [!warning]
> Mercantilism is frequently confused with capitalism on the AP exam. Mercantilism = government-directed accumulation of bullion and trade surpluses through colonial extraction. Free-market capitalism (Adam Smith, laissez-faire) develops later, as a *reaction against* mercantilism — [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview\|Enlightenment Overview]] (pp. 275–284) and [[Unit-5.7/Economic Developments & Innovations Overview\|Economic Developments & Innovations Overview]] (pp. 325–331).

## Core Principles

- **Bullionism** — precious metals (gold and silver) are the measure of national wealth; accumulate as much as possible
- **Favorable balance of trade** — export more than you import; the difference flows in as bullion
- **Colonial system** — colonies exist to supply raw materials to the mother country and buy its finished goods; they are not permitted to develop competing industries
- **High tariffs and monopolies** — governments used tariffs to block competing imports and chartered monopolies to control colonial trade profits

## How It Worked in Practice

*(Topics 4.4, 4.5)*

**Spain** extracted silver from Potosí (Bolivia) and Zacatecas (Mexico) on a massive scale. A percentage of all silver production flowed directly to the crown, funding military expansion and European trade. Spain's tobacco monopoly alone generated roughly one-third of total government revenue. *(AMSCO p. 235)*

**Portugal** controlled Brazilian sugar production and Indian Ocean spice trade through a system of royal grants and monopoly rights. Portuguese trading posts in Africa and Asia were commercial-military chokepoints forcing merchants to pay for access.

**The Netherlands, England, France** used **joint-stock companies** (VOC, EIC) to pursue mercantilist goals through private capital rather than royal treasury — a more efficient model. *(See [[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|Joint-Stock Companies]])*

## Mercantilism and the Slave Trade

The plantation economy was mercantilist logic in action: colonies (Caribbean, Brazil, southern North America) produced raw commodities (sugar, tobacco, cotton) using coerced labor; finished goods flowed back from Europe. The Atlantic slave trade was the labor system that made plantation mercantilism work. *(See [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]])*

## The Price Revolution as Evidence

The massive influx of American silver into Europe caused the **Price Revolution** — sustained inflation across the 16th and early 17th centuries. This demonstrates a mercantilist paradox: accumulating bullion devalued it. More silver meant each unit bought less, eventually undermining the bullionist premise. *(See [[Concept Notes/Price Revolution|Price Revolution]])*

## Transition to Capitalism

The mercantilist era ended gradually as theorists — especially Adam Smith (*The Wealth of Nations*, 1776) — argued that free markets, not government direction, produce national wealth. Smith's **laissez-faire** capitalism became the ideological foundation for 19th-century industrial economies. The shift from mercantilism to capitalism is one of the AP's central economic transition moments. *([[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview\|Enlightenment Overview]] (pp. 275–284), [[Unit-5.7/Economic Developments & Innovations Overview\|Economic Developments & Innovations Overview]] (pp. 325–331))*

## AP Exam Threads

**Continuity and Change:** Mercantilism dominated c. 1450–1750 (Period 2); capitalism replaced it c. 1750–1900 (Period 3). Know the ideological difference and the transition moment (Adam Smith).

**Causation:** Mercantilism → colonial extraction → Atlantic slave trade → Price Revolution. These are all causally linked.

**Comparison:** European mercantilist colonial economy vs. Indian Ocean trade network (decentralized, multi-actor, not state-directed). See [[Concept Notes/Indian Ocean Trade Network\|Indian Ocean Trade Network]].
