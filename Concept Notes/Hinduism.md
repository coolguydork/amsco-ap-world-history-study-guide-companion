# Hinduism

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Hinduism** is among the world's oldest religions, emerging in the Indian subcontinent over millennia. Unlike Islam or Christianity, it has no single founder, no single sacred text, and no central authority — it is better understood as a family of related traditions sharing common concepts, texts, and practices. In AP World History, Hinduism matters primarily as the dominant religion of South Asia and a major influence on Southeast Asian civilization.

---

## Core Concepts

- **Dharma**: moral duty and cosmic order; each person has a dharma determined by their role in society
- **Karma**: the accumulated moral weight of one's actions across lifetimes; good actions → better rebirth
- **Samsara**: the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth (reincarnation); the goal of Hindu practice is liberation from samsara
- **Moksha**: liberation from the cycle of rebirth; union with the divine (Brahman)
- **Atman**: the eternal soul or self within each being; identical with Brahman at the deepest level (contrast with Buddhist *anatta* — no permanent self)

**Major Deities:**
- **Brahma** (creator), **Vishnu** (preserver/sustainer), **Shiva** (destroyer/transformer)
- Hinduism accommodates both monotheistic and polytheistic interpretations

**Sacred Texts:**
- **Vedas** — the oldest scriptures; basis of ritual and philosophy
- **Upanishads** — philosophical texts exploring the nature of reality and self
- **Bhagavad Gita** — beloved text on duty, devotion, and action embedded in the epic Mahabharata

## The Caste System and Social Structure

Hinduism is deeply intertwined with the **varna system** (caste):

| Varna | Group | Traditional Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Brahmin** | Priests and scholars | Religious ritual, education |
| **Kshatriya** | Warriors and rulers | Governance, military |
| **Vaishya** | Merchants and farmers | Trade, agriculture |
| **Shudra** | Laborers and servants | Manual labor |
| **Dalits** (outside varna) | "Untouchables" | Considered ritually impure |

> [!exam-tip]
> The caste system is one of the AP exam's key examples of **social hierarchy reinforced by religion**. Note that Hindu merchants (Vaishya) held a middle rank — higher than in Confucian China, lower than in Islam. This distinction matters for comparison questions.

## Hinduism and Southeast Asia

Hinduism spread to Southeast Asia via Indian Ocean trade networks, carried by merchants, Brahmin priests, and later by rulers who adopted Hindu frameworks for legitimizing their power. Key examples:

- **Angkor Wat** (Cambodia, 12th century) — originally a Hindu temple complex dedicated to Vishnu; later converted to Buddhism; one of the largest religious monuments in the world
- Hindu-influenced kingdoms in Java, Sumatra, and the Malay Peninsula adopted Sanskrit, Hindu court rituals, and the concept of the god-king (**devaraja**)
- Buddhism and Hinduism spread together in this region and often coexisted — another example of religious syncretism

> [!tip]
> The spread of Hinduism to Southeast Asia via trade (not conquest) is a powerful example of **cultural diffusion without military force**. Rulers adopted Hindu elements because they enhanced their legitimacy, not because they were compelled to.

## Bhakti Movement

The **Bhakti movement** (c. 7th–17th centuries) was a devotional reform movement within Hinduism that:
- Emphasized personal, emotional devotion to a deity (rather than ritual and priestly mediation)
- Was accessible to all castes, including women and lower castes
- Produced a rich tradition of devotional poetry in regional languages
- Paralleled Sufism in Islam in its emphasis on personal spiritual experience over formal orthodoxy

> [!exam-tip]
> AP exams often ask you to compare the **Bhakti movement** (Hinduism) with **[[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]]** (Islam). Both were devotional reform movements that made religion more accessible, spread across social boundaries, and blurred the line between the two faiths in South Asia.
