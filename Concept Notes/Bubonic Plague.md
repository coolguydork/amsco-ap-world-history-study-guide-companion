# Bubonic Plague

Bacterial disease (*Yersinia pestis*) spread by fleas on rodents; the Black Death pandemic (1347–1353) killed an estimated one-third of Europe's population and tens of millions across Afro-Eurasia. It traveled along Mongol trade routes — most notoriously via Genoese merchants at Caffa on the Black Sea — demonstrating how connectivity accelerates disease transmission. The plague's demographic devastation reshaped labor markets, weakened feudal structures in Europe, and contributed to the decline of Mongol power.

> [!exam-tip]
> Bubonic plague is a perfect example of trade networks as a double-edged sword: the same routes that spread goods and ideas also spread disease. Use it in any essay about the consequences of the Mongol Empire or Silk Roads to show unintended negative outcomes of economic integration.

> [!tip]
> The plague recurred in waves through the 14th–17th centuries. The 14th-century Black Death is the AP focus, but noting recurrences strengthens arguments about long-term demographic and economic change.
