# Buddhism

## Overview

Buddhism is a universalizing religion that originated in northern India (~5th century BCE) with the teachings of Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha). Its core doctrines — the **Four Noble Truths** and the **Eightfold Path** — teach that suffering arises from craving and desire, and can be overcome through right conduct, mindfulness, and meditation. By the period c. 1200–1450, Buddhism had spread across Asia via the Silk Roads and Indian Ocean trade networks, producing three distinct regional branches.

## The Three Branches

**Theravada Buddhism**
The oldest surviving school; emphasizes personal spiritual growth through meditation and self-discipline. "The way of the elders." Strongest in **Southeast Asia** — Sri Lanka, Thailand, Cambodia, Myanmar. (AMSCO p. 9)

**Mahayana Buddhism**
Emphasizes spiritual growth for all beings and compassion. The "Greater Vehicle." Strongest in **China, Korea, and Vietnam**. Includes the bodhisattva ideal — enlightened beings who postpone nirvana to help others. (AMSCO p. 9)

**Tibetan (Vajrayana) Buddhism**
Emphasizes esoteric ritual and chanting. Developed in **Tibet** and Mongolia. The Dalai Lama tradition comes from this branch.

> [!tip]
> Memory aid — match branches to regions: **T**heravada → **T**hailand/SE Asia; **M**ahayana → **M**ainland East Asia; **T**ibetan → **T**ibet.

## Spread and Adaptation

Buddhism spread via the **Silk Roads** into Central Asia and then China during the Han dynasty. The monk Xuanzang (7th century) helped popularize it in China. When Buddhism encountered existing traditions, it adapted rather than displaced them:

- In **China**, Buddhist monks introduced Buddhism by relating its concepts to Daoist principles (dharma ≈ dao). The fusion of Buddhist meditation with Daoist direct experience produced **Chan (Zen) Buddhism** — a syncretic tradition that thrived even after official Tang-era suppression. (AMSCO p. 9)
- In **Japan**, Buddhism blended with Shinto, the indigenous Japanese religion. Japanese rulers used Buddhist monasteries as centers of literacy and administration.
- In **Korea and Vietnam**, Buddhism spread as part of broader Chinese cultural influence and became integrated into court culture.
- In **Southeast Asia**, both Theravada Buddhism (from Sri Lanka) and Mahayana Buddhism (from India and China) shaped royal legitimacy. Rulers like the Khmer king Jayavarman VII converted to Buddhism; Angkor Wat was originally Hindu, then Buddhist. The **Srivijaya Empire** (7th–13th c.) on Sumatra became a major Buddhist center along Indian Ocean trade routes.

## Decline in South Asia

Buddhism declined in its birthplace (South Asia) during this period. Corruption among monasteries, raids by early Muslim conquerors, and the appeal of Islam (especially to Buddhists seeking an alternative to the caste hierarchy) all weakened Buddhism in India. The spread of Islam effectively made Buddhism a minority religion in the subcontinent. (AMSCO p. 26)

## Buddhism and Syncretism

Buddhism is one of the clearest examples of **syncretism** in AP World History — the blending of religious traditions. See [[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]] for the broader pattern.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP frequently asks: **Why did Buddhism spread so successfully?** Key answers: (1) It did not require abandoning existing beliefs; (2) it spread along trade routes with merchants; (3) rulers adopted it for legitimacy; (4) it offered an alternative to caste (in South Asia). Compare this to how Islam spread — similar mechanisms, different doctrine.
