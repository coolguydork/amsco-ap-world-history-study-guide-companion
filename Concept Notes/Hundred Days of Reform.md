# Hundred Days of Reform (1898)

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

The **Hundred Days of Reform** (June–September 1898) was a sweeping but short-lived attempt by **Emperor Guangxu** of China's Qing Dynasty to modernize China's political, economic, and educational institutions. It lasted approximately 103 days before being ended by a coup staged by **Empress Dowager [[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Cixi]]**.

---

## What Triggered It

The **Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)** ended in a humiliating Chinese defeat at the hands of Japan — a country that had only begun modernizing in 1868. The loss proved the inadequacy of the [[Concept Notes/Self-Strengthening Movement|Self-Strengthening Movement]]'s approach of modernizing technology without restructuring institutions.

In the aftermath, reform clubs proliferated. **Kang Youwei**, a civil servant and reform intellectual, organized one such club and secured a meeting with Emperor Guangxu. He convinced the emperor to attempt wholesale reform.

---

## What the Reforms Proposed

- Abolish the outdated **Confucian civil service examination** (which tested knowledge of classical texts, not modern governance)
- Eliminate corruption in government
- Establish Western-style industrial, commercial, and medical systems
- Create modern schools and a constitutional framework

---

## Why It Failed

**Empress Dowager Cixi** — the emperor's aunt and adopted mother, and the real power at court — opposed the reforms as too radical and threatening to dynastic stability. In a **coup d'état**, she:
- Imprisoned Emperor Guangxu (he remained under house arrest until his death in 1908)
- Immediately repealed the reform edicts
- Reasserted her control over the Qing court

Kang Youwei fled abroad and continued advocating for constitutional monarchy from exile.

---

## Significance

The failure of the Hundred Days is a key inflection point in Chinese history:
- It demonstrated that the Qing Dynasty could not reform itself from within
- It radicalized a generation of reformers who concluded that the dynasty itself had to go
- It contributed directly to the **Boxer Rebellion (1900)** and ultimately the **Chinese Revolution of 1911** ([[Unit-7.1/Chinese Revolution of 1911]])

---

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse the Hundred Days of Reform with the Hundred Days of Napoleon (1815). These are different events separated by nearly a century. The Chinese "hundred days" is named for its brief duration.
