# Islam

> [!summary] Source-built concept note
> This note collects relevant excerpts from existing study-vault notes and the structured AMSCO reference system. Use it as a quick concept hub, then return to the full topic notes for complete context.

**Islam** ("submission to God") is a monotheistic religion founded in the Arabian Peninsula in the 7th century CE through the prophet Muhammad (c. 570–632 CE). It spread rapidly across the Middle East, North Africa, Central Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia, becoming one of the largest and most geographically expansive religions in world history.

---

## Core Beliefs and Practices

**Five Pillars of Islam:**
1. **Shahada** — declaration of faith: "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is his messenger"
2. **Salat** — prayer five times daily facing Mecca
3. **Zakat** — charitable giving (2.5% of wealth annually)
4. **Sawm** — fasting during Ramadan
5. **Hajj** — pilgrimage to Mecca at least once in a lifetime if able

**Key concepts:**
- **Monotheism (tawhid)**: absolute unity of God (Allah); no Trinity, no intermediaries
- **Quran**: the holy scripture; God's direct word revealed to Muhammad in Arabic
- **Hadith**: recorded sayings and actions of Muhammad; guides Islamic law
- **[[Concept Notes/Shariah|Shariah]]**: Islamic law derived from the Quran and Hadith; governs both religious and civic life
- **Ummah**: the global community of Muslim believers, transcending ethnicity and nationality

---

## Spread and Dar al-Islam

Within a century of Muhammad's death, Muslim armies and missionaries had spread Islam across:
- The Arabian Peninsula, Persia, the Levant, Egypt, and North Africa
- Spain (al-Andalus, 711 CE) in the west
- Central Asia and the Indus Valley in the east

By Period 1 (c. 1200–c. 1450), the **Dar al-Islam** ("House of Islam") formed a vast, interconnected civilizational zone stretching from West Africa to Southeast Asia. Trade networks — especially the Indian Ocean and overland routes — carried Islam to sub-Saharan Africa, India, and island Southeast Asia.

---

## Islam and Governance

Islam deeply shaped political structures throughout its spread:

- The **caliphate** was the institution of political-religious leadership following Muhammad's death
- The [[Concept Notes/Abbasid Caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] (750–1258) represented Islam's golden age of scholarship and governance
- After the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258), political authority fragmented into multiple sultanates and regional kingdoms — but Islam remained the unifying cultural force
- The later **Ottoman**, **Safavid**, and **Mughal** empires (Unit 3) all governed through an Islamic framework, demonstrating Islam's continuity as a force of political legitimacy

---

## Islam and Trade

Islam and commerce were deeply intertwined from the beginning:
- Muhammad himself was a merchant; Islam explicitly honored trade as a legitimate profession
- Muslim merchants were high-status (contrast with Confucian China, where merchants ranked lowest)
- The hajj pilgrimage annually brought merchants, scholars, and rulers together in Mecca, creating a trans-regional network of exchange

> [!exam-tip]
> The contrast between **Islamic merchant status (high)** and **Confucian merchant status (low)** is a high-frequency AP comparison. Muhammad was a merchant; Islam encouraged commerce. Confucian ideology saw merchants as unproductive. This shapes how each civilization organized its economy and social hierarchy.

## Islam and Intellectual Culture

The [[Concept Notes/Abbasid Caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]'s **House of Wisdom** in Baghdad became the world's leading center of scholarship (8th–13th centuries). Muslim scholars:
- Preserved and built on Greek, Persian, and Indian knowledge
- Made advances in mathematics (algebra), astronomy, medicine, and philosophy
- Transmitted this knowledge to Europe via al-Andalus and Sicily — setting the stage for the European Renaissance

## Islam and Gender

Islamic law gave women more legal rights than contemporaries in Christian Europe:
- Women could own and inherit property
- Women had some rights of divorce
- However, new urban interpretations increasingly restricted women's public participation — a contrast with women's roles in rural or earlier Islamic contexts

> [!warning]
> Avoid overgeneralizing about women in Islam. The AP exam expects nuance: formally, Islamic law granted more rights than Christian law; in practice, urban social norms often restricted women's public roles. Regional variation was enormous.

---

## Divisions: Sunni and Shia

A major split emerged after Muhammad's death over who should lead the ummah:
- **Sunni**: believed leadership should go to the most qualified companion; became the majority
- **Shia**: believed leadership should stay within Muhammad's family (Ali and his descendants); concentrated in Persia/Iran

This division shaped the political geography of the Islamic world and remains significant today.
