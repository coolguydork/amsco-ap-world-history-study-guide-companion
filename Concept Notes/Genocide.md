# Genocide

The deliberate and systematic destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. The term was coined by Polish-Jewish lawyer **Raphael Lemkin** in 1944 and codified in the **UN Genocide Convention** (1948), which obligated signatory states to prevent and punish genocide.

## Legal Definition

Under the Genocide Convention, genocide requires **intent to destroy** a group "in whole or in part." This distinguishes genocide from ethnic cleansing (forced removal without necessary intent to destroy the group) and from general war crimes or mass atrocities without group-targeting intent.

## AP Case Studies

| Case | Perpetrator | Target | Approx. Deaths | Date |
|------|------------|--------|----------------|------|
| Armenian Genocide | Ottoman Empire (Young Turks) | Armenian Christians | 600,000–1.5 million | 1915–1916 |
| Holocaust | Nazi Germany (SS) | Jews + others | ~11 million | 1941–1945 |
| Cambodian Genocide | Khmer Rouge (Pol Pot) | "Impure" Cambodians | 1.6–1.8 million | 1975–1979 |
| Rwandan Genocide | Hutu-led government | Tutsis + moderate Hutus | 500,000–1 million | 1994 |
| Bosnian Genocide | Serbian forces (Milošević) | Bosnian Muslims | 100,000+ | 1992–1995 |
| Darfur | Sudan + Janjaweed | Non-Arab Muslims | 200,000+ | 2003–ongoing |

## Common Enabling Conditions (AP Causation)

All AP case studies share: (1) war or political crisis, (2) state organization, (3) ideological dehumanization of the target group, (4) failure of outside powers to intervene.

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the difference: **Genocide** = intent to destroy a group. **Ethnic cleansing** = forced removal of a group from a territory. They overlap (ethnic cleansing can escalate into genocide, as in Bosnia) but are legally distinct. The AP may ask you to distinguish them.
