# Pan-Africanism

Pan-Africanism is the political and cultural movement asserting the unity, solidarity, and shared destiny of all African peoples — both on the African continent and in the African diaspora (particularly in the Americas and the Caribbean). It emerged in the late 19th century as a response to slavery, colonialism, and racism, and became a major intellectual and political force driving African decolonization in the mid-20th century.

## Origins and Intellectual Foundations

Pan-Africanism developed simultaneously among Black intellectuals in the Caribbean, the United States, and Africa who argued that the common experience of slavery, colonialism, and racial oppression created a shared African identity that transcended national and ethnic boundaries.

**Key early figures:**
- **Edward Blyden (1832–1912):** West Indian–born scholar who promoted African cultural pride and the idea that Africans should return to and develop Africa
- **W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963):** American scholar and activist who organized the **Pan-African Congresses** (1919, 1921, 1923, 1927, 1945) — international meetings that brought together Black intellectuals and leaders to demand African rights and decolonization
- **Marcus Garvey (1887–1940):** Jamaican-born leader of the **Back-to-Africa movement**; his Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA) attracted millions of followers across the diaspora; promoted Black economic self-sufficiency and cultural pride

## The Négritude Movement

A parallel literary and intellectual movement among French-speaking Black writers — Aimé Césaire (Martinique), Léopold Sédar Senghor (Senegal) — that celebrated African cultural heritage and rejected the assimilationist idea that Africans should aspire to become French. Négritude argued that African cultural values (community, emotion, connection to nature) were equal to and distinct from European rationalism. Senghor would become Senegal's first president.

## Political Pan-Africanism and Decolonization

The **5th Pan-African Congress (Manchester, 1945)** — attended by Kwame Nkrumah, Jomo Kenyatta, and other future independence leaders — explicitly demanded African self-governance and independence for the first time, turning Pan-Africanism from a cultural movement into a political program.

**Kwame Nkrumah** (Ghana's first prime minister and president) was the central figure linking intellectual Pan-Africanism to practical decolonization. After leading Ghana to independence in 1957 (the first sub-Saharan African nation to gain independence), he promoted the **Organization of African Unity (OAU, 1963)** — a continental body of newly independent African states — as a step toward a "United States of Africa."

## AP Significance

Pan-Africanism is essential for understanding **African decolonization** (Topic 8.5) and the **cultural dimensions of anti-colonial resistance** (Topic 7.5). It shows that decolonization was not just a political and military process but also a cultural and psychological one — reclaiming African identity and dignity after centuries of degradation under slavery and colonialism.

> [!exam-tip]
> Pan-Africanism frequently appears in AP SAQ and LEQ prompts about how colonized peoples resisted European imperialism. Know the distinction between cultural Pan-Africanism (Négritude — reclaiming African identity) and political Pan-Africanism (demands for independence and continental unity). Both are exam-relevant.
