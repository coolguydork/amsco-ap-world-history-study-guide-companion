# Imperialism

Policy by which one country extends its power over other territories through military force, economic control, or political domination. The AP distinguishes several forms: **formal (direct) imperialism** — outright annexation and colonial rule (e.g., British India, French West Africa); **informal imperialism** — economic dominance without direct rule, through unequal treaties, [[Concept Notes/Spheres of Influence\|spheres of influence]], and debt dependency (e.g., China, Latin America); and **settler colonialism** — large-scale European migration displacing indigenous populations (e.g., Australia, Algeria, South Africa).

The 19th-century "New Imperialism" was driven by industrialization (need for raw materials and markets), Social Darwinism and the "civilizing mission" (ideological justifications), nationalism (competition among European powers), and technological advantages (steamships, telegraphs, quinine, repeating rifles). By 1914, European powers controlled roughly 85% of the world's land surface.

> [!exam-tip]
> Imperialism is one of the AP's highest-frequency essay topics — it appears in LEQs and DBQs about Unit 6 almost every year. The key analytical move is to distinguish *causes* (economic, ideological, technological, competitive nationalism) from *effects* (economic extraction, cultural disruption, demographic collapse, resistance movements) and *forms* (formal vs. informal vs. settler). Always bring in specific evidence: Berlin Conference (1884), Sepoy Mutiny, Scramble for Africa, Opium Wars.

> [!warning]
> Avoid treating imperialism as purely European or purely 19th century. The AP expects awareness that: (1) the U.S. practiced imperialism (Spanish-American War, Philippines); (2) Japan practiced imperialism in Asia; (3) earlier forms of imperialism existed in Units 3–4 (Spanish, Portuguese, Ottoman). "New Imperialism" is a specific acceleration, not the birth of the concept.
