# Meiji Restoration

The **Meiji Restoration** (1868) was Japan's state-directed transformation from a feudal society into an industrialized world power, carried out in response to the threat posed by Western imperial expansion. It is the defining example of **defensive modernization** in AP World History.

## Background: Isolation and the Perry Crisis

Japan had maintained a *sakoku* (closed-country) isolation policy since the **17th century**, limiting European contact to a single Dutch trading post at Nagasaki. This policy insulated Japan from Western influence but also prevented the adoption of industrial-era technology.

In **1853–1854**, U.S. Commodore Matthew Perry arrived with steam-powered warships and forced Japan to sign trade agreements. Perry's "black ships" demonstrated Japan's military vulnerability dramatically — and convinced Japanese elites that *sakoku* was no longer viable. The choice was stark: modernize or be colonized.

## The Restoration

The Meiji Restoration overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate and nominally restored power to the **Meiji Emperor (Mutsuhito, r. 1868–1912)**, though real authority rested with a coalition of reformist samurai-turned-statesmen. The name "Meiji" (meaning "enlightened rule") captured the program's ambition.

The **Charter Oath of 1868** set out the governing principles: deliberative assemblies, broad class participation in governance, freedom of occupation, rejection of feudal customs, and above all — "knowledge shall be sought throughout the world to strengthen the foundations of imperial rule."

## Key Policies and Changes

**Social restructuring**: Formal feudal class distinctions were abolished. Samurai lost their privileged status and stipends; peasants and merchants gained legal mobility. Universal military conscription replaced the samurai monopoly on military service.

**Deliberate technology transfer**: The Meiji government sent official delegations abroad — most notably the **Iwakura Mission (1871–1873)** — to study Western factories, legal codes, railroad systems, and educational institutions. Foreign experts ("*oyatoi gaikokujin*") were hired to train Japanese workers and officials. The goal was selective adaptation, not wholesale Westernization.

**State-directed industrialization**: The government built state-owned factories, railroads, and arsenals, then sold or transferred many to private industrial families (the *zaibatsu*). Tariff protections shielded infant industries. By 1900, Japan was the first Asian country to industrialize successfully.

**Military modernization**: A conscript army modeled on the Prussian system and a navy modeled on the British Royal Navy were built from scratch. Japan's new military capacity would be demonstrated in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) and the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05).

## Defensive Modernization Logic

The Meiji Restoration is the clearest example of **defensive modernization** — the strategic adoption of a rival's technology and institutions specifically to resist that rival's domination. Japan studied the West not to become Western but to become strong enough to maintain Japanese sovereignty and culture. Compare with the Ottoman Tanzimat reforms (same period, less successful) and contrast with India, which lost sovereignty and was prevented from industrializing precisely when it mattered most.
