## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 395–396*

---

**Questions 1–3** refer to the following passage:

> "I have warned you again and again not to dally in those villages, where there is nothing to do—but you continue to saunter, ignoring the fact that soldiers are running short of food. They are receiving their pay, but the money will not last forever. Then they will all depart, leaving us to pay with our lives, because you must have learned by this time that they came only for reasons of self-interest, and to get all they can out of us. They are already beginning to desert. . . . Thus we will lose all the people that I have gotten together for the descent on Cuzco. . . . I gave you plenty of warnings to march immediately on Cuzco, but you took them all lightly, giving the Spaniards time to prepare as they have done, placing cannon on Picchu Mountain, and devising other measures so dangerous that you are no longer in a position to attack them. . . . God must want me to suffer my sins. Your wife."
>
> — Micaela Bastidas, letter to Túpac Amaru II, 1781

---

**1.** What does the above letter indicate about the author's point of view?

A. She believed the Spanish were fighting only to earn money.
B. She did not understand the importance of planning for rebellion.
C. She supported the rebellion of indigenous people in Peru against the Spanish.
D. She was too anxious to be a valuable contributor to the rebellion.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Bastidas is clearly a committed participant — she is urgently coordinating military movements against the Spanish. The letter expresses frustration with tactical delay, not doubt about the cause itself. Choice A is a misreading: she mentions soldiers' self-interest, but she is talking about the rebel soldiers, not the Spanish. Choice B is wrong — she shows sophisticated strategic thinking. Choice D is wrong — her anxiety is evidence of serious command responsibility, not a weakness.

---

**2.** Which statement provides the most useful context in evaluating the author's argument about the Spanish?

A. She and her husband were able to escape the Spaniards and flee into the Andean wilderness.
B. She and her husband were brutally executed by the Spaniards.
C. She and her husband's forces defeated the Spanish conquerors.
D. She and her husband made peace with the Spaniards and were given a role in the colonial government.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Knowing that Micaela Bastidas was herself captured and executed by the Spanish (along with her husband Túpac Amaru II) confirms the stakes that shaped her urgency. Her letter was written knowing the consequences of failure were lethal. This makes her argument about the need for immediate action — rather than delay — historically grounded and contextually coherent. The other choices describe outcomes that did not happen.

---

**3.** How was the rebellion of Túpac Amaru II different from other Latin American rebellions?

A. Its leader identified as a person of European ancestry.
B. It was effective in changing governmental policy.
C. Its leader identified as an Incan.
D. It received support from Spain's rivals for influence.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Túpac Amaru II explicitly identified with his Inca heritage, taking the name of the last Inca ruler and framing his revolt as an indigenous uprising — unusual among Latin American resistance leaders of the period, many of whom were criollos (Spanish-born in America) drawing on Enlightenment ideas rather than indigenous identity. Choice A is wrong — he was of Inca descent, not European. Choice B is wrong — the revolt was crushed and produced no policy change. Choice D is wrong — no foreign power supported his revolt.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO p. 396*

---

**SAQ 1.** Use the passage below to answer all parts.

> "It is well known to all that in this age the people of Hindustan [northern India], both Hindus and Muslims, are being ruined under the tyranny and oppression of the infidel and the treacherous English. It is therefore the bounden duty of all the wealthy people of India, especially of those who have any sort of connection with any of the Muslim royal families and are considered the pastors and masters of their people, to stake their lives and property for the well-being of the public. . . . I, who am the grandson of Bahadur Shah, have come here to extirpate the infidels residing in the eastern part of the country, and to liberate and protect the poor helpless people now groaning under their iron rule. . . ."
>
> — Firoz Shah, Proclamation in the *Delhi Gazette*, 1857

**(A)** Identify ONE reason for the proclamation by Firoz Shah.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization/Identification
> Firoz Shah issued the proclamation because of widespread Indian resentment of British East India Company rule, which many Indians — both Hindus and Muslims — experienced as oppressive and threatening to their religious and cultural life. The immediate trigger was the Indian Rebellion of 1857, in which sepoys (Indian soldiers) revolted partly in response to rifle cartridges greased with cow and pig fat. Firoz Shah, as a member of the Mughal royal family, framed his call to action in religious terms — presenting the British as "infidels" and Indian resistance as a religious duty.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which the ideas in the proclamation affected political structures in India in 1857.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Firoz Shah's proclamation helped mobilize both Hindu and Muslim populations against British rule by emphasizing shared oppression across religious communities. During the rebellion itself, this cross-religious framing contributed to coordinated uprisings across northern Indian cities. However, the proclamation also reveals the political weakness of the rebellion: Firoz Shah appealed to loyalty to the Mughal dynasty at a moment when that dynasty's authority was fading. Britain responded to the rebellion by abolishing the East India Company and establishing direct Crown rule through the British Raj (1858), fundamentally restructuring the political relationship between Britain and India.

**(C)** Explain ONE political development in India that occurred after 1857 as a result of the rebellion.

> [!tip] Model Response — Consequence/Causation
> One major political development following the rebellion was the founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885. Britain's response to the rebellion — establishing direct Crown rule and expanding British-style education — inadvertently created a class of British-educated Indian professionals who used their education to organize politically. The INC began as a forum for airing grievances but quickly became a vehicle for demands for self-rule. The rebellion thus set in motion a sequence that, over the next 90 years, culminated in Indian independence (1947).

**SAQ 2.** Answer all parts of the question that follows.

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which Enlightenment ideals influenced the series of revolts throughout India in 1857.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Enlightenment ideals of natural rights and popular sovereignty influenced the Indian Rebellion of 1857 by providing intellectual frameworks that delegitimized colonial rule. Indian resistance leaders, including those educated in British institutions, drew on the idea that no people should be subjected to rule that violated their fundamental rights. Firoz Shah's proclamation reflects this by framing British rule as tyranny that violated the people's well-being — a framing parallel to Enlightenment arguments against illegitimate governance.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which nationalist movements in Africa differed from nationalist movements in France in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> African nationalist movements in this period differed from French nationalism in that African nationalism was primarily a response to *external* colonial domination, while French nationalism was an internal restructuring of political identity. French nationalism sought to redefine the relationship between the French people and their own government (as seen in the French Revolution); African nationalist movements — such as Pan-Africanism or the Mahdist movement in Sudan — sought to expel or resist *foreign* political and military control. African nationalism also often fused with religious identity (Islam in the Sokoto Caliphate and Mahdist Sudan) in ways that French secular nationalism explicitly rejected.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which nationalist movements in Southeast Asia were similar to nationalist movements in South American states in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Both Southeast Asian and South American nationalist movements drew on European Enlightenment ideas to justify resistance to colonial rule. In South America, leaders like Simón Bolívar used natural rights philosophy to argue for independence from Spain. In Southeast Asia, José Rizal and Philippine nationalists similarly drew on ideas of sovereignty and natural rights — absorbed through European education — to challenge Spanish and then American colonial authority. In both regions, an educated colonial elite used the colonizers' own intellectual frameworks to delegitimize empire.

## Quick Review

**The one sentence per movement:**
- Túpac Amaru II → last Inca-identified revolt in Peru; crushed but symbolic
- Indian Rebellion 1857 → sepoys + religious grievance → Raj replaces Company
- Philippine Revolution → Rizal's execution → revolt; US takeover → Philippine-American War
- Sokoto Caliphate → dan Fodio's jihad → largest 19th-c African empire
- Mahdist Sudan → Ahmad's Mahdi claim → Khartoum (1885) → Omdurman (1898)
- Anglo-Zulu War → Isandlwana (Zulu win) → British win; Zululand annexed
- Battle of Adwa 1896 → Menelik II defeats Italy → only African country to beat colonizer
- Ghost Dance → spiritual resistance on Plains → Wounded Knee (1890)
- Cherokee + Indian Removal Act → assimilation failed; Trail of Tears
- Indian National Congress 1885 → political organizing → India independent 1947

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks: "Explain ONE cause of anti-colonial resistance in this period" or "Compare the outcomes of TWO resistance movements." Always anchor causation in *specific* colonial actions (a particular policy, a particular war), not just vague "oppression."
