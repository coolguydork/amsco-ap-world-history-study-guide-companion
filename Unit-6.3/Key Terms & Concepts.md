# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 6.3 — Indigenous Responses to State Expansion | AMSCO pp. 388–397**

---

## Rebel Leaders

**[[Concept Notes/Túpac Amaru II\|Túpac Amaru II]]**
Peruvian Inca-descended cacique (hereditary chief) who led the last major Indian revolt against Spanish colonial rule (1780–1781). Educated by Jesuits but deeply identified with his Inca heritage, he arrested and executed a colonial administrator before the revolt spread through southern Peru, Bolivia, and Argentina. He and his family were captured and publicly executed in Cuzco. A defining symbol of indigenous resistance in Latin America.

**José Rizal**
Filipino novelist and reformer who founded the Liga Filipina (1892) as a peaceful reform movement under Spanish rule. The Spanish executed him in 1896 despite the Liga's loyalty, shocking Filipinos and directly igniting the Philippine Revolution. Rizal is the Philippine national hero; his execution illustrates how colonial repression can radicalize a population.

**Usman dan Fodio**
Fulani Muslim scholar who launched a jihad (1804–1808) to purify Islamic practice among the Hausa states of West Africa. He founded the Sokoto Caliphate, the largest African empire of the 19th century. His movement shows how religious reform and political resistance could merge into state-building.

**Samory Touré**
Mande chieftain who built a powerful kingdom in Guinea and led sustained armed resistance against French colonization of West Africa (1883–1898). He fought the French twice before being captured and exiled in 1898. One of the most effective military resisters of the colonial era in Africa.

**Muhammad Ahmad**
Sudanese Islamic cleric who declared himself the Mahdi ("guided one") in 1881 and united Sudan against Egyptian and British rule. His forces captured Khartoum in 1885, killing British General Gordon — a major shock to British imperial prestige. He died shortly after; Britain crushed the Mahdist state at the Battle of Omdurman (1898).

**Yaa Asantewaa**
Queen mother of the Ejisu state of the Asante Empire who led the War of the Golden Stool (1900–1901) against British demands for the sacred Golden Stool, a symbol of Asante sovereignty and national unity. The last major African resistance war led by a woman; the British exiled her after their victory.

## States and Regions

**Balkan Peninsula**
Region of southeastern Europe under Ottoman control where ethnic nationalist movements emerged in the 19th century, inspired by the French Revolution. Greece (1829) and Serbia, Romania, Bulgaria, and Montenegro all eventually won independence. The ethnic tensions and power struggles left in the wake of Ottoman retreat contributed directly to the outbreak of World War I.

**Vietnam**
Southeast Asian country colonized by France in the 19th century. Vietnamese leaders including Emperor Ham Nghi and guerrilla commander Phan Dinh Phung resisted French control from the beginning, establishing a tradition of anti-colonial resistance that extended well into the 20th century.

**Philippines**
Southeast Asian archipelago colonized by Spain in the 16th century. An educated Filipino nationalist class emerged in the 19th century; their resistance — the Philippine Revolution (1896) — outlasted Spanish rule only to face U.S. colonization after the Spanish-American War (1898), triggering the Philippine-American War (1899–1902).

**Sokoto Caliphate**
Large West African Islamic theocracy founded by Usman dan Fodio (1804), centered at Sokoto in present-day Nigeria. The largest African empire of the 19th century; maintained the slave trade while Britain worked to abolish it; subdued by Britain in 1903 and incorporated into the colony of Nigeria.

**Sudan**
Northeast African territory where the Mahdist revolt (1881–1898) under Muhammad Ahmad challenged Egyptian and British rule. The Mahdist army captured Khartoum (1885); Britain retook control at the Battle of Omdurman (1898). A key example of religiously motivated anti-colonial resistance.

**Asante Empire**
Powerful West African kingdom in present-day Ghana that resisted British colonization through five wars (1823–1900). The final conflict — the Yaa Asantewaa War (1900) over the Golden Stool — ended in British annexation of Asante as part of the Gold Coast colony.

## Wars, Rebellions, and Movements

**Ghost Dance**
Spiritual resistance movement that swept across Native American nations (c. 1889–1890), prophesying the return of the dead and the removal of white settlers. Sioux warriors wore "ghost shirts" for protection. Ended with the Wounded Knee Massacre (1890), marking the end of the Indian Wars. A key example of cultural/spiritual resistance.

**Indian Rebellion of 1857**
Also called the Sepoy Mutiny. Widespread uprising against British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] rule triggered by rifle cartridges greased with cow and pig fat (offensive to Hindu and Muslim sepoys). The rebellion spread across northern India before Britain suppressed it violently. Led Britain to abolish the Company, exile the Mughal emperor, and establish direct Crown rule as the British Raj.

**Philippine Revolution**
1896 uprising against Spanish colonial rule in the Philippines, sparked by the execution of José Rizal. Led by Emilio Aguinaldo, the revolution appeared to succeed when the U.S. defeated Spain in 1898 — but the Treaty of Paris transferred control to the U.S., triggering the Philippine-American War.

**Philippine-American War**
Conflict (1899–1902) between Filipino nationalists and the United States after the U.S. acquired the Philippines through the Treaty of Paris. Filipinos who had fought Spain for independence now fought America. An estimated 20,000 Filipino troops and over 200,000 civilians died. Illustrates the contradiction between U.S. anti-colonial rhetoric and its own imperial ambitions.

**Maori Wars**
Series of conflicts in New Zealand (1840s–1872) between the Maori and British settlers over land rights. Britain repeatedly violated the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), which had promised Maori land protection. By 1900 the Maori had lost most of their land.

**Xhosa Cattle Killing**
1856–1857 millenarian movement in which the Xhosa people of South Africa destroyed their cattle and crops, believing ancestral spirits would expel the British in return. The result was mass famine and tens of thousands of deaths; the disaster weakened Xhosa resistance and accelerated British land seizures.

**Anglo-Zulu War**
Conflict (1879) between the British Empire and the Zulu Kingdom in South Africa. The Zulu inflicted a stunning early defeat on British forces at Isandlwana — one of Britain's worst colonial defeats — before being ultimately defeated and annexed. Demonstrates organized African capacity for armed resistance.

## Treaties

**Treaty of Paris (1898)**
Treaty ending the Spanish-American War; transferred control of the Philippines, Guam, and Puerto Rico from Spain to the United States. Filipino nationalists who had fought for independence found themselves under new colonial rule, directly triggering the Philippine-American War.

**Treaty of Waitangi (1840)**
Treaty between the British Crown and Maori chiefs of New Zealand guaranteeing protection of Maori land rights. Repeatedly violated as European settlers expanded; the gap between treaty promises and colonial reality is a textbook example of settler colonialism's pattern of indigenous dispossession.

## Colonial Policies

**[[Concept Notes/Proclamation of 1763\|Proclamation of 1763]]**
British royal decree after the French and Indian War reserving all land west of the Appalachian Mountains for Native Americans — the first European governmental recognition of indigenous territorial rights. Widely ignored by colonists and abandoned after American independence; settlers pushed westward regardless.

**Indian Removal Act**
U.S. law (1830) authorizing the forced relocation of Native American peoples from the southeastern states to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), leading to the Trail of Tears. Demonstrates that even deep cultural accommodation — as the Cherokee had practiced — offered no protection against settler colonialism.

## Organizations

**[[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]]**
Political organization founded in 1885 by British-educated Indians to seek greater autonomy from colonial rule. Began as a forum for airing grievances to the colonial government; quickly evolved into a call for self-rule. Became the main vehicle of the Indian independence movement, which achieved independence in 1947.

## Peoples

**Cherokee nation**
Native American people who assimilated deeply into white settler culture after 1800 — developing a syllabic alphabet, the *Cherokee Phoenix* newspaper, and a U.S.-style constitution — yet were still forcibly relocated west by the Indian Removal Act (1830). A key example proving that assimilation offered no protection against settler colonialism.

**Aboriginal**
Indigenous peoples of Australia, with over 50,000 years of continuous culture and up to 1 million people in 500 clans at the time of British settlement (1788). Denied land ownership and legal protection under colonial law; thousands were killed defending their territories. Their dispossession is a foundational example of settler colonialism.

**Xhosa**
South African Bantu-speaking people who resisted British colonization from 1811 to 1858 in a series of frontier wars. The Xhosa Cattle Killing Movement (1856–57) was a spiritual-millenarian response to colonial pressure that caused mass starvation and accelerated British land seizures.

**Zulu**
South African people whose Zulu Kingdom became a powerful, centralized state in the 19th century. They inflicted a major defeat on Britain at Isandlwana (1879) during the Anglo-Zulu War before being ultimately defeated and annexed into the British colony of South Africa. Exemplify organized armed African resistance to imperialism.

## Ideas

**Mahdi**
Islamic messianic title meaning "guided one" — the figure who will restore justice before the Day of Judgment. Claimed by Muhammad Ahmad in Sudan (1881), who used it to mobilize both religious conviction and political resistance against Egyptian and British rule. Shows how religious identity could unite and sustain anti-colonial movements.

**[[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]]**
Political and cultural ideology asserting the shared identity and solidarity of African and diaspora peoples across national boundaries. Emerged among Western-educated Africans after World War I. Challenged European colonial rule and provided the ideological foundation for 20th-century African independence movements.

## British in India

**sepoys**
Indian soldiers employed by the British East India Company who made up the majority of Company forces by the mid-19th century. Their revolt in 1857 — triggered by rifle cartridges greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to both Hindu and Muslim soldiers — became the Indian Rebellion of 1857 and ended Company rule in India.

**Raj**
The British Crown's direct colonial government of India from 1858 to 1947, established after the Indian Rebellion ended the East India Company's authority. The Raj educated Indians at British universities, inadvertently creating the nationalist leadership class that founded the Indian National Congress (1885) and ultimately achieved independence.
