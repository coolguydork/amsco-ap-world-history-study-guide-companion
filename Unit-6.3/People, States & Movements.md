# People, States & Movements

**Topic 6.3 — Indigenous Responses to State Expansion | AMSCO pp. 388–397**

## Rebel Leaders

**[[Concept Notes/Túpac Amaru II\|Túpac Amaru II]] (c. 1742–1781)**
José Gabriel Condorcanqui was a cacique (hereditary chief) in southern Peru, descended from the last Inca ruler. Educated by Jesuits but committed to his Inca identity, he arrested and executed a cruel colonial administrator in 1780, sparking the last general Indian revolt against Spain. The revolt spread through Peru, Bolivia, and Argentina and drew some initial criollo support before being suppressed. Túpac Amaru II was taken to Cuzco and forced to watch the execution of his wife and sons before being tortured and killed himself. He remains a foundational symbol of indigenous resistance in Latin America.

**José Rizal (1861–1896)**
Filipino intellectual, novelist, and reformer who was educated in Europe and returned to advocate for Philippine reform under Spanish rule. He founded the Liga Filipina (Philippine League, 1892) as a peaceful civic organization, but Spanish authorities arrested him immediately. His execution in 1896 shocked Filipinos and is considered the immediate trigger of the Philippine Revolution. Rizal's case demonstrates how colonial repression of moderate reformers often radicalizes resistant populations.

**Usman dan Fodio (1754–1817)**
Fulani Muslim scholar and theologian in West Africa who launched a jihad (1804–1808) against the Hausa rulers of northern Nigeria, whom he accused of mixing Islamic and traditional practices. His campaign unified dozens of Hausa city-states under a new Islamic polity — the Sokoto Caliphate — centered at Sokoto. The Caliphate became the largest African empire of the 19th century and a powerful center of Islamic scholarship. Dan Fodio's movement shows how religious reform could serve as the vehicle for political restructuring and resistance.

**Samory Touré (c. 1830–1900)**
Mande military leader from Guinea who built a substantial empire in West Africa through conquest and trade from the 1860s onward. When the French began advancing into West Africa in the early 1880s, Touré resisted militarily for nearly two decades — twice going to war with France (1883 and 1891) and repeatedly relocating his kingdom to avoid encirclement. The French finally captured him in 1898 and exiled him to Gabon, where he died. Touré represents one of Africa's most sustained and tactically sophisticated resistance efforts.

**Muhammad Ahmad (1844–1885)**
Sudanese Islamic cleric who declared himself the Mahdi ("guided one" of Islamic prophecy) in 1881, transforming a political grievance against Egyptian and British rule into a religious movement. His Mahdist army swept across Sudan, capturing the capital Khartoum in 1885 and killing British General Charles Gordon — a moment that scandalized Victorian Britain. Ahmad died of typhus weeks after Khartoum fell; infighting weakened his movement, and Britain retook Sudan at the Battle of Omdurman (1898). His movement illustrates the power of religious identity to mobilize resistance.

**Yaa Asantewaa (c. 1840–1921)**
Queen mother of Ejisu in the Asante Empire of present-day Ghana. When the British governor of the Gold Coast demanded the Golden Stool — the symbol of Asante sovereignty — in 1900, male Asante leaders hesitated. Yaa Asantewaa shamed them into action and personally led the resistance, commanding troops in the field. The Yaa Asantewaa War (1900–1901) was the last African resistance war led by a woman. It ended in British victory; she was exiled to the Seychelles. The Golden Stool was never surrendered.

**Emperor Menelik II of Ethiopia (r. 1889–1913)**
The only African ruler to defeat a European colonial army during the Scramble for Africa. At the Battle of Adwa (1896), Ethiopian forces decisively defeated an Italian army that had attempted to colonize Ethiopia. Adwa made Ethiopia a worldwide symbol of African resistance and sovereignty; it remained independent throughout the colonial era.

> [!exam-tip]
> Ethiopia under Menelik II is the exam's go-to example of successful African resistance. Always contrast it with cases of failed resistance (Zulu, Asante, Sudan) when comparing outcomes.

## States and Empires

**Sokoto Caliphate (1804–1903)**
West African Islamic theocracy founded by Usman dan Fodio after his jihadist campaign against the Hausa states. Centered at Sokoto in present-day Nigeria, it administered dozens of emirates through Islamic law and scholarship. The Caliphate was the largest African empire of the 19th century. It maintained the slave trade when Britain was working to abolish it — a direct source of Anglo-Caliphate conflict. The British conquered it in 1903 and made it part of the Colony of Nigeria.

**Zulu Kingdom**
Powerful South African state consolidated by Shaka Zulu in the early 19th century; by the 1870s it was the most formidable military power in southern Africa. At the Battle of Isandlwana (1879), the Zulu inflicted one of the worst defeats Britain ever suffered in a colonial war, killing over 1,300 soldiers. Britain regrouped and defeated the Zulu later that year; Zululand was subsequently annexed. The Zulu kingdom's early success is a key example of African military capability against European forces.

**Asante Empire**
West African kingdom in present-day Ghana organized around the Golden Stool — a sacred throne said to hold the spirit of the Asante people. The Asante fought Britain five times (1823–1900). The first four wars ended inconclusively or in Asante victories. Only in the Yaa Asantewaa War (1900) did Britain finally annex Asante as part of the Gold Coast colony.

**Mahdist Sudan (1885–1898)**
Theocratic Islamic state established in Sudan after the Mahdist forces of Muhammad Ahmad captured Khartoum (1885). After Ahmad's death, his successor Khalifa Abdallahi held power. Britain, seeking control of the Nile Valley, invaded under General Kitchener and defeated the Mahdists at the Battle of Omdurman (September 1898), effectively ending the Mahdist state.

## Resistance Movements

**[[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]] (est. 1885)**
Founded by British-educated Indian professionals, the INC began as a forum for educated Indians to express political grievances to colonial authorities. It quickly evolved into an organization demanding self-rule. Moderate leaders initially sought constitutional reform; by the early 20th century, figures like Bal Gangadhar Tilak were demanding immediate independence. The INC became the organizational engine of the Indian independence movement that achieved independence in 1947.

**Ghost Dance Movement**
Originating among Northern Paiute prophet-dreamers in the 1860s and spreading rapidly in 1889–1890, the Ghost Dance movement prophesied that ancestral spirits would soon return, restore Native American lands, and expel white settlers. Rituals of dancing and song were meant to hasten this transformation. When the U.S. Army feared the movement was preparing an armed uprising, they moved to suppress it; Sioux leader Sitting Bull was killed and the Wounded Knee Massacre (1890) crushed the movement.

**[[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]]**
Political ideology emerging among Western-educated Africans and the African diaspora after World War I, asserting that all African peoples shared a common identity and common cause against colonial rule. Early Pan-African intellectuals like W.E.B. Du Bois and Marcus Garvey gave it global reach. Pan-Africanism became the ideological foundation of the independence movements that swept Africa after World War II.

**Liga Filipina (Philippine League)**
Reform organization founded by José Rizal in 1892 to seek political representation and equal rights for Filipinos under Spanish rule. Deliberately non-revolutionary, it was nonetheless suppressed immediately; Rizal's subsequent execution transformed moderate Filipino political opinion into revolutionary action, contributing to the outbreak of the Philippine Revolution (1896).

## Key Comparison: Patterns of Resistance Leadership

| Leader | Region | Method | Outcome |
|--------|--------|--------|---------|
| Túpac Amaru II | Andes | Armed revolt | Executed; revolt crushed |
| Usman dan Fodio | West Africa | Religious jihad → state | Succeeded: Sokoto Caliphate |
| Samory Touré | West Africa | Armed resistance | Captured, exiled 1898 |
| Muhammad Ahmad | Sudan | Religious-military | State held until 1898 |
| Yaa Asantewaa | West Africa | Armed revolt | Defeated, exiled |
| Menelik II | East Africa | Military defeat of colonizer | Succeeded: Ethiopia independent |
| Indian National Congress | South Asia | Political organizing | Succeeded: independence 1947 |
| José Rizal | SE Asia | Reform movement → revolution | Executed; revolution sparked |
