## Builds On

### [[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview\|State Expansion Overview]] 

Topic 6.3 is the direct response to 6.2. Every resistance movement in this topic was triggered by the specific mechanisms of state expansion covered in 6.2: European industrial-age military superiority, the Scramble for Africa, the British consolidation of India, the Spanish colonization of the Philippines. Without understanding *what* was being resisted and *why* it was so difficult to resist, 6.3's case studies float without context. For the AP exam, always frame resistance explanations with reference to the specific imperial advance that provoked them.

### [[Unit-5.2/Nationalism & Revolutions Overview\|Nationalism & Revolutions Overview]] 

The revolutionary wave of the late 18th and early 19th centuries — American, French, Haitian, and Latin American independence movements — created the ideological vocabulary that indigenous resistance leaders later deployed. [[Concept Notes/Túpac Amaru II\|Túpac Amaru II]] operated partly in this tradition. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 is compared directly to the Haitian Revolution in the AMSCO text: both challenge colonial power, but the Haitian Revolution succeeded (producing an independent state) while the Indian Rebellion was crushed (producing tighter colonial control). That contrast is a powerful comparison question on the AP exam.

## Leads To

### **Unresolved Tensions After World War I** [[Unit-7.5/Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview|Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview]] ← not yet built

World War I was a watershed for global nationalism. Colonial peoples who had served in the war — Indian, West African, Vietnamese, Filipino soldiers — returned with heightened expectations of self-determination that were largely denied at Versailles. The movements built in Topic 6.3 (INC, Pan-Africanism) intensified dramatically after 1918. The unfulfilled promises of Versailles became a direct cause of the nationalist explosions of the mid-20th century.

### **Decolonization After 1900** [[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview|Decolonization after 1900 Overview]] ← not yet built

The decolonization wave of the 1940s–1960s did not appear from nowhere. Its organizational infrastructure — the Indian National Congress, Pan-African networks, Philippine nationalist traditions — was built during the period covered in Topic 6.3. Every success of decolonization has roots in the resistance strategies developed during imperial expansion. Students who understand 6.3 have the causal groundwork for 8.5.

## AP Comparison Angles

### Comparing the Indian Rebellion (1857) and the Haitian Revolution

| Dimension | [[Unit-5.2/Nationalism & Revolutions Overview\|Haitian Revolution]] | Indian Rebellion of 1857 |
|-----------|-------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Trigger | Plantation slavery; French Revolution's ideals | Greased cartridges; religious grievance |
| Leaders | Formerly enslaved Toussaint Louverture | Sepoys + Indian princes (Firoz Shah, etc.) |
| Outcome | Independence: new state of Haiti | Crushed; Raj imposed; tighter British control |
| Long-term effect | First Black republic; signal to enslaved people worldwide | Abolished East India Company; birth of organized Indian nationalism |

### Comparing African Resistance Movements

| Movement | Leader | Method | Outcome |
|----------|--------|--------|---------|
| Sokoto Caliphate | Usman dan Fodio | Religious state-building | Held until 1903; demonstrates Islamic resistance model |
| Samory Touré's wars | Samory Touré | Military resistance, repeated | Captured 1898; delayed French advance for decades |
| Mahdist Sudan | Muhammad Ahmad | Religious-military state | Captured Khartoum 1885; crushed 1898 |
| Anglo-Zulu War | Zulu military | Conventional battle | Initial victory (Isandlwana); ultimately defeated |
| Yaa Asantewaa War | Yaa Asantewaa | Armed revolt | Defeated; Golden Stool never surrendered |
| Battle of Adwa | Menelik II | Conventional battle | Victory; Ethiopia remains independent |

### Comparing the Role of Education in Resistance

A recurring pattern: the colonizers' own educational systems produced the leaders who challenged them.

- **India:** British universities → Indian National Congress (1885) → independence movement
- **Philippines:** European education → José Rizal → Liga Filipina → Philippine Revolution
- **West Africa:** Western-educated Africans → Pan-Africanism → 20th-century independence movements
- **Contrast:** Cherokee assimilation — literacy, written constitution — offered no protection, because U.S. settler colonialism was driven by land, not cultural difference

### Spiritual vs. Political Resistance

| Type | Examples | Why it emerged | Why it failed |
|------|---------|----------------|---------------|
| Spiritual/millenarian | Ghost Dance, Xhosa Cattle Killing | Traditional authorities destroyed; no political alternative | Couldn't match colonial military power; backfired (Cattle Killing caused famine) |
| Political organizing | Indian National Congress, Liga Filipina | Colonial education created capable leaders | Not immediate — took decades — but ultimately succeeded |
