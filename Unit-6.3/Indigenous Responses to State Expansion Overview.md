# Indigenous Responses to State Expansion

**Unit 6 — Consequences of Industrialization | AP Period 3 | AMSCO pp. 388–397**

**Essential Question:** How did indigenous peoples and colonized societies resist colonial rule?

**Learning Objective:** Explain how and why various groups responded to the states that were expanding in this period.

**AP Themes:** GOV · CDI · SIO | **AP Skill:** Causation

---

## The Big Picture

European imperialism met resistance everywhere it expanded. The irony of colonialism was that it often provided colonized peoples with the very tools — Enlightenment ideas about natural rights and nationalism, Western-style education, shared grievances — they used to challenge their colonizers. Resistance took many forms: armed revolt, spiritual movements, legal and political organizing, and nationalist coalition-building. By the early 20th century, these movements were reshaping the globe.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to compare resistance movements across regions. Three key patterns repeat: (1) educated colonial subjects leading nationalist movements; (2) religious or spiritual frameworks inspiring resistance; (3) assimilation failing to protect indigenous peoples from dispossession. Know at least one example of each.

---

## Nationalist Movements in the Balkans

The Ottoman Empire had once stretched deep into Europe, but by the early 19th century ethnic nationalism — inspired by the French Revolution — was fragmenting its European territories in the [[Concept Notes/Balkan nationalism|Balkan Peninsula]]. Serbia (1815) and Greece (1829) won independence after long wars. Bosnia, Montenegro, and Bulgaria all revolted. After the Russo-Turkish War (1877–1878), the Treaty of Berlin freed Bulgaria, Romania, and Montenegro, though Bosnia-Herzegovina passed to Austria-Hungary. The ethnic tensions this left unresolved would set the stage for World War I.

## Resistance in the Americas

**North America** After the French and Indian War, Britain's [[Concept Notes/Proclamation of 1763|Proclamation of 1763]] reserved land west of the Appalachians for Native Americans — the first time a European government recognized indigenous territorial rights. After independence, the U.S. ignored it entirely. The **Cherokee nation** assimilated deeply, developing a written alphabet, a newspaper (the *Cherokee Phoenix*), and a U.S.-style constitution — yet the Indian Removal Act (1830) forced them west anyway. The Trail of Tears proves that cultural accommodation offered no protection against settler colonialism.

The Ghost Dance movement (c. 1889–1890) was a spiritual form of resistance spreading across the Great Plains, prophesying the return of the dead and the expulsion of white settlers. It ended at the Wounded Knee Massacre (1890).

**Latin America** [[Concept Notes/Túpac Amaru II|Túpac Amaru II]], a Jesuit-educated Inca-descended cacique in Peru, led the last great Indian revolt against Spain (1780–1781). Spreading from Peru into Bolivia and Argentina, the revolt drew initial criollo support before being crushed. Túpac Amaru II and his family were executed in Cuzco. His uprising became a symbol of indigenous resistance across the Americas.

## South Asian Movements

India's **sepoys** — Indian soldiers in British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] employ — made up the majority of Company forces by the mid-19th century. In 1857 the introduction of rifle cartridges greased with cow and pig fat (offensive to Hindu and Muslim soldiers alike) triggered the Indian Rebellion of 1857. The rebellion spread across northern India before Britain crushed it. Its consequences were sweeping: Britain exiled the Mughal emperor, abolished the East India Company, and imposed direct Crown rule — the British Raj (1858–1947).

The Raj had an unintended consequence. By educating Indians at British universities, it created a class of nationalist leaders. In 1885 British-educated Indians founded the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]], initially as a forum for grievances, quickly as a vehicle for self-rule. The INC eventually led India to independence in 1947.

> [!tip]
> Compare the Haitian Revolution [[Unit-5.2/[[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] & Revolutions Overview|Nationalism & Revolutions Overview]] with the Indian Rebellion of 1857: both challenged colonial rule, both drew on a sense of violated rights, and both produced very different outcomes — one produced independence, the other tightened imperial control.

## Southeast Asian Resistance

By the 1880s only Siam (Thailand) remained independent in Southeast Asia. In **Vietnam**, resistance to French colonialism began immediately; Emperor Ham Nghi and guerrilla leader Phan Dinh Phung sustained it through the 1880s–1890s.

In the **Philippines**, wealthy Filipino families sent sons to Europe, where José Rizal founded the Liga Filipina (1892) as a reform movement. The Spanish executed Rizal, shocking Filipino opinion and triggering the Philippine Revolution (1896). When the Spanish-American War (1898) ended, Filipinos expected independence — but the Treaty of Paris transferred control from Spain to the United States. The Philippine-American War (1899–1902) followed. Roughly 20,000 Filipino troops and over 200,000 civilians died. The Philippines remained a U.S. possession until 1946.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse the Spanish-American War (1898) with the Philippine-American War (1899–1902). The first transferred colonial authority; the second was the Filipino resistance to that transfer.

## Resistance in Australia and New Zealand

**Australia's Aboriginal** peoples — with over 50,000 years of continuous culture — were denied land ownership and legal protection under British colonial law. Thousands were killed defending their territory.

**New Zealand's Maori** had been promised land rights under the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), but British settlers repeatedly encroached. The Maori Wars (1840s–1872) resulted from these violations. By 1900, the Maori had lost most of their land.

## African Resistance

African resistance developed later than South Asian resistance, partly because European penetration of Africa's interior was slower. Three patterns stand out:

**Islamic state-building as resistance:** In West Africa, Usman dan Fodio founded the Sokoto Caliphate (1804) — the largest African empire since the 16th century — partly as a religious reform movement that resisted both corrupt rulers and, later, the British. In Sudan, Muhammad Ahmad declared himself the Mahdi (1881) and built a theocratic state that controlled Khartoum by 1885. Britain retook Sudan at the Battle of Omdurman (1898).

**Armed resistance:** Samory Touré built a powerful Mande kingdom in Guinea and fought the French twice (1883–1898) before being captured. The Xhosa of South Africa resisted British rule through a series of wars from 1811 to 1858. The Xhosa Cattle Killing (1856–57) was a spiritual-millenarian response — killing their cattle and destroying crops in hope that ancestral spirits would expel the British — but resulted instead in mass famine and land loss. The Zulu Kingdom inflicted one of Britain's worst colonial defeats at Isandlwana (1879) during the Anglo-Zulu War before being ultimately defeated and annexed.

**Late resistance:** Yaa Asantewaa, queen mother of the Asante Empire in present-day Ghana, led the War of the Golden Stool (1900) against British demands for the sacred Golden Stool — the last major African resistance war led by a woman.

**[[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]]** emerged among Western-educated Africans after World War I as a shared political identity challenging colonial rule; it became the ideological foundation of 20th-century African independence movements.

> [!exam-tip]
> Ethiopia is the key exception: Emperor Menelik II defeated Italy at the Battle of Adwa (1896), making Ethiopia the only African country to defeat a European colonizer during the Scramble for Africa. Examiners love this as a comparison point.

## Patterns of Resistance — AP Comparison Framework

| Type | Example(s) | Outcome |
|------|------------|---------|
| Armed revolt | Túpac Amaru II, Zulu, Samory Touré, Anglo-Zulu War | Usually suppressed; sometimes temporary success |
| Spiritual/millenarian | Ghost Dance, Xhosa Cattle Killing | Crushed; accelerated land loss |
| Religious-political state | Sokoto Caliphate, Mahdist Sudan | Created functioning states; eventually defeated |
| Legal/political organizing | Indian National Congress, Philippine Liga | Most successful long-term; led to independence |
| Assimilation | Cherokee nation | Failed to prevent dispossession |
