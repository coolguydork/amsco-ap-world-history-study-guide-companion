## Society: Belief Systems

**Hari Krishna**
The International Society for Krishna Consciousness (ISKCON), popularly called Hare Krishna — a Hindu devotional movement that spread globally from the 1960s after Prabhupada brought it to the West. Rooted in Vaishnavite Hindu scripture, it exemplifies how non-Western religious traditions spread globally amid the counterculture era. AP relevance: cultural diffusion; challenge to secular Western norms.

**New Age**
An eclectic spiritual movement emerging in the late 20th century that blended elements of [[Concept Notes/Buddhism\|Buddhism]], shamanism, [[Concept Notes/Sufism\|Sufism]], astrology, and other traditions for a largely Western audience. Reflected both dissatisfaction with organized religion and the global exchange of religious ideas. AP relevance: cultural diffusion; secularization trends and their counterreactions.

**Falun Gong**
A Chinese spiritual practice blending Buddhist and Daoist traditions with meditation and moral teachings, founded in 1992. Gained tens of millions of followers before the Chinese government banned and suppressed it in 1999, framing it as a threat to state authority. AP relevance: state repression of religion; human rights concerns in authoritarian states; connects to [[Concept Notes/Human Rights|Human Rights]].

**nonbelievers**
People who do not identify with any religion, including atheists and agnostics. A growing trend especially among younger generations in developed nations, reflecting secularization — the declining social influence of religion in public life. AP relevance: cultural change in the modern era; tension between secularism and religious revival.

## Culture: Arts & Entertainment

**modernism**
A broad cultural movement in the arts and literature of the late 19th–early 20th centuries that rejected traditional forms in favor of experimentation, abstraction, and the expression of the fractured modern experience. Manifested in cubism, stream-of-consciousness writing, and atonal music. AP relevance: cultural change driven by industrialization, war, and urbanization. (See also [[Unit-7.4/Economy in the Interwar Period Overview|Economy in the Interwar Period Overview]] for Cubism/Guernica context.)

**popular culture**
Mass entertainment and cultural forms appealing to broad audiences rather than the educated elite — radio, film, television, music. After World War II it became the primary vehicle through which American values and consumer habits spread globally. AP relevance: technology enabling cultural diffusion; Americanization.

**Bollywood**
The popular name for the Hindi-language film industry based in Mumbai (Bombay), India — the world's most prolific film industry by output. Known for elaborate musical sequences blending Indian and Western styles. AP relevance: non-Western cultural production competing with Hollywood; global cultural exchange flowing both ways.

**anime**
A style of Japanese animated film and television characterized by distinctive artwork and a wide range of genres. Became a major global cultural export from the 1980s onward, with 60% of the world's animated TV shows based on anime by 2016. AP relevance: Japan as cultural exporter; non-Western influence on global popular culture.

**reggae**
A music genre originating in Jamaica in the late 1960s, blending African rhythms, American R&B, and Jamaican folk traditions. Associated with Rastafarianism and artists like Bob Marley. AP relevance: cultural synthesis; Global South cultural production reaching worldwide audiences; music as resistance.

**K-pop**
Korean popular music blending electronic music, hip-hop, R&B, and pop, developed from the 1990s. Became a global phenomenon via social media and streaming, exemplifying how digital technology enabled non-Western popular culture to reach global audiences. AP relevance: cultural diffusion; challenge to Americanization of global culture.

## Culture: Sports

**Olympic Games**
International athletic competition revived in 1896 by Pierre de Coubertin, held every four years. The Olympics became a vehicle for internationalism, nationalism, and Cold War rivalry (boycotts of 1980 and 1984). AP relevance: cultural exchange; use of culture for political purposes; early symbol of globalization.

**World Cup**
FIFA's quadrennial international soccer (football) tournament, first held in 1930. The most-watched sporting event in the world, illustrating how global sports culture both unifies nations and channels national pride. AP relevance: cultural globalization; sport as soft power and national identity.

**National Basketball Association (NBA)**
The premier professional basketball league in the United States, founded 1946. Became a global cultural export from the 1980s–90s, especially through players like Michael Jordan, spreading American consumer culture and sports entertainment worldwide. By 2014 it included players from 30 countries. AP relevance: Americanization; global spread of cultural products.

## Society: Influences & Tensions

**consumer culture**
A cultural pattern, prominent after World War II in wealthy nations and spreading globally, in which personal identity and social status are tied to the purchase and display of goods. Driven by mass production, advertising, and rising incomes. AP relevance: economic development; Americanization; criticism of throwaway culture and environmental impact.

**Americanization**
The spread of American cultural products, values, consumer habits, and English-language media globally — especially after World War II through film, television, fast food, and the internet. Often perceived as cultural imperialism by other societies. AP relevance: cultural diffusion; tension between globalization and preservation of local cultures.

**throwaway culture**
A consumer pattern characterized by the rapid disposal of goods after brief use rather than repair or reuse, driven by cheap mass production and built-in obsolescence. Critics link it to environmental degradation and waste. AP relevance: environmental consequences of consumer culture; global spread of American consumption patterns.

**Malala Yousafzai**
Pakistani activist for girls' education (born 1997) who survived a Taliban assassination attempt in 2012 and became the youngest Nobel Peace Prize laureate (2014). Used global social media to amplify her cause. AP relevance: [[Concept Notes/Human Rights|Human Rights]] activism; women's rights in the developing world; social media as vehicle for political change.

## Technology: Commerce & Communication

**global brand**
A product or company recognized and sold across multiple countries, such as Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Apple, or Nike. Global brands spread consumer culture and are symbols of Americanization or corporate globalization. AP relevance: economic globalization; cultural homogenization debate.

**streaming video**
[[Concept Notes/Internet\|Internet]]-based delivery of video content in real time, through platforms like YouTube, Netflix, and Vimeo. Transformed how culture is distributed globally, enabling small producers from any country to reach worldwide audiences and accelerating the spread of K-pop, anime, and other non-Western cultural forms. AP relevance: technology and cultural diffusion; democratization of media.

**online commerce**
The buying and selling of goods and services over the internet (e-commerce), exemplified by Amazon and Alibaba. Made shopping global, disrupting traditional retail and enabling small producers to reach international markets. AP relevance: economic globalization; digital economy; erosion of geographic barriers to trade.

**social media**
Digital platforms (Facebook, Twitter/X, Instagram, Weibo, TikTok) enabling users to create and share content globally. Accelerated cultural diffusion, facilitated political mobilization (Arab Spring), and also enabled disinformation and government surveillance. AP relevance: technology transforming communication; civil society and resistance; double-edged impact of globalization.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves the tension between **homogenization** (one global consumer culture) and **hybridization** (cultures blending into something new, like Bollywood or K-pop). Know both arguments and examples for each.

> [!warning]
> "Americanization" does not mean only cultural exports. It also includes the spread of **English** as a global language, **consumer values** (individualism, brand loyalty), and **business practices** — not just McDonald's and Hollywood.
