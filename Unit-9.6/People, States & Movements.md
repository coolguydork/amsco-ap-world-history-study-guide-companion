# People, States & Movements

**Unit 9: Globalization** | AMSCO pp. 679–687

---

## Key Figures

**Walt Disney** (1901–1966)
American animator and media entrepreneur whose studio (founded 1923) created some of the most widely distributed cultural products of the 20th century — from *Snow White* to *The Lion King*. Disney theme parks and merchandise spread globally, making the company a symbol of American cultural imperialism. AP relevance: Americanization; the reach of US entertainment as soft power; consumer culture.

**Rupert Murdoch** (born 1931)
Australian-born media mogul whose company (News Corp, Fox) built a global news and entertainment empire spanning the US, UK, and Australia. His networks spread Anglo-American journalistic norms and conservative political culture worldwide. AP relevance: media globalization; the role of mass communication in spreading cultural values; corporate consolidation of culture.

**Malala Yousafzai** (born 1997)
Pakistani activist for girls' education who survived a Taliban assassination attempt in 2012 and became the youngest Nobel Peace Prize laureate (2014). Used global social media platforms to amplify her message. AP relevance: [[Concept Notes/Human Rights|Human Rights]] activism; women's rights in the developing world; social media as vehicle for political change; resistance to religious extremism.

**Bob Marley** (1945–1981)
Jamaican musician who brought reggae and Rastafarianism to global audiences in the 1970s. His music blended New Orleans jazz, R&B, Jamaican mento, and African rhythms, and carried a message of [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]] and social justice. AP relevance: Global South cultural production achieving worldwide reach; music as political resistance; cultural diffusion from periphery to core.

**George Harrison** (1943–2001)
Former Beatle whose 1970s release of music incorporating Hindu mantras sparked Western interest in the Hari Krishna movement. AP relevance: celebrity as vehicle for cultural diffusion; spread of non-Western religious practices to the West.

**Michael Jordan** (born 1963)
American basketball player whose global fame in the 1980s–90s turned the NBA into a worldwide cultural brand and spread **consumer culture** through Nike Air Jordan endorsements. AP relevance: sports as vehicle for Americanization; celebrity endorsement and global brands.

**Prabhupada (A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami)** (1896–1977)
Indian Hindu monk who brought the Hare Krishna (ISKCON) movement to the United States and Europe in the 1960s. AP relevance: spread of non-Western religious traditions through migration and charismatic leadership; cultural diffusion.

## Key States

**United States**
The dominant cultural force in the post–World War II era. American film, music, television, fast food, and consumer brands spread globally — a phenomenon called **Americanization**. The US also wielded pop culture as Cold War soft power (Radio Free Europe). Simultaneously a generator of global consumer culture and a target of resentment for cultural homogenization.

**China**
Both a subject of cultural globalization and a state that actively controlled it. China banned foreign social media (Facebook, Twitter, YouTube) and allowed only state-monitored domestic platforms (WeChat, Weibo, YuKu), censoring Communist Party criticism. Also suppressed Falun Gong (1999), treating it as a threat to state authority. AP relevance: authoritarian management of cultural globalization; internet censorship as state power.

**India**
Home to Bollywood — the world's most prolific film industry — India demonstrates that cultural globalization is not one-directional. Indian films reach audiences across the Middle East, East Africa, and diaspora communities worldwide. India also has 300 million English learners, illustrating both Americanization and active participation in the global knowledge economy.

**South Korea**
Invested in K-pop as a deliberate instrument of cultural diplomacy (soft power). The Korean government funded K-pop concerts and international tours. K-pop's global rise via streaming platforms reversed the dominant direction of cultural flow, demonstrating non-Western soft power in the 21st century.

**Jamaica**
Source of reggae — a globally influential music genre emerging from a small Caribbean nation, demonstrating that cultural production is not proportional to economic or military power. Reggae's global spread in the 1970s is a model AP example of how the Global South can shape global culture.

## Key Movements

**Modernism**
An international artistic and intellectual movement rejecting tradition in favor of experimentation and uncertainty. Manifested in Cubism, stream-of-consciousness fiction, jazz, and atonal music. A response to the disruptions of industrialization, World War I, and urbanization. AP relevance: cultural change as consequence of political/social upheaval.

**Harlem Renaissance**
1920s African American cultural movement centered in New York City. Writers, poets, and musicians reclaimed African American identity and created jazz — an early example of Global South (African diaspora) culture becoming internationally influential. AP relevance: cultural change driven by migration and social struggle; forerunner of later non-Western cultural exports.

**Hare Krishna / ISKCON**
International Society for Krishna Consciousness — a Hindu devotional movement that spread to the West from the 1960s, exemplifying the global spread of non-Western religious traditions. AP relevance: cultural diffusion; counterculture era religious seeking.

**New Age Religion**
A loosely defined spiritual movement blending Buddhist, shamanist, Sufi, and other traditions for Western audiences, arising in the 1970s–80s. Reflected both the global exchange of religious ideas and dissatisfaction with mainstream organized religion. AP relevance: cultural diffusion; secularization and its counterreactions.

**Falun Gong**
A Chinese spiritual practice (Buddhist and Daoist blend) that gained tens of millions of followers before government suppression in 1999. Its suppression generated international [[Concept Notes/Human Rights|Human Rights]] protests. AP relevance: state vs. civil society; religious freedom; authoritarian responses to perceived threats.

**Rastafarianism / Pan-Africanism**
Religious and political movement originating in Jamaica, asserting the spiritual and cultural unity of all people of African descent. Spread globally through reggae music. AP relevance: anti-colonial thought; Global South identity; connection to Topic 8.5 decolonization movements.

> [!tip]
> For essay prompts asking about cultural exchange or soft power, you can draw from multiple people here: Jordan (American soft power), Bob Marley (Global South cultural influence), Malala Yousafzai (social media as activist tool), and K-pop government investment (state-sponsored soft power).

> [!exam-tip]
> "Soft power" — the ability to influence through culture and values rather than force — is a key AP concept for this topic. Examples cut both ways: American soft power (Radio Free Europe, Hollywood, NBA) AND non-Western soft power (K-pop government investment, Bollywood's diaspora reach, Malala's Nobel Prize).
