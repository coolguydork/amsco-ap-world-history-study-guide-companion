# Globalized Culture

**Unit 9: Globalization** | **AP Period 4 (1900–present)** | AMSCO pp. 679–687

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam consistently asks about cultural diffusion in the modern era. Know the causes (technology, trade, migration), the examples (Bollywood, anime, K-pop, reggae as non-Western flows; Americanization as US dominance), and the tensions (homogenization vs. local preservation). Compare this "second wave" of globalization to the first wave (1800–1914).

## The Big Picture

Globalization after 1900 transformed culture in ways earlier empires never could. Three forces drove it: technological change (radio → film → television → internet → social media), the Cold War (which weaponized American pop culture as "soft power"), and the economic integration that followed it. The result was a world where a teenager in Seoul and one in São Paulo watched the same anime, wore the same sneakers, and followed the same social media feeds — while also producing K-pop and Bollywood films that bounced back toward the United States.

The AP key distinction is **bidirectionality**: globalization is not simply Americanization. Culture flows outward from the US (consumer culture, Hollywood, English), but also back from the Global South and East Asia (reggae, Bollywood, K-pop, anime). The debate over whether this homogenizes or enriches culture is central to the topic.

## Political, Social, and Artistic Changes (pp. 679–680)

The 20th century began with intellectual and artistic upheaval that mirrored its political violence. World War I and the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]] shattered confidence in progress. Artists, writers, and musicians responded with **modernism** — a deliberate rejection of tradition and embrace of uncertainty. Cubism (Picasso's *Guernica*), stream-of-consciousness fiction (Joyce, Proust), and atonal music (Schoenberg) all reflected a mechanized, urbanized society that felt fractured.

The **Harlem Renaissance** of the 1920s was modernism's most vital American expression: African American writers, poets, and musicians reclaimed their cultural identity, and jazz became a genuinely international language — an early example of Global South culture flowing northward.

Meanwhile, **social changes** multiplied: scientists (Einstein, Freud) upended assumptions about reality and the mind; rights movements brought marginalized voices into public discourse; and rapid technological change reshaped daily life from transport to medicine.

> [!tip]
> Harlem Renaissance is a great example for "cultural change caused by social/political change" essay prompts. Jazz also doubles as an early case of American cultural export, well before Hollywood.

## Consumer Culture and the American Century (pp. 681–682)

After World War II, the United States became the world's dominant cultural force. Radio and television created mass national audiences; commercial sponsorship tied entertainment to consumption. **Consumer culture** — identity built around buying and owning — spread first through the US and then globally as incomes rose and trade barriers fell.

**Americanization** defined this era: Hollywood films, Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Nike, and Apple became global brands. English spread as a second language worldwide (300 million Chinese were learning it by the early 21st century). Critics called American exports **throwaway culture** — cheap, disposable, wasteful — and many societies experienced American dominance as cultural imperialism.

Yet American culture also adapted locally. McDonald's sold McFalafel in Egypt and Mashed Potato Burgers in China — a process sometimes called "glocalization." **Online commerce** (Amazon, Alibaba) and **global brands** extended this economic-cultural integration further into the 21st century.

> [!warning]
> Do not conflate globalization with Americanization. The AP explicitly rewards students who recognize that cultural flows are multidirectional. Bollywood, K-pop, anime, and reggae are all examples of non-Western culture achieving global reach.

## Non-Western Cultural Exports (pp. 682–683)

From the 1970s onward, non-Western cultures carved out global space:

- **Bollywood**: India's Mumbai-based film industry — the world's most prolific by output — blends Indian musical traditions with global film styles and reaches audiences from the Middle East to East Africa to the US.
- **Anime**: Japanese animated television and film, introduced to the US in the 1980s, accounts for 60% of the world's animated TV shows by 2016. A major Japanese soft-power export.
- **Reggae**: Jamaican music that fused African, American R&B, and Caribbean traditions. Bob Marley carried it globally in the 1970s; its connection to Rastafarianism and [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]] gives it a political dimension.
- **K-pop**: Korean pop music blending multiple global traditions, amplified by YouTube and streaming platforms. The South Korean government actively invested in K-pop as cultural diplomacy.

**Streaming video** and **social media** were the crucial accelerants: they allowed small producers anywhere in the world to reach global audiences without needing a Hollywood distributor.

## Sports as Global Culture (p. 683)

Sports globalization paralleled entertainment. The **Olympic Games** (revived 1896) embodied early internationalism — and Cold War rivalry (boycotts in 1980 and 1984 turned them into geopolitical weapons). The **World Cup** became the most-watched sporting event on earth. Basketball — especially the **NBA** — spread as a global product from the 1980s through Michael Jordan and later LeBron James, with players from 30 countries by 2014.

Women's sports participation also expanded globally, including Muslim female athletes who adapted athletic wear to compete while observing religious practices — a microcosm of the negotiation between tradition and globalization.

## Religion in a Globalized World (p. 684)

Religious culture also spread and transformed. The **Hari Krishna** movement carried Vaishnavite Hinduism to the West from the 1960s. **New Age** religion blended Buddhist, shamanist, Sufi, and other traditions for Western audiences. In China, **Falun Gong** — a blend of Buddhist and Daoist practice — gained tens of millions of followers before the Chinese government banned and suppressed it in 1999, sparking international human rights protests.

Simultaneously, secularization accelerated in developed nations: an increasing share of young people, especially in Europe, identified as **nonbelievers** — not necessarily atheist, but unaffiliated with organized religion.

> [!exam-tip]
> Religion in 9.6 is often overlooked. Know that globalization both spread religious movements (Hare Krishna, Islam, Christianity) AND produced a secular counterreaction. Falun Gong also illustrates the intersection of globalization and state authoritarianism.

## Social Media: Power to Do Both Good and Ill (pp. 682–684)

**Social media** transformed communication faster than any prior technology. Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube enabled grassroots political organizing (Arab Spring), cultural movements, and individual voices like **Malala Yousafzai** — who used global platforms to advocate for girls' education after surviving a Taliban assassination attempt, becoming the youngest Nobel Peace laureate (2014).

But social media also enabled censorship: China banned foreign platforms and allowed only state-monitored domestic alternatives (WeChat, Weibo, YuKu), censoring any Communist Party criticism. The same technology that empowered dissidents worldwide was used by governments as a surveillance and control tool.

> [!warning]
> Social media's dual nature — tool of liberation AND tool of control — is a high-frequency AP prompt. Always give both sides. The Arab Spring and Chinese censorship are complementary examples.
