**Question 1.** In Source A, what was the historical context that caused Henry Luce to advocate for the United States to take a worldwide leadership position?

(A) Great Britain had no powerful country as an ally in its war against Germany when Luce wrote this.
(B) America had isolated itself economically from the rest of the world since 1917.
(C) The United States had entered World War I as soon as Britain and Germany began fighting.
(D) American culture, including magazines, had little appeal outside the United States.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Luce wrote in February 1941 — before Pearl Harbor. Britain stood largely alone against Nazi Germany (France had fallen in 1940). Luce was making a case for American global engagement to counter the Axis. (B) is wrong — America was economically engaged globally. (C) is wrong — the US was late to WWI. (D) contradicts Luce's explicit argument.

**Question 2.** In Source B, which conclusion is best supported by the McDonald's menu evidence?

(A) McDonald's has exactly the same menu items in every restaurant around the globe.
(B) Consumers of McDonald's menu items are not familiar with typical McDonald brand names.
(C) McDonald's menu items have been globalized but reflect local tastes and preferences.
(D) People in China do not like McDonald's Quarter Pounder hamburgers.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The chart shows local variations (McFalafel in Egypt, Mashed Potato Burger in China) alongside global brand recognition — classic "glocalization." (A) is directly contradicted by the chart. (B) and (D) cannot be concluded from this evidence.

**Question 3.** Based on both sources, from the mid-20th century to the early 21st century:

(A) arts, entertainment, and popular culture continued to reflect the influence of a globalized society
(B) global conflict was the most significant cause of globalization
(C) localized culture was mostly ignored because of globalized culture
(D) only American culture underwent globalization during this period

> [!tip] Answer: **A**

## Short-Answer Questions

**SAQ 1** — Use the passage below to answer all parts.

> "Western leaders exploited the lure of their popular culture during the Cold War. They created radio and television stations such as Radio Free Europe / Radio Liberty to broadcast as much popular culture material into the Soviet bloc as could be programmed. . . . In using American pop culture as a tool of the Cold War, Western policymakers were exploiting what political scientist Joseph Nye has referred to as 'soft power.' The notion of soft power refers to cultural, social, intellectual, and ideological ideas, values, attitudes, and behaviors that influence human life. . . . Soft power is a significant factor in global politics."
>
> — Lane Crothers, *Globalization and American Popular Culture*, 2013

**(A)** Identify ONE historical situation, other than the one illustrated in the passage, that supports Crothers's argument about soft power.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The spread of K-pop globally through government-funded concerts and tours supports Crothers's argument. The South Korean government deliberately invested in K-pop as a cultural export, demonstrating that states use popular culture to enhance their international reputation and influence — the same soft-power logic Crothers identifies in Cold War America.

**(B)** Explain how Crothers's argument affects international politics today.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity
> Crothers's argument remains relevant because states continue to use cultural exports as soft power. China bans foreign social media and promotes its own platforms (Weibo, WeChat) both to control domestic audiences and to project state-approved cultural content internationally. The United States' continued dominance through streaming platforms (Netflix, YouTube) and global brands shapes how billions of people worldwide engage with entertainment, values, and consumer culture. Soft power remains a dimension of geopolitical competition that complements hard military and economic power.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which globalization during the Cold War differs from globalization in the late 20th century to the present.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Cold War globalization was more deliberately state-directed and ideologically motivated: the US funded Radio Free Europe specifically to undermine Soviet bloc loyalty. Post-Cold War globalization is driven more by corporate actors (Apple, Netflix, Alibaba) and individual users on social media platforms, and flows more bidirectionally — non-Western cultures like K-pop and Bollywood now reach global audiences without state sponsorship.

**SAQ 2** — Answer all parts.

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which a globalized society influenced entertainment.

> [!tip] Model Response
> The global spread of anime illustrates this: a distinctly Japanese art form became the dominant format of animated television worldwide. By 2016, 60% of the world's animated TV shows were based on anime, demonstrating how a single cultural tradition from a non-Western country achieved global entertainment dominance — reshaping what audiences worldwide expected animation to look like.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which social changes of the 20th century led to changes in the arts.

> [!tip] Model Response
> World War I's industrialized mass slaughter led directly to modernism in the arts. Writers like James Joyce and artists like Picasso rejected traditional narrative forms and realistic representation, embracing fragmentation and uncertainty as the only honest responses to a world that had produced such mechanized destruction. The arts reflected the social reality that pre-war confidence in progress had been shattered.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which consumer culture transcended national borders in the late 20th century.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The internet and online commerce platforms (Amazon, Alibaba) made global shopping possible by the 1990s, allowing consumers in any country to purchase products from any other country. Simultaneously, multinational corporations established global supply chains so that the same brands — Nike shoes, Apple iPhones, Coca-Cola — appeared in stores worldwide. Consumer culture became transnational not just through imitation but through literal economic integration: the same products, manufactured in the same factories, marketed with the same advertising, sold everywhere.

## TAH: Compare Periods of Globalization

**AMSCO prompt:** Explain how globalization beginning c. 1960 is similar to and different from globalization c. 1800–1914.

| Dimension | First Wave (c. 1800–1914) | Second Wave (c. 1960–present) |
|---|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** | Largely one-directional (European → rest) | More bidirectional (US dominance + non-Western flows back) |
| **Leadership** | European colonial states | US initially; then MNCs and internet platforms |
| **Motives** | Raw materials, markets, civilizing mission | Consumer markets, soft power, ideological competition |
| **Knowledge economy** | Minimal — industrial goods dominated | Central — music, film, software, data are key exports |
| **Technology** | Steamship, telegraph, railroad | Television, internet, social media, streaming |
| **Scale of cultural exchange** | Significant but mostly elite and material | Mass-level; everyday cultural consumption crosses borders |

> [!exam-tip]
> Both waves brought economic integration AND cultural exchange. Key point for essays: the second wave is distinguished by (1) greater bidirectionality — non-Western cultures successfully exported; (2) speed and scale enabled by digital technology; (3) individual-level participation in cultural production and consumption.

## Key Themes for Essay Evidence

**Soft power / cultural diplomacy:** Radio Free Europe; K-pop government investment; Malala Yousafzai's Nobel Prize; Bollywood diaspora reach

**Glocalization (global + local):** McDonald's menu adaptations; Bollywood blending Indian + Hollywood styles; reggae blending African + American + Caribbean

**Technology as cause:** Radio → TV → internet → social media chain; streaming enables K-pop global reach; YouTube popularizes anime beyond Japan

**Homogenization critique:** American throwaway culture; English as global language; loss of local languages and traditions

**Hybridization response:** K-pop, Bollywood, reggae as genuinely new forms created by mixing; not pure Americanization
