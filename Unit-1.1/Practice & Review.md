## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Stimulus: Marco Polo, The Travels of Marco Polo, c. 1300 — describing Hangzhou, China*

> "[Hangzhou, China, has] ten principal markets. . . . [They] are all squares of half a mile to the side, and along their front passes the main street, which is 40 paces in width, and runs straight from end to end of the city, crossing many bridges of easy and commodious [convenient] approach. . . . So also parallel to this great street, but at the back of the market places, there runs a very large canal, on the bank of which towards the squares are built great houses of stone, in which the merchants from India and other foreign parts store their wares, to be handy for the markets. In each of the squares is held a market three days in the week, frequented by 40,000 or 50,000 persons."

**Question 1.** Based on the passage, which of the statements concerning the trading city of Hangzhou is most accurate?

(A) Foreigners were not welcome in Chinese trading cities, because they were considered barbarians and had nothing of value to trade.
(B) Many of the people coming to trade in cities in China were Europeans traveling along the Silk Roads.
(C) Hangzhou was a vital trading city because it had foreign merchants and was also accessible by the Grand Canal for internal trade.
(D) Chinese imperial governments limited the number of markets in Hangzhou to just a few centrally-located areas of the city.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The passage explicitly mentions merchants from India and other foreign parts storing goods near the markets, and the canal running through the city — consistent with the Grand Canal connecting internal markets. Option A is contradicted by the presence of foreign merchants. Option B is unsupported (the passage mentions Indian merchants, not Europeans). Option D is contradicted by the passage describing ten principal markets.

---

**Question 2.** What development, which began before the passage above was written, allowed Europeans to obtain the products of East Asia?

(A) Chinese dynasties, such as the Tang and the Song, grew stronger, so long-distance trade could be conducted with less risk.
(B) New maritime technology allowed for ocean-going ships to travel safely between Europe and Asia, going around southern Africa.
(C) The Tang Dynasty expanded into the Middle East, so the trade routes between Europe and Africa were under one ruler.
(D) With the defeat of the Islamic caliphates, Europeans were able to establish direct contact with the Tang and the Song dynasties.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage dates to c. 1300, so "before" means before 1300. Tang and Song military strength protected Silk Road trade networks — this is the most historically accurate answer for the period. Option B (rounding Africa) describes Portuguese voyages in the late 1400s — after the passage. Option C misrepresents Tang geography and is inaccurate. Option D is false — Islamic caliphates were not defeated by Europeans in this period.

---

**Question 3.** What products would Indians and other non-Chinese merchants be most likely to purchase in a market such as the one described in the passage?

(A) silk, porcelain, and tea
(B) pepper, nutmeg, and cinnamon
(C) cows, pigs, and oxen
(D) rugs, parchment, and horses

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Silk, porcelain, and tea were China's premier exports — the goods foreign merchants traveled to obtain. Options B and D describe goods that merchants would bring *to* China, not buy there. Option C (livestock) was not a major Chinese export.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Stimulus-Based (Empress Xu, *Instructions for the Inner Quarters*, c. 1420)

*Source*: Empress Xu argued that a woman's moral nature requires uprightness, modesty, and quiet dignity; that virtue must be cultivated within the self; and that "the wife is one who follows her husband."

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which Empress Xu's argument was influenced by long-standing Asian cultural traditions.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Empress Xu's argument reflects the Confucian tradition of filial piety and patriarchal hierarchy, in which women were expected to subordinate their desires to the male head of household. The Confucian concept that social harmony depends on clearly defined hierarchical relationships — ruler/subject, husband/wife, father/child — directly shaped her claim that a wife "follows her husband." This Confucian framework had been central to Chinese society since the Han Dynasty.

**(B)** Explain ONE example of how cultures in Asia from 1200–1450 resisted the expectations for women as recommended by Xu.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison / Continuity & Change
> Vietnamese women resisted the Confucian expectations described by Empress Xu. Unlike their Chinese counterparts, Vietnamese women enjoyed greater independence in marriage and rejected practices like foot binding and polygyny. Vietnamese society also preferred nuclear families (husband, wife, children only) over the extended family structure that Confucian patriarchy promoted. Vietnamese scholar-officials, who often led revolts against oppressive governments, reflected a broader cultural pattern of resisting Chinese cultural imposition.

**(C)** Explain ONE historical situation from 1200–1450 in which states in Asia attempted to limit Chinese political power.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation / Contextualization
> Vietnam repeatedly resisted Chinese political domination. When the Tang Dynasty began to crumble in the 8th century, Vietnamese rebels expelled China's occupying army using guerrilla warfare tactics rooted in their deep knowledge of local terrain. During the period 1200–1450, Vietnam continued to resist Chinese tributary demands and maintained its own governance structures, even while selectively adopting Chinese cultural practices such as writing and architecture. This resistance demonstrates that sinification was not passive acceptance but a contested process.

### SAQ 2 — No Stimulus (Buddhism and Confucianism in China)

**(A)** Identify ONE response by Chinese political leaders to the growing influence of Buddhism during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> During the Tang Dynasty (which preceded but shaped the 1200–1450 period), Chinese political leaders responded to Buddhism's growth by closing monasteries and seizing their land. Tang rulers, who saw themselves as the rulers of the "Middle Kingdom," were uncomfortable with a foreign religion gaining such prominence and drawing individuals away from native Chinese traditions like Confucianism and Daoism. Despite this suppression, Chan Buddhism remained popular among ordinary people.

**(B)** Describe ONE example of a Chinese cultural tradition that withstood the spread of Buddhism during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity & Change
> Confucianism, particularly its emphasis on filial piety and social hierarchy, withstood and ultimately absorbed Buddhist influence during this period. Rather than being displaced, Confucianism adapted by incorporating Buddhist ideas of self-cultivation and Daoist metaphysics to produce Neo-Confucianism. Neo-Confucianism reinforced Confucian values of loyalty, deference to authority, and family hierarchy even as it engaged with Buddhist thought — demonstrating the continuity of Confucian social norms.

**(C)** Explain ONE example of how the spread of Buddhism influenced Confucianism during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The spread of Buddhism in China directly influenced the development of Neo-Confucianism. As Buddhist texts became widely available through woodblock printing during the Song Dynasty, Confucian scholars began incorporating Buddhist concepts of meditation, self-cultivation, and abstract metaphysics into Confucian ethical frameworks. The result was Neo-Confucianism (c. 960–1280), which synthesized Confucian rational ethics with the spiritual introspection of Buddhism and the naturalistic philosophy of Daoism. This new intellectual system became dominant across East Asia, demonstrating how Buddhist influence reshaped rather than destroyed Confucian tradition.

## Historical Reasoning Practice: Contextualization

**Prompt**: Explain how the passage below provides context for the spread of Buddhism in China in light of other developments of the time.

*Passage*: In 629, a Chinese Buddhist monk named Xuanzang left China to make a pilgrimage to India, birthplace of Buddhism. He traveled the Silk Roads to Central Asia, then to India, studying in Buddhist monasteries and at Nalanda University. After 16 years, he returned to China as a celebrity, bringing Buddhist texts he spent the rest of his life translating into Chinese.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization
> Xuanzang's pilgrimage (629–645) provides context for the spread of Buddhism in China by illustrating how the Silk Roads served as conduits not just for goods but for religious ideas and texts. His journey was only possible because Tang-era investment in roads, caravansaries, and diplomatic relationships had made the Silk Roads relatively safe and traversable. The fact that he studied at Nalanda University in India — a major center of Buddhist scholarship — demonstrates that Buddhism in this period was a sophisticated, intellectually rich tradition, not a simple folk religion. Xuanzang's celebrity return and decades of translation work also shows how the Chinese state could selectively absorb foreign religious traditions: the Tang emperor welcomed Xuanzang back, suggesting official openness to Buddhism despite later suppression. This tension — between welcoming Buddhist learning and fearing its social influence — contextualizes the broader ambivalence of Tang and Song rulers toward the religion.

## Essential Question Response

**Prompt**: In one to three paragraphs, explain how developments in China and other parts of East Asia between c. 1200 and c. 1450 reflect continuity, innovation, and diversity.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity, Change, Comparison
> The period c. 1200 to c. 1450 in East Asia was defined by the continuation of Chinese imperial traditions alongside remarkable economic and cultural innovation. The Song Dynasty (960–1279) maintained the imperial bureaucracy that had existed since the Qin Dynasty — a clear continuity — but expanded and modified it through the meritocratic civil service exam, creating the new scholar-gentry class. At the same time, the Song represented dramatic innovation: the adoption of Champa rice triggered a population boom, proto-industrialization transformed manufacturing, and the commercialization of the economy made China the most market-oriented society in the world. Technological innovations — the compass, woodblock printing, and the first guns — further distinguished the Song era.
>
> Diversity appears most clearly in how neighboring societies — Japan, Korea, and Vietnam — responded to Chinese cultural dominance. All three engaged in sinification, adopting Chinese writing, Buddhism, Confucianism, and administrative structures. Yet each resisted in distinctive ways. Japan, separated from China by sea, developed a feudal shogunate system with a distinctive warrior culture (bushido, samurai, daimyo) and produced original literature like *The Tale of Genji*. Korea maintained a more powerful landed aristocracy than China and blocked commoners from the civil service exam. Vietnam's women rejected foot binding and polygyny, and Vietnamese scholar-officials owed loyalty to villages rather than the emperor — while Vietnamese rebels successfully expelled Tang occupiers through guerrilla warfare. The period thus reflects not a simple story of Chinese dominance but a dynamic, contested process of cultural exchange and adaptation across a diverse region.
