## Multiple Choice Questions

**Question 1.** The Song Dynasty's civil service examination system is best described as an example of:

A) A meritocracy that eliminated class distinctions in Chinese society
B) A system that allowed some upward mobility while still underrepresenting the poor
C) A purely aristocratic system that maintained hereditary privilege
D) A religious test requiring knowledge of Buddhist scriptures

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The exam opened positions to men from lower economic classes, allowing more upward mobility than any contemporary system — but the poor were still vastly underrepresented because years of study required resources. A is wrong: a new dominant class (the scholar gentry) replaced the old aristocracy — class distinctions remained. C is wrong: the exam explicitly moved away from aristocratic exclusivity. D is wrong: exams tested Confucian texts, not Buddhist scriptures.

---

**Question 2.** Which of the following best explains why merchants held lower social status than farmers in Song China?

A) Merchants were prohibited from participating in the civil service exam
B) Confucian ideology viewed merchants as unproductive because they exchanged rather than created or grew goods
C) The Tang Dynasty had passed laws restricting merchant wealth
D) Merchants were primarily foreigners who did not share Chinese cultural values

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Confucian philosophy ranked labor that produced tangible goods (farming, crafting) above labor that merely exchanged them. Merchants profited from others' work without growing crops or making things — Confucianism considered this less virtuous. A is wrong: social status and exam eligibility were separate things. C is wrong: no such Tang laws are described. D is wrong: most Song merchants were Chinese citizens.

---

**Question 3.** The spread of Buddhism from India to China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam is best explained by which combination of factors?

A) Military conquest and forced conversion
B) Trade networks, missionary activity, and Buddhism's adaptability to local traditions
C) The political directives of the Tang and Song dynasties
D) The decisions of individual rulers to mandate Buddhist practice

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Silk Roads provided pathways; monks like Xuanzang made pilgrimages and returned with texts; and Buddhism's ability to fuse with local traditions (Daoism → Chan Buddhism) made adoption natural. A is wrong: Buddhism spread through peaceful means, not conquest. C is wrong: the Tang was sometimes hostile to Buddhism (monastery closures, land seizures). D is wrong: no single political mandate explains a pattern spanning multiple independent states.

---

**Question 4.** How did Vietnam's relationship with Chinese cultural influence differ most significantly from Korea's?

A) Vietnam rejected all aspects of Chinese culture, while Korea adopted Chinese governance completely
B) Vietnam maintained a more adversarial relationship with China, including violent rebellions, while Korea's resistance was mainly political
C) Vietnam adopted Buddhism but rejected Confucianism, while Korea adopted Confucianism but rejected Buddhism
D) Vietnam became a tributary state of China, while Korea maintained full independence

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Vietnam fought violent rebellions against Chinese influence and expelled Chinese occupying armies in the 8th century through guerrilla warfare. Korea's resistance was subtler: its aristocracy blocked meritocratic reforms politically but did not launch armed revolts. A overstates Vietnam's rejection — it adopted the Chinese writing system, architectural styles, and a merit-based bureaucracy. C is wrong: both adopted Buddhism and Confucianism. D is wrong: Korea was also a tributary state.

## Short Answer Questions (SAQs)

### SAQ 1 — Comparison
*"Using specific evidence, explain ONE similarity and ONE difference between the roles of women in Song China and in Dar al-Islam during the period c. 1200–c. 1450."*

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Both Song China and Dar al-Islam maintained patriarchal social structures in which women's roles were subordinated to male authority. In China, Confucian filial piety required women to defer to the male head of household, and foot binding physically restricted women's mobility and public participation. In Dar al-Islam, urban growth led to new restrictions — veiling became more widespread and the harem system confined upper-class women. In both societies, women's public roles were constrained by cultural and religious norms, even if the specific mechanisms differed.

> [!tip] Model Response — Difference
> A key difference is that Islamic women held significantly more formal legal rights than women in Song China. Under shariah, Islamic women could inherit property, retain it after marriage, initiate divorce under certain conditions, and receive a cash settlement if divorced. Women in Song China operated within a Confucian framework that offered fewer legal protections, and foot binding — which had no Islamic parallel — further physically restricted Chinese women's autonomy in ways Islamic women did not experience.

---

### SAQ 2 — Continuity and Change
*"Explain ONE continuity and ONE change in Chinese governance from the Tang Dynasty to the end of the Song Dynasty (c. 618–1279)."*

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity
> One continuity was the imperial bureaucracy itself. The system of appointed officials carrying out state policy had existed since the Qin Dynasty (221–207 BCE) and remained the foundational structure of Chinese government through both the Tang and Song dynasties. Though it expanded significantly under the Song, the principle of centralized administrative governance — rather than feudal decentralization or military rule — remained constant across this period.

> [!tip] Model Response — Change
> One significant change was the shift toward meritocracy. While the Tang also had a civil service exam, Emperor Song Taizu deliberately expanded access to men from lower economic classes. This created the scholar gentry as a new dominant social class — men who earned status through demonstrated knowledge of Confucian texts rather than through hereditary land ownership. The aristocracy was gradually displaced, representing a genuine change in who held power and how they obtained it.

### Building a Strong Thesis

A strong AP World History thesis should make a historically defensible claim, establish a line of reasoning, and go beyond restating the prompt.

**Weak:** "Song China and Dar al-Islam had similarities and differences in their social structures."

**Strong:** "Although both Song China and Dar al-Islam maintained patriarchal social hierarchies that constrained women's public roles, Islamic women held significantly more formal legal rights under shariah law, reflecting the different ways in which Confucian ideology and Islamic theology conceptualized gender and family structure."

The strong thesis names a specific similarity, a specific difference, and explains *why* the difference exists.

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning]
> These mistakes appear frequently on student essays. Know them before exam day.

**"The civil service exam created a classless society in China."** Wrong. The poor were vastly underrepresented because years of study required resources. China remained a class society dominated by the scholar gentry.

**"Islam treated women poorly."** Incomplete. Islamic women had *more* legal rights than their Christian and Jewish counterparts — property rights, divorce rights, inheritance rights. Restrictions were real but intensified mainly in urban contexts.

**"Japan simply copied Chinese culture."** Wrong. Japan selectively adopted Chinese elements while developing distinctly Japanese institutions (the shogunate, bushido) and literature (*The Tale of Genji*). Island geography gave Japan the luxury of choosing what to borrow.

**"The Abbasid Caliphate unified the entire Islamic world."** Wrong. The Abbasids coexisted with rival caliphates (the Umayyads in Spain) and later Turkic sultanates. Political unity was never what held Dar al-Islam together — religion and commerce were.

**"The Battle of Tours ended Islam in Western Europe."** Wrong. Muslims ruled Spain for seven more centuries after Tours. Tours stopped *further northward expansion*, not Islamic presence in Europe.
