## Multiple Choice Questions

### Question 1
The Song Dynasty's civil service examination system is best described as an example of:

A) A meritocracy that eliminated class distinctions in Chinese society  
B) A system that allowed some upward mobility while still underrepresenting the poor  
C) A purely aristocratic system that maintained hereditary privilege  
D) A religious test requiring knowledge of Buddhist scriptures

**Answer: B**

*Why A is wrong:* The civil service exam did not eliminate class distinctions — the poor were still vastly underrepresented because studying for years required resources. The scholar gentry emerged as a new dominant class, not a classless society.

*Why C is wrong:* The exam explicitly opened positions to men from lower economic classes — it was less aristocratic than previous systems, though imperfect.

*Why D is wrong:* The exams were based on Confucian texts, not Buddhist scriptures.

*Why B is correct:* The exam was more open than any contemporary system but still favored those with resources to study. It allowed *some* mobility while structural inequalities remained.

### Question 2
Which of the following best explains why merchants held lower social status than farmers in Song China?

A) Merchants were prohibited from participating in the civil service exam  
B) Confucian ideology viewed merchants as unproductive because they exchanged rather than created or grew goods  
C) The Tang Dynasty had passed laws restricting merchant wealth  
D) Merchants were primarily foreigners who did not share Chinese cultural values

**Answer: B**

*Why A is wrong:* The exam was open to merchants' sons — social status and exam eligibility are different things.

*Why C is wrong:* No specific Tang laws are cited; this confuses the explanation.

*Why D is wrong:* Most merchants in China were Chinese citizens, not foreigners.

*Why B is correct:* Confucian philosophy ranked labor that produced tangible goods (farming, crafting) above labor that merely exchanged them. Merchants didn't grow crops or make things — they profited from others' work, which Confucianism considered less virtuous.

### Question 3
The spread of Buddhism from India to China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam is best explained by which combination of factors?

A) Military conquest and forced conversion  
B) Trade networks, missionary activity, and Buddhism's adaptability to local traditions  
C) The political directives of the Tang and Song dynasties  
D) The decisions of individual rulers to mandate Buddhist practice

**Answer: B**

*Why A is wrong:* Buddhism spread primarily through peaceful means — trade routes and missionaries — not military conquest.

*Why C is wrong:* The Tang was sometimes *hostile* to Buddhism (monastery closures, land seizures). The Song tolerated but did not particularly promote it. Buddhism spread despite, not because of, dynastic directives.

*Why D is wrong:* While some rulers promoted Buddhism (Prince Shotoku in Japan), Buddhism spread across multiple states with different rulers. No single political mandate explains the pattern.

*Why B is correct:* The Silk Roads provided pathways; monks like Xuanzang made pilgrimages and returned with texts; and Buddhism's ability to fuse with local traditions (Daoism → Chan Buddhism) made it easy to adopt.

### Question 4
The Battle of Tours (732) is historically significant because it:

A) Ended Islamic rule in Spain permanently  
B) Marked the westernmost limit of rapid Islamic military expansion into Europe  
C) Prevented the spread of Islam into the Byzantine Empire  
D) Forced the Abbasid Caliphate to abandon its expansion into Western Europe

**Answer: B**

*Why A is wrong:* Muslims continued to rule Spain for the next seven centuries after Tours.

*Why C is wrong:* Tours was in modern-day France, not near the Byzantine Empire; the Byzantines were a separate conflict.

*Why D is wrong:* The Abbasids were centered in Baghdad and had indirect involvement; the armies at Tours were primarily Umayyad-connected forces.

*Why B is correct:* Tours stopped the northward expansion of Islamic military power. Most of Western Europe remained Christian, while al-Andalus (Spain) continued under Islamic rule for centuries.

### Question 5
How did Vietnam's relationship with Chinese cultural influence differ most significantly from Korea's?

A) Vietnam rejected all aspects of Chinese culture, while Korea adopted Chinese governance completely  
B) Vietnam maintained a more adversarial relationship with China, including violent rebellions, while Korea's resistance was mainly political  
C) Vietnam adopted Buddhism but rejected Confucianism, while Korea adopted Confucianism but rejected Buddhism  
D) Vietnam became a tributary state of China, while Korea maintained full independence

**Answer: B**

*Why A is wrong:* Vietnam did adopt Chinese elements (writing system, architectural styles, merit-based bureaucracy). "Rejected all aspects" overstates the case.

*Why C is wrong:* Both countries adopted elements of both Buddhism and Confucianism.

*Why D is wrong:* Korea was also a tributary state of China.

*Why B is correct:* Vietnam fought violent rebellions against Chinese influence and expelled Chinese occupying armies in the 8th century through guerrilla warfare. Korea's aristocracy resisted meritocratic reform politically but didn't launch armed revolts against Chinese influence in the same way.

## Short Answer Questions (SAQs)

### SAQ 1 — Comparison
*"Using specific evidence, explain ONE similarity and ONE difference between the roles of women in Song China and in Dar al-Islam during the period c. 1200–c. 1450."*

**Sample Similarity:**
Both Song China and Dar al-Islam maintained **patriarchal social structures** in which women's roles were subordinated to male authority. In China, the Confucian concept of filial piety required women to defer to the male head of household, and foot binding physically restricted women's mobility and participation in public life. In Dar al-Islam, while Muhammad had elevated women's status relative to pre-Islamic Arabia, urban growth led to new restrictions — veiling became more widespread, and the harem system confined women in upper-class households. In both societies, women's public roles were constrained by cultural and religious expectations, even if the specific mechanisms differed.

**Sample Difference:**
A key difference is that **Islamic women had more legal property rights** than women in Song China. Under shariah, Islamic women could inherit property and retain ownership after marriage, initiate divorce under certain conditions, and receive a cash settlement if divorced. In contrast, women in Song China operated within a Confucian framework that emphasized deference to male authority and offered fewer formal legal protections. The practice of foot binding — which had no direct Islamic parallel — further physically restricted Chinese women's autonomy in ways that Islamic women did not experience.

### SAQ 2 — Causation
*"Explain ONE cause of the Islamic Golden Age in Baghdad and ONE effect of the Golden Age on later developments in Europe."*

**Sample Cause:**
One cause of the Islamic Golden Age was the **Abbasid Caliphate's culture of intellectual inquiry**, rooted in Muhammad's teaching to "go in quest of knowledge even unto China." The Abbasids established the **House of Wisdom** in Baghdad, which attracted scholars from across Afro-Eurasia. They funded the translation of Greek, Indian, and Chinese texts into Arabic, creating a foundation for original scholarship in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, philosophy, and law. This institutional and ideological commitment to learning made Baghdad the intellectual capital of the medieval world.

**Sample Effect:**
One effect of the Islamic Golden Age on Europe was the **transmission of Aristotelian philosophy** through al-Andalus. Islamic scholars, especially **Ibn Rushd (Averroes)**, wrote detailed commentaries on Aristotle in Arabic. These commentaries were translated into Latin and influenced the Jewish philosopher **Maimonides**, whose syntheses then influenced the Christian philosopher **St. Thomas Aquinas**. This chain of intellectual transmission helped lay the groundwork for **Scholasticism** and eventually the **Renaissance** in Europe — movements that integrated reason and faith in ways that transformed European intellectual life.

---

### SAQ 3 — Continuity and Change
*"Explain ONE continuity and ONE change in Chinese governance from the Tang Dynasty to the end of the Song Dynasty (c. 618–1279)."*

**Sample Continuity:**
One continuity in Chinese governance was the **imperial bureaucracy** itself. The bureaucratic system of appointed officials carrying out state policy had existed since the Qin Dynasty (221–207 BCE) and remained the foundational structure of Chinese government through both the Tang and Song dynasties. Though it expanded significantly under the Song, the *principle* of centralized administrative governance — rather than feudal decentralization or military rule — remained constant across this period.

**Sample Change:**
One significant change was the **shift toward meritocracy** under the Song. While the Tang Dynasty also had a civil service exam, Emperor Song Taizu deliberately expanded educational access to men from lower economic classes so they could compete for bureaucratic positions. This created the **scholar gentry** as a new dominant social class — men who earned their status through demonstrated knowledge of Confucian texts rather than through hereditary land ownership. The aristocracy, which had dominated Chinese governance through most of earlier history, was gradually displaced by this new examination-based elite, representing a genuine change in *who* held power and *how* they obtained it.

## DBQ / LEQ Guidance

### DBQ Themes to Watch For

Topic 1.1 content frequently appears in DBQ documents related to:
- Chinese technology and its global spread
- Comparisons of women's roles across cultures
- The role of religion in shaping governance
- Cross-cultural intellectual exchange

**For any DBQ using a Song Dynasty source**, ask:
- Does this document reflect government perspective? Commercial perspective? Confucian scholar perspective?
- Does it represent a typical Chinese view, or an outsider's view of China?
- What does it *not* say that would complicate the argument?

**For any DBQ using an Islamic Golden Age source**, ask:
- Is this from the Abbasid core, or from the Islamic periphery (al-Andalus, etc.)?
- Does the source reflect the scholar class? The merchant class? The ruler's perspective?
- What role does religion play in the argument?

### Building a Strong Thesis

A strong AP World History thesis for a comparison prompt should:
1. Make a historically defensible claim that responds to the prompt
2. Establish a line of reasoning (not just list facts)
3. Go beyond simply restating the prompt

**Weak thesis:** "Song China and Dar al-Islam had similarities and differences in their social structures."

**Strong thesis:** "Although both Song China and Dar al-Islam maintained patriarchal social hierarchies that constrained women's public roles, Islamic women held significantly more formal legal rights under shariah law, reflecting the different ways in which Confucian ideology and Islamic theology conceptualized gender and family structure."

Notice: the strong thesis names *a specific type of similarity*, names *a specific type of difference*, and explains *why* the difference exists.

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning]
> These mistakes appear frequently on student essays. Know them before exam day.

**"The civil service exam created a classless society in China."**
Wrong. The poor were vastly underrepresented because years of studying required resources most people didn't have. Upward mobility was greater than elsewhere, but China remained a class society dominated by the scholar gentry.

**"Islam treated women poorly."**
Incomplete and inaccurate for this period. The AP source emphasizes that Islamic women had *more* legal rights than their Christian and Jewish counterparts — property rights, divorce rights, inheritance rights. Restrictions were real but not absolute, and they intensified in urban contexts.

**"Japan simply copied Chinese culture."**
Wrong. Japan *selectively* adopted Chinese elements — Buddhism, woodblock printing, artistic styles — while developing distinctly Japanese institutions (the shogunate, the samurai code of bushido) and literature (*The Tale of Genji*). Japan's island geography gave it the luxury of choosing what to borrow.

**"The Abbasid Caliphate unified the entire Islamic world."**
Wrong. The Abbasids were always one political entity within a larger Dar al-Islam. Rival caliphates (the Umayyads in Spain) and later Turkic sultanates existed alongside and independent of Abbasid authority. Political unity was never what held the Islamic world together — religion and commerce were.

**"The Battle of Tours ended Islam in Western Europe."**
Wrong. Muslims continued to rule Spain for seven centuries after the Battle of Tours (732). Tours stopped *further northward expansion*, not Islamic presence in Europe.
