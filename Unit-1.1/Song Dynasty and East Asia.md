# Topic 1.1 — Developments in East Asia

> [!summary] Essential Question
> How did developments in China and the rest of East Asia between c. 1200 and c. 1450 reflect **continuity, innovation, and diversity**?

## Context: Why China Matters Here

The **Song Dynasty (960–1279)** was the wealthiest, most commercially sophisticated, and most technologically innovative state on earth during this period. China sets the standard against which everything else in Unit 1 is measured. Understanding Song China is the key to understanding the entire unit.

---

## Government: The Song Dynasty State

### The Imperial Bureaucracy

China's [[Concept Notes/Imperial Bureaucracy|imperial bureaucracy]] was one of the most enduring institutions in world history — it had been running continuously since the [[Concept Notes/Qin Dynasty|Qin Dynasty]] (221–207 BCE). Under the Song, it **expanded significantly**.

The bureaucracy was staffed through the **[[Concept Notes/Civil Service Exam|civil service exam]]** — a meritocracy in which men (mostly of the scholar class) could earn government positions by demonstrating mastery of [[Concept Notes/Confucianism|Confucian]] texts. Emperor Song Taizu expanded access to lower-income men, creating more upward mobility than any other system of the time. However, the poor remained vastly underrepresented, and women were excluded entirely.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse "meritocracy" with full equality. The civil service exam gave *some* upward mobility to non-elites, but the system still heavily favored those wealthy enough to afford years of education.

By the end of the Song period, the bureaucracy had grown so large and expensive that it became a financial burden — contributing to the dynasty's weakness.

### The Scholar Gentry

Bureaucratic expansion created a new, dominant social class: the **scholar gentry**. Educated in Confucian philosophy, they soon outnumbered the old hereditary aristocracy. Below them in the Confucian hierarchy: farmers, artisans, and merchants (in that order). Merchants ranked last because they created no goods — they merely exchanged them.

### Tributary System

China reinforced its political dominance through the **tributary system**: neighboring states (Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and SE Asian kingdoms) sent representatives to the Chinese emperor bearing gifts and performing the **kowtow** — bowing until one's head touched the floor. In return, they received trade privileges and protection. This system projected Chinese power without requiring military occupation.

## Economy: The World's Most Advanced

### Agricultural Revolution

Two developments supercharged Chinese agriculture:

1. **Champa rice** — a fast-ripening, drought-resistant strain from present-day Vietnam. It spread to China before the 11th century, allowed double-cropping (summer *and* winter harvests), and enabled farming in previously unusable lands.
2. **Innovative farming techniques** — manure fertilization, elaborate irrigation (ditches, water wheels, pumps, terraces), and heavy plows pulled by water buffalo.

Result: China's population grew from about 25% of total world population to nearly **40%** over the three centuries of Song rule.

### Manufacturing and Proto-Industrialization

The Song Dynasty experienced **[[Concept Notes/Proto-Industrialization|proto-industrialization]]** — rural artisans producing goods beyond local consumption, using simple equipment rather than factories. Key products:

- **Steel** (made by removing carbon from cast iron) — used for bridges, gates, ship anchors, religious items, and farm tools
- **Porcelain** — lightweight, strong, easily decorated; a prized export
- **Silk** — transported along trade networks, especially by sea

China became the world's most **commercialized** society, shifting from local consumption to market production. Major exports: porcelain, textiles, tea.

### Technology Enabling Trade

- **Compass** — used in maritime navigation
- **Printed navigation charts** — allowed sailing in open water, out of sight of land
- **Grand Canal** — 30,000+ miles of internal waterways, enabling cheap bulk transport of goods
- **Gunpowder** — Song innovators made the first guns; the technology spread via the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]] to all of Eurasia

> [!exam-tip]
> Chinese technological innovations — gunpowder, printing, the compass — are among the most frequently tested examples of **cross-cultural technology transfer** on the AP exam. Be ready to explain *how* these technologies spread westward (merchants on the Silk Roads; later, the Mongol networks).

### Taxes and Money Economy

Rather than requiring labor on public projects, the Song government **paid** workers in cash — increasing money in circulation and stimulating economic growth. The tributary system also brought in wealth.

## Society: Class, Gender, Religion

### Foot Binding

One of the most distinctive (and historically significant) constraints on Chinese women was **[[Concept Notes/Foot Binding|foot binding]]**, which became common among aristocratic families during the Song Dynasty. Girls' feet were wrapped tightly from a young age so bones wouldn't grow naturally. A bound foot signaled social status and desirability — but it also restricted women's movement and participation in public life. Foot binding was finally banned in 1912.

> [!warning]
> Foot binding is often tested as evidence of how patriarchal systems *intensified* during the Song period. Confucian traditions "included both respect for women and the expectation that they would defer to men" — and Song-era practices strengthened the latter at the expense of the former.

### Buddhism and Neo-Confucianism

Buddhism had traveled to China from India via the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]], becoming widespread during the Tang Dynasty. Three branches shaped Asia:

| Branch | Emphasis | Where Dominant |
|--------|----------|----------------|
| **Theravada** | Personal spiritual growth; meditation | Southeast Asia |
| **Mahayana** | Spiritual growth for all; service | China, Korea |
| **Tibetan** | Chanting | Tibet |

Buddhism blended with [[Concept Notes/Daoism|Daoism]] to produce **Chan (Zen) Buddhism**, which emphasized direct experience and meditation — a syncretic, distinctly Chinese form of the faith.

**[[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]]** emerged between 770 and 840 CE. It combined rational thought with Daoist and Buddhist ideas, emphasizing ethics over metaphysics. It became the intellectual foundation of governance in China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam — arguably the most powerful ideology in East Asian history.

### Urban Culture and Intellectual Life

At the height of the Song Dynasty, China was the world's most **urbanized** land. Chang'an, Hangzhou, and Guangzhou were cosmopolitan centers of commerce and culture. The development of **woodblock printing** (7th century) and **paper** enabled the mass production of books, expanding literacy among the scholar gentry and spreading Confucian and Buddhist texts widely.

## East Asia's Neighbors: Sinification and Resistance

An important pattern runs through Japan, Korea, and Vietnam: each country borrowed selectively from China while fighting to maintain its own identity. This process is called **[[Concept Notes/Sinification|sinification]]** — the adoption of Chinese practices.

### Japan

Japan had more ability to control its interactions with China because it was separated by sea. Key developments:

- Prince Shotoku Taishi (574–622) promoted Buddhism and Confucianism alongside Japan's native **[[Concept Notes/Shinto|Shinto]]** religion
- The **Heian period (794–1185)** was characterized by emulation of Chinese politics, art, and literature — but also Japanese literary innovation (the 11th-century *Tale of Genji*, the world's first novel, written by a woman)
- In 1192, after the decline of the Heian court, the **Minamoto clan** installed a **shogun** (military ruler). Though an emperor remained, real power lay with the shogun and the feudal aristocracy (the **daimyo**)

**Japanese feudalism** resembled European feudalism: rigid hereditary hierarchy, little social mobility, mutual obligations between lords and peasants. Key differences:

| Feature | Japan | Europe |
|---------|-------|--------|
| Top lord | Daimyo (more powerful than the shogun in practice) | King (theoretically supreme) |
| Warrior code | **Bushido** (frugality, loyalty, martial arts, honor unto death) | **Chivalry** (honor, courtesy, duty to God, courtly love) |
| Warrior class | Samurai | Knights |
| Peasant class | Serfs | Serfs |

### Korea

Korea shared a land boundary with China and was deeply influenced by it through the tributary relationship. Koreans adopted Confucian governance, Chinese writing (though awkwardly — Korean is structurally very different), and Buddhist and Confucian beliefs. However, Korea's **landed aristocracy was more powerful** than in China, preventing some reforms — notably, the civil service exam was not open to peasants, so Korea never achieved a true meritocracy.

### Vietnam

Vietnam adopted Chinese writing and architectural styles but had a **more adversarial** relationship with China, including violent rebellions. Key differences from China:
- Vietnamese women enjoyed **greater independence** in married life
- Vietnamese preferred **nuclear families** (not extended families)
- Villages operated **independently** of a national government
- Scholar-officials in Vietnam owed allegiance to **village peasants** more than to the emperor

Vietnamese resistance to foot binding and polygyny reflects how sinification was negotiated, not simply absorbed.
