# Developments in East Asia — Overview
**Topic 1.1 | Unit 1: The Global Tapestry | Period 1 (c. 1200–c. 1450)**
*AMSCO pp. 1–14*

## Essential Question

How did developments in China and the rest of East Asia between c. 1200 and c. 1450 reflect **continuity, innovation, and diversity**?

---

## The Song Dynasty (960–1279)

The Song Dynasty is the centerpiece of Topic 1.1. Though it ruled a smaller territory than the Tang, it was arguably the most commercially and intellectually advanced civilization of its era.

### Government: Bureaucracy and Meritocracy

China's **imperial bureaucracy** had existed since the Qin Dynasty (221 BCE), giving the Song a foundation of political continuity. [[Unit-1.1/People, States & Movements|Song Taizu]], the dynasty's founder, expanded both the bureaucracy and access to the [[Concept Notes/Civil Service Examination|civil service exam]], which was based on mastery of **Confucian** texts. Young men from lower economic classes could theoretically rise by scoring well — making China's system a **meritocracy**. This allowed more upward mobility than any comparable system of the time.

> [!warning]
> The civil service exam did NOT create equality. The poor were underrepresented, and by the late Song the bureaucracy had grown so expensive that it drained the empire's surplus wealth — contributing to its eventual decline.

The government also used the **tributary system**: neighboring states (Japan, Korea, kingdoms in Southeast Asia) paid money or goods to honor the Chinese emperor and performed the **kowtow** ritual. This cemented China's dominance while stimulating regional trade.

### Economy: Commercialization and Innovation

The Song oversaw a dramatic commercial revolution, building on Tang-era infrastructure (roads, canals, trade networks).

- **Grand Canal** — Over 30,000 miles of internal waterway; made China the most populous trading area in the world.
- **Champa rice** — A fast-ripening, drought-resistant strain from the Champa kingdom (Vietnam); adopted before the 11th century; allowed double-cropping and population growth. China's population rose from ~25% to ~40% of world total during the Song.
- **Gunpowder and guns** — Invented in earlier dynasties; Song innovators developed the first guns; the technology spread westward via the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]].
- **Compass and navigation charts** — Printed paper navigation charts enabled open-ocean sailing.
- **Proto-industrialization** — Rural and community-based production (steel, porcelain, silk) beyond local consumption needs — earlier than Western Europe.
- **Key exports**: porcelain, silk, tea.

China became the most commercialized society in the world, with its economy shifting from local consumption to **market production**.

### Society: Scholar-Gentry and Foot Binding

The expansion of the bureaucracy created a new and dominant social class: the **scholar gentry**. Below them ranked farmers, artisans, and merchants (the last held lowest prestige in Confucian thought, as they created nothing new). At the top, the scholar gentry soon outnumbered the old hereditary aristocracy.

**Women and foot binding**: **Confucianism** reinforced patriarchal norms. During the Song, **foot binding** became common among aristocratic families. Girls' feet were tightly wrapped to stunt growth, signaling status — and severely limiting mobility. Foot binding was finally banned in 1912.

**Filial piety** — the duty to subordinate personal desires to the male head of household and to the ruler — reinforced Song political stability.

### Religion and Intellectual Life

Buddhism spread to China from India via the Silk Roads and became widespread during the Tang. Three main branches shaped East Asia:

| Branch | Emphasis | Strongest In |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| [[Concept Notes/Buddhism|Theravada Buddhism]] | Personal spiritual growth, meditation | Southeast Asia |
| [[Concept Notes/Buddhism|Mahayana Buddhism]] | Spiritual growth for all beings, service | China, Korea |
| [[Concept Notes/Buddhism|Tibetan Buddhism]] | Chanting | Tibet |

The Tang briefly suppressed Buddhism (monasteries closed, land seized), but it remained popular. During the Song, Confucian scholars adopted Buddhist ideals, producing **[[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]]** (c. 960–1280) — a syncretic fusion of Confucian ethics with Daoist and Buddhist ideas. Neo-Confucianism became the dominant intellectual tradition in China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.

**Woodblock printing** (invented in the 9th century) expanded literacy among privileged classes and spread agricultural knowledge and Buddhist scripture widely.

## Dynastic Change: Yuan and Ming

The Song Dynasty fell in **1279** when **[[Unit-1.1/People, States & Movements|Kublai Khan]]**'s Mongols completed their conquest of China, establishing the **Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368)**. The Mongol period is explored more deeply in [[Topic 2.2 — The Mongol Empire|Topic 2.2]]; for 1.1, the key point is that the Yuan represented a break in Chinese governance — ruled by foreign conquerors who nonetheless adopted many Chinese administrative practices.

In **1368**, **[[Unit-1.1/People, States & Movements|Zhu Yuanzhang]] (the Hongwu Emperor)** led a peasant revolt that expelled the Mongols and founded the **Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)**. The Ming restored native Chinese rule and Confucian governance structures. They also sponsored [[Zheng He]]'s famous naval expeditions (covered in Topic 2.3).

## East Asian Neighbors: Japan, Korea, Vietnam

A crucial dynamic of this period is **sinification** — the adoption of Chinese cultural, political, and intellectual practices by neighboring societies. Each country had to navigate how much to absorb and how much to resist.

### Japan

Japan's separation by sea gave it more control over Chinese influence than Korea or Vietnam had. Chinese cultural imports included Buddhism, Confucianism, and woodblock printing. The **Heian period (794–1185)** saw Japanese court culture heavily emulate Chinese art and literature — but also produce original works, most famously *The Tale of Genji* (c. 11th c.) by **[[Unit-1.1/People, States & Movements|Lady Murasaki Shikibu]]**, considered the world's first novel.

Japan developed a **feudal system** structurally similar to Europe's, with **daimyo** (landowning lords), **samurai** (warriors), and peasant serfs. Key differences:
- Japanese daimyo wielded *more* power relative to the emperor than European nobles wielded relative to monarchs.
- The Japanese warrior code (**bushido**) emphasized frugality, loyalty, martial arts, and honor unto death; Europe's **chivalry** emphasized duty to God and to women.

In **1192**, the **Minamoto clan** installed **[[Unit-1.1/People, States & Movements|Minamoto Yoritomo]]** as **shogun** (military ruler), establishing the **Kamakura Shogunate**. Japan retained an emperor, but real power rested with the shogun for centuries.

### Korea

Korea shared a land boundary with China and maintained a close **tributary relationship**. It adopted Confucian and Buddhist beliefs, the Chinese writing system (which was structurally awkward for the Korean language — a new system was developed in the 15th century), and a civil service exam — but the exam was *not* open to peasants. The Korean landed aristocracy was more powerful than China's, limiting meritocratic reforms.

### Vietnam

Vietnam adopted Chinese writing and architecture but had the most adversarial relationship with China of the three. Vietnamese culture differed in important ways: women enjoyed greater independence, nuclear families were preferred over extended families, and scholar-officials owed loyalty to village peasants rather than the emperor. Vietnamese women rejected foot binding and polygyny. Vietnamese guerrilla fighters successfully pushed out China's occupying Tang army in the 8th century.

## Big Picture: Continuity, Innovation, and Diversity

| Theme | Continuity | Innovation / Change |
|-------|-----------|---------------------|
| **GOV** | Bureaucracy from Qin/Han; Mandate of Heaven | Civil service exam expanded; Yuan foreign rule; Ming restoration |
| **ECN** | Grand Canal; Silk Roads trade | Champa rice, proto-industrialization, compass, guns, commercialization |
| **CDI** | Buddhism spreads from India | Neo-Confucianism synthesizes Confucian + Daoist + Buddhist ideas |
| **SIO** | Patriarchal Confucian norms; filial piety | Scholar-gentry displaces hereditary aristocracy; foot binding spreads |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the Song Dynasty's bureaucratic meritocracy, the spread of Neo-Confucianism, and comparisons between Chinese feudalism (the scholar-gentry) and Japanese feudalism (shogunate/samurai). Know specific examples of sinification AND resistance to sinification in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.
