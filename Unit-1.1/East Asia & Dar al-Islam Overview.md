# Topic 1.1 — The Global Tapestry: Developments in East Asia & Dar al-Islam

## What This Topic Is About

Topic 1.1 sets the stage for *all* of AP World History Modern. Before diving into the details, you need to understand the **world as it looked around 1200 CE** — who the major players were, what drove their power, and how they connected to each other.

The AP exam labels 1200–1450 as **Period 1**, and the unit theme is "The Global Tapestry." That phrase is deliberate: this era's story is about threads of religion, trade, governance, and culture weaving together across the world's major civilizations — often without any awareness of each other.

Topic 1.1 specifically covers two major regions:

1. **East Asia** — centered on the [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]] in China, with comparisons to Japan, Korea, and Vietnam
2. **Dar al-Islam** — the Islamic world stretching from Spain to Central Asia, centered on the declining [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Abbasid Caliphate]]

---

## The Big Picture Context (c. 1200–c. 1450)

> [!tip]
> The AP exam loves questions about *why* civilizations succeeded or declined. For Period 1, the answer almost always involves some combination of **trade networks**, **religion as a unifying force**, and **bureaucratic governance**.

Between 1200 and 1450, several dynamics shaped the world:

**The revival of large empires.** The [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]] was the wealthiest and most innovative empire on earth. In the Islamic world, the Abbasid Caliphate had created a vast zone of shared law, scholarship, and commerce — though it was now under pressure. In Mesoamerica, the [[Unit-1.5/Aztec Empire|Aztec Empire]] was rising, influenced by the earlier Mayans.

**New states from trade.** In regions without a classical imperial tradition, trade itself generated complexity. Trans-Saharan commerce produced the West African empires of [[Unit-1.5/Mali Empire|Ghana and Mali]]. Indian Ocean trade connected [[Unit-1.5/East African City-States|Zimbabwe]] and states across Southeast Asia.

**The Mongol wildcard.** Looming over everything was the rise of the [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire|Mongol Empire]]. By the mid-13th century, Mongol armies would reshape Eurasia — destroying Baghdad in 1258, ending the Song Dynasty, and simultaneously creating the conditions for unprecedented cross-continental trade. The Mongols are the hinge between Periods 1 and 2.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to explain:

- How the **Song Dynasty** governed China and what made its economy exceptional
- How **Neo-Confucianism** and **Buddhism** shaped Chinese society and spread to neighboring states
- How **Japan, Korea, and Vietnam** each responded to Chinese cultural influence ([[sinification]])
- How the **Abbasid Caliphate** functioned and why it declined
- How the **Islamic Golden Age** produced intellectual achievements that shaped later European thought
- How **women's roles** differed across Chinese and Islamic societies
- How **al-Andalus** (Islamic Spain) became a center of cross-cultural scholarship

## How This Topic Connects Forward

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to trace how developments in this period *caused* or *enabled* later developments. Know these forward links cold.

- Song technology (gunpowder, compass, paper) → spread via [[Unit-2.1/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]] → enables European exploration after 1450
- Islamic scholarship in Baghdad and al-Andalus → lays groundwork for the [[Unit-3.3/Renaissance|Renaissance]] and [[Unit-4.6/Scientific Revolution|Scientific Revolution]]
- Neo-Confucianism in East Asia → continues shaping governance in [[Unit-3.1/Ming Dynasty|Ming China]], Korea, Vietnam, and Japan into Period 2
- Abbasid decline + Mongol conquest → opens Eurasia to the [[Unit-2.2/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]] and intensified trade in Topic 2.1–2.3
- Tributary system under Song → continues and expands under [[Unit-3.1/Ming Dynasty|Ming Dynasty]]
