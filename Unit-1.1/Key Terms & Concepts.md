# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.1: Developments in East Asia
*AMSCO pp. 1–14*

---

## Government & Political Structures

**Imperial bureaucracy**
A vast organization of appointed officials who carry out the emperor's policies. Present in China since the Qin Dynasty (221 BCE); expanded significantly under the Song. Represents political *continuity* across dynasties — but the Song expansion eventually became so costly it weakened the empire.

**Civil service exam (keju)**
Examinations based on knowledge of Confucian texts; used to select officials for the bureaucracy. Expanded by [[Unit-1.1/People, States & Movements|Song Taizu]] to include young men of lower economic classes. Produced a *meritocracy* — advancement by demonstrated ability rather than birth.

**Meritocracy**
A system in which positions are earned through merit (skill, knowledge, performance). China's civil service exam made its bureaucracy a meritocracy, allowing limited upward mobility — more than any other system of the time.

**Tributary system**
An arrangement in which foreign states pay money or provide goods to honor the Chinese emperor. Tributary states sent representatives to perform the **kowtow**. Cemented Chinese political dominance while stimulating trade. Japan, Korea, and many Southeast Asian kingdoms were tributary states by the Song era.

**Kowtow**
A ritual of deep respect: bowing until one's head touched the floor. Required of representatives from tributary states when meeting the Chinese emperor.

**Mandate of Heaven**
The belief that the emperor rules with divine approval; if a dynasty falls, Heaven has withdrawn its mandate. Provided legitimacy for dynastic succession and could be used to justify rebellion.

**Shogun**
A military ruler in Japan. In 1192, the Minamoto clan installed the first shogun, reducing the emperor to a figurehead. Real political power rested with the shogun.

**Daimyo**
Powerful landowning aristocrats in Japan. In the Japanese feudal system, daimyo commanded samurai and ruled vast territories — wielding more effective power than the emperor or shogun.

**Sinification**
The process by which a non-Chinese culture adopts Chinese customs, language, political structures, and traditions. Japan, Korea, and Vietnam all experienced sinification to varying degrees.

## Economy & Trade

**Grand Canal**
An internal waterway transportation system extending over 30,000 miles. Connected northern and southern China; made Song China the most populous trading area in the world. A key piece of infrastructure linking agricultural production to markets.

**Champa rice**
A fast-ripening, drought-resistant strain of rice originating in the Champa kingdom (present-day Vietnam). Adopted in China before the 11th century; allowed farming in previously unsuitable areas and enabled double-cropping. Caused rapid population growth during the Song era — China's share of world population rose from ~25% to ~40%.

**Proto-industrialization**
An early form of economic development in which people in rural areas produce more goods than local consumption requires. Song-era China experienced proto-industrialization in steel, silk, and porcelain production — earlier than Western Europe. Relied on dispersed home/community-based production rather than factory machinery.

**Commercialization**
The shift from a subsistence or locally focused economy to one oriented around market production and exchange. Song China became the world's most commercialized society, exporting porcelain, silk, and tea.

## Society & Social Structure

**Scholar gentry**
A new social class created by the expansion of the bureaucracy. Educated in Confucian philosophy; grew to outnumber the old hereditary aristocracy. Became the most influential social class in Song China. Below them: farmers, artisans, merchants.

**Filial piety**
The Confucian duty of family members to subordinate their desires to the male head of the family — and, by extension, to the ruler. Reinforced both family hierarchy and political obedience to the emperor.

**Foot binding**
The practice, common among aristocratic Chinese families during the Song Dynasty, of tightly wrapping girls' feet to prevent natural bone growth. A bound foot signaled social status and desirability — but severely restricted women's mobility and participation in public life. Finally banned in 1912.

**Bushido**
The Japanese warrior code followed by samurai. Emphasized frugality, loyalty, martial arts, and honor unto death. Contrast with European chivalry (duty to God and to women).

## Religion & Philosophy

**Neo-Confucianism**
A syncretic intellectual system developed in China between c. 960 and 1280. Combined Confucian rational ethics with the more abstract spiritual ideas of Daoism and Buddhism. Emphasized ethics over theology. Became the dominant intellectual tradition in China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Immensely important for the AP exam.

**Buddhism (three branches)**

| Branch | Core Emphasis | Strongest Region |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|
| Theravada | Personal spiritual growth; silent meditation; self-discipline | Southeast Asia |
| Mahayana | Spiritual growth for all beings; service to others | China, Korea |
| Tibetan | Chanting; ritual practice | Tibet |

**Chan (Zen) Buddhism**
A syncretic form of Buddhism that fused Buddhist ideas with Daoist principles. Emphasized direct experience and meditation over formal scripture study. Very popular in China despite Tang-era suppression of monasteries.

**Sincretic**
Combining elements of different religious or philosophical traditions into a new, fused system. Both Neo-Confucianism and Chan Buddhism are examples of syncretism.

## Technology & Innovation

**Woodblock printing**
A printing technique invented in China (9th century). Multiple copies of text or images could be produced by pressing paper against a carved wooden block. Expanded literacy and the availability of books; spread agricultural manuals, Buddhist scripture, and navigation charts.

**Compass**
Used by the Chinese in maritime navigation. Combined with printed navigation charts, it allowed sailors to travel in open water out of sight of land — expanding China's reach in the South China Sea.

**Gunpowder / guns**
Gunpowder invented in earlier Chinese dynasties; Song innovators made the first guns. Technology spread westward via the Silk Roads over centuries, eventually reaching all of Eurasia.
