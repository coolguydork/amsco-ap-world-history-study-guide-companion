## East Asia: China

### Government & Political Systems

**Imperial bureaucracy**
A vast organization of appointed officials who carry out the empire's policies. Existed in China since the [[Qin Dynasty]] (221–207 BCE) — making it a profound *continuity* across dynasties. Under the Song, it expanded significantly but eventually became a financial burden. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Meritocracy**
A system in which positions are awarded based on demonstrated ability (merit) rather than birthright. China's civil service exam system was the world's most prominent example in this period. Imperfect (poor were underrepresented) but more open than any contemporary alternative. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Song Dynasty (960–1279)**
The wealthiest and most innovative empire in the world during Period 1. Replaced the Tang Dynasty; lost northern lands to the Jin Empire; eventually conquered by the Mongols in 1279. Known for bureaucratic expansion, economic dynamism, and cultural achievement. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Tributary system**
An arrangement in which foreign states pay money or goods to honor the Chinese emperor, who in turn provides trade access and recognition of their legitimacy. Japan, Korea, and Southeast Asian kingdoms were tributary states. Involved the ritual **kowtow** (bowing until forehead touched floor). Demonstrated and reinforced China's political and economic dominance. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Kowtow**
A ritual in which those greeting the Chinese emperor bow until their head touches the floor. Required of tributary state representatives. Symbolic of Chinese hierarchical supremacy.

**Heian period (794–1185)**
The era in Japanese history during which Japan most consciously emulated Chinese traditions in politics, art, and literature. Also the period in which Japanese writers created distinctive works like *The Tale of Genji*.

### Economics

**Champa rice**
A fast-ripening, drought-resistant strain of rice from the Champa Kingdom (present-day Vietnam). Introduced to China before the 11th century, it dramatically expanded agricultural production by enabling two crops per year and farming in previously unsuitable terrain. Key driver of Song population growth. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Proto-industrialization**
Economic changes in which people in rural areas produce more goods than they can sell locally. Characteristic of Song China. Relied on home-based/community-based production using simple equipment — a precursor to, but distinct from, the Industrial Revolution of the 18th century. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Artisans**
Skilled craftworkers who produced goods like steel, porcelain, and silk. Ranked third in the Confucian social hierarchy (below farmers, above merchants). Their dispersed production facilities under government supervision exemplified proto-industrialization.

**Grand Canal**
An internal waterway transportation system extending over 30,000 miles. Provided cheap, efficient movement of goods within China. The economic spine connecting northern and southern China; enabled the Song to become the world's most populous trading area. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

### Society

**Scholar gentry**
A new social class created by the expansion of the civil service exam system. Educated in Confucian philosophy; earned their status through demonstrated ability. Eventually outnumbered the aristocracy and became the most influential social class in Song China. A key *change* from earlier dynasties dominated by hereditary aristocrats.

**Filial piety**
The Confucian duty of family members to subordinate their own desires to those of the male head of the family, and to the ruler. Reinforced the hierarchical social order and supported dynastic stability. Extended from family to political loyalty.

**Foot binding**
The practice of tightly wrapping girls' feet from a young age so bones couldn't grow naturally. Became common among aristocratic families during the Song Dynasty. Signified high social status; also physically restricted women's mobility and participation in public life. Banned in 1912. A *continuity* in patriarchal social structure; contrasts sharply with Vietnamese women's greater freedom. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

### Technology

**Woodblock printing**
A printing technique invented in China (7th century). A Buddhist scripture from the 7th century is the world's first known woodblock-printed work. Expanded access to literature and educational materials. Spread agricultural knowledge through printed booklets. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Gunpowder**
Invented in China in previous dynasties; Song innovators made the *first guns*. Technology spread via [[Unit-2.1/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]] across Eurasia. Eventually transformed warfare globally, including enabling European military dominance in later periods.

### Religion & Philosophy

**Buddhism** (general)
A religion originating in India; spread to China via the Silk Roads and gained widespread popularity during the Tang Dynasty. Three major forms developed in East Asia. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Theravada Buddhism**
"Way of the Elders." Focused on **personal spiritual growth** through silent meditation and self-discipline. Became strongest in Southeast Asia.

**Mahayana Buddhism**
"Greater Vehicle." Focused on **spiritual growth for all beings** and on service/community. Became strongest in China and Korea.

**Tibetan Buddhism**
Focused on **chanting** and ritual. Became strongest in Tibet.

**Chan (Zen) Buddhism**
A **syncretic** form combining Buddhist doctrines with elements of Daoism. Emphasized direct experience and meditation over studying scripture. Very popular among ordinary Chinese; parallels Zen Buddhism in Japan. Example of *religious syncretism* — two traditions fusing into something new.

**Neo-Confucianism**
Evolved in China between 770 and 840. A syncretic system combining rational Confucian thought with the more abstract spiritual ideas of Daoism and Buddhism. Emphasized **ethics** rather than metaphysics. Became immensely popular in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Key example of intellectual change through cultural synthesis. → [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty|Song Dynasty]]

**Syncretic**
Describes a belief system or cultural practice that fuses elements from multiple traditions. Neo-Confucianism and Chan Buddhism are both syncretic. The term appears constantly in AP World History and signals *cultural blending*.

### East Asia Comparisons

**Sinification**
The assimilation of Chinese cultural traditions and practices by neighboring peoples. Japan, Korea, and Vietnam all underwent sinification to varying degrees. Each balanced adoption with preservation of local identity. → [[Unit-1.1/Japan Korea Vietnam|Japan, Korea & Vietnam]]

**Feudalism (Japanese)**
A decentralized political system in Japan characterized by landowning aristocrats (daimyo) competing for territory, with most people working as rice farmers. Formalized with the shogunate in 1192. Parallels European feudalism but with key differences (daimyo more powerful than nobles relative to monarch). → [[Unit-1.1/Japan Korea Vietnam|Japan, Korea & Vietnam]]

**Daimyo**
Japanese landowning aristocrats who competed for territorial control. In the feudal hierarchy, they were effectively more powerful than the shogun or emperor in practice.

**Shogun**
Military ruler of Japan, installed by the Minamoto clan in 1192. Though the emperor continued to exist ceremonially, real power rested with the shogun. Led to centuries of regional rivalry rather than centralized rule.

**Bushido**
The Japanese samurai warrior code, stressing frugality, loyalty, martial arts, and honor unto death. Parallel to European chivalry, but with a much harsher, more austere emphasis.

**Nuclear families**
In Vietnam, the preference for households consisting only of parents and their children — in contrast to the Chinese extended family system. Reflects Vietnamese cultural independence from Chinese norms.

**Polygyny**
The practice of having more than one wife simultaneously. Practiced among Chinese upper classes; explicitly rejected in Vietnamese cultural tradition as part of resistance to Chinese norms.

## Dar al-Islam

### Government & Political Systems

**Abbasid Caliphate**
The Islamic empire centered on Baghdad; the dominant political and intellectual force in the Islamic world. Entered decline in the 1100s–1200s due to pressure from Mamluks, Seljuks, Crusaders, and Mongols. Destroyed by Mongols in 1258. Represented the height of the Islamic Golden Age. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517)**
An Egyptian state founded by enslaved soldiers (Mamluks) of Turkic origin who seized power. Prospered through trade in cotton and sugar. Stopped the Mongol advance in Egypt. Declined when Portuguese sea routes bypassed their trade networks. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Seljuk Turks**
Central Asian Muslim warriors who conquered parts of the Middle East from the 11th century. Their leader took the title **sultan**, reducing the Abbasid caliph to a purely religious role. Their restriction of Christian pilgrimage to Jerusalem triggered the Crusades. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Sultan**
A political ruler in the Islamic world, distinct from the religious authority of the caliph. Seljuk Turks popularized this title as they took real power from Abbasid caliphs.

**Crusaders**
European Christian soldiers who organized military campaigns (the Crusades) to reopen access to holy sites in Jerusalem, which the Seljuk Turks had restricted. Put the Islamic world in direct military and cultural contact with Latin Christianity. → [[Unit-1.6/Christian Crusades|Christian Crusades]]

**Shariah**
Islamic law derived from the Quran and the teachings of Muhammad. Provided a consistent legal framework across the politically fragmented Islamic world — one of the key forces of cultural unity in Dar al-Islam. Regulated personal conduct, commercial transactions, family law, and criminal justice.

### Culture & Religion

**House of Wisdom**
A renowned center of learning in Baghdad under the Abbasid Caliphate. Scholars from across the Islamic world and beyond traveled there to study. Central to the Islamic Golden Age; destroyed by the Mongols in 1258. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Muhammad**
The founder and prophet of Islam. His teachings and life set key precedents: pro-merchant culture (he was himself a trader), elevated status of women relative to pre-Islamic Arabia, encouragement of knowledge-seeking across cultures. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Sufis**
Islamic mystics who emphasized **introspection and direct spiritual experience** of God over intellectual study of the Quran. Effective missionaries who adapted to local cultures, enabling Islam's spread across diverse regions. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274)**
Celebrated Islamic scholar; contributed to astronomy, law, mathematics, and medicine. His observatory produced the world's most accurate astronomical charts of the era. Helped establish trigonometry as a discipline. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)**
Islamic historian and social thinker. Acknowledged founder of **historiography** and **sociology**. Analyzed how group solidarity (*asabiyyah*) drives civilizations' rise and fall. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**'A'ishah al-Ba'uniyyah (1460–1507)**
Sufi poet and mystic; most prolific female Muslim writer before the 20th century. Her poetry reflects the Sufi path toward mystical illumination and demonstrates the breadth of Islamic women's intellectual life. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Hijab**
A term referring either to the practice of dressing modestly or to a specific type of head covering. The practice predated Islam in Central Asia and the Byzantine Empire, but was formalized under Islamic law. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

### Al-Andalus / Islamic Spain

**Al-Andalus**
The Islamic state in Spain, ruled by the Umayyad dynasty for centuries after the 711 conquest. Centered on **Córdoba**, which had the largest library in the world at its peak. A model of relatively tolerant coexistence among Muslims, Christians, and Jews. A crucial conduit for knowledge transfer from the Islamic world to Christian Europe. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Battle of Tours (732)**
A military defeat of Islamic forces by Frankish armies. Marked the limit of rapid Islamic expansion into Western Europe. Most of the continent remained Christian, while Muslims ruled Spain for the next seven centuries. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Ibn Rushd / Averroes (12th century)**
Islamic scholar from al-Andalus. Wrote influential commentaries on Aristotle that were transmitted to Jewish and Christian philosophers, triggering a chain of intellectual synthesis. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Maimonides (c. 1135–c. 1204)**
Jewish philosopher from al-Andalus, influenced by Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle. Developed a synthesis of Aristotelian reasoning and biblical interpretation that subsequently influenced Christian thought. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)**
Christian philosopher influenced by Maimonides and the Islamic transmission of Aristotle. His synthesis of reason and faith helped lay groundwork for the Renaissance and Scholasticism in Europe. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]

**Dhow**
A type of ship with a long, thin hull, first developed in India or China. Used extensively in Indian Ocean and Mediterranean trade. Excellent for carrying goods; less suited for warfare. Symbol of the commercial connectivity of Dar al-Islam. → [[Unit-1.1/Dar al-Islam|Dar al-Islam]]
