# Topic 1.1 — Key Terms, Concepts & People

> [!tip]
> For AP essays, you need to be able to **define** these terms, **explain their significance**, and **use them as evidence**. Don't just memorize definitions — know *why each one mattered*.

---

## Core Vocabulary

**Song Dynasty (960–1279)**
The dominant Chinese dynasty in Period 1. Known for bureaucratic expansion, economic commercialization, technological innovation, and proto-industrialization. Lost northern lands to the Jin Empire but remained prosperous. Fell to the [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]] in 1279.

**Imperial bureaucracy**
A vast organization of appointed officials carrying out imperial policies. China's bureaucracy dated to the Qin Dynasty (221 BCE) — one of the great **continuities** in Chinese history. Expanded under the Song; became a financial burden by the dynasty's end.

**Meritocracy**
A system where individuals earn positions through demonstrated ability (exams) rather than birth. The Song civil service exam system was the closest thing to a true meritocracy in the premodern world — though still heavily skewed toward the educated and wealthy.

**Civil service exam**
The test Chinese men took to earn government positions, based on knowledge of Confucian texts. Under Emperor Song Taizu, access was expanded to lower-income men, enabling some upward mobility.

**Scholar gentry**
The social class created by bureaucratic expansion — educated Confucian men who staffed the government. More influential than the hereditary aristocracy.

**Champa rice**
Fast-ripening, drought-resistant rice from the Champa Kingdom (present-day Vietnam). Transformed Chinese agriculture before the 11th century by enabling double-cropping and farming in new regions. Key driver of population growth.

**Proto-industrialization**
Rural, home-based or community-based production of goods beyond local needs, using simple equipment. China experienced this *before* Western Europe. Products included steel, porcelain, and silk.

**Grand Canal**
China's internal waterway network (30,000+ miles), enabling cheap mass transport of goods. A key infrastructure achievement supporting China's commercialization.

**Tributary system**
An arrangement where neighboring states (Japan, Korea, Vietnam, SE Asian kingdoms) paid tribute — goods, money, or ritual submission (the kowtow) — to the Chinese emperor. Reinforced Chinese dominance while stimulating trade.

**Kowtow**
The ritual bow (head to the floor) required of tributary-state representatives when meeting the Chinese emperor. A symbol of submission and Chinese cultural superiority.

**Foot binding**
The practice of tightly wrapping girls' feet to prevent natural bone growth, common among Song Dynasty aristocratic families. Signaled social status but severely restricted women's mobility and public participation. A key example of intensifying patriarchy in Song China.

**Filial piety**
The Confucian duty of family members to subordinate their desires to the male head of the family — and by extension, to the ruler. The Song Dynasty relied on this to maintain political order.

**Neo-Confucianism**
A synthesis of rational Confucian ethics with Daoist and Buddhist ideas, emerging 770–840 CE. Became the dominant intellectual and governmental philosophy in China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Emphasized ethics, social hierarchy, and loyalty.

**Chan (Zen) Buddhism**
A syncretic Chinese faith blending Mahayana Buddhism with Daoism. Emphasized direct experience and meditation over formal learning. Became popular among ordinary Chinese people.

**Sinification**
The process by which neighboring cultures adopt Chinese practices, language, writing, religion, and governance structures. Japan, Korea, and Vietnam all navigated sinification differently.

**Woodblock printing**
Chinese invention (7th century) allowing multiple copies of texts to be made cheaply. A key driver of literacy, scholarship, and the spread of Confucian and Buddhist ideas.

**Shogun**
Military ruler of Japan. After the decline of the Heian court, the Minamoto clan installed a shogun in 1192. Japan retained an emperor, but the shogun held real power.

**Daimyo**
Japan's landowning aristocrats. In the feudal system, they were more powerful than the emperor or shogun in practice. Comparable to European nobles/lords.

**Samurai**
Japan's warrior class, bound by the code of **bushido** (frugality, loyalty, martial arts, honor unto death). Comparable to European knights.

**Bushido**
The Japanese warrior code: frugality, loyalty, martial arts mastery, and honor unto death. Compare to European **chivalry** (honor, courtesy, duty to God, courtly love).

## Key People

**Emperor Song Taizu**
Founder of the Song Dynasty. Expanded educational access so lower-income men could take the civil service exam, deepening the meritocratic system.

**Xuanzang (629–645 CE pilgrimage)**
A Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled to India on the Silk Roads, spent 17 years studying Buddhism, and returned with texts he spent his life translating. His journey helped build Buddhism's popularity in China. *(Note: predates our period, but provides context for Buddhist spread.)*

**Zheng He**
Chinese admiral who led massive fleets into the Indian Ocean to demonstrate imperial power and collect tribute. (Covered more in [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Trade]].)

**Prince Shotoku Taishi (574–622)**
Japanese prince who promoted Buddhism and Confucianism alongside Shinto. Represents early sinification in Japan.

**Minamoto clan**
The Japanese aristocratic family that seized power after the Heian court's decline. In 1192, they installed Japan's first shogun, beginning Japan's military-rule era.
