# Dar al-Islam: The Islamic World, c. 1200–1450

## What Is Dar al-Islam?

"Dar al-Islam" means "house of submission" or "house of Islam" — it refers to the entire **zone of Islamic civilization** stretching from Spain in the west to Central Asia and India in the east. By 1200, this zone was not a single empire but a **culturally unified region** held together by shared religion ([[Islam]]), shared law ([[shariah]]), shared language (Arabic for scholarship), and interconnected trade networks.

At the center of this world stood **Baghdad** and the **[[Abbasid Caliphate]]** — though by 1200, both were in decline.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests students on **what held Dar al-Islam together** even as it fragmented politically. The answer: shared religion, shariah law, Arabic as a scholarly language, and trade networks. Political unity was never the binding force.

## The Abbasid Caliphate: Context and Decline

### The Abbasid Golden Age

Under the Abbasids, Baghdad became the intellectual capital of the world. Their centerpiece was the **House of Wisdom** — a renowned institution where scholars traveled from across the Islamic world and beyond to study. The Abbasids embodied Muhammad's famous teaching: *"Go in quest of knowledge even unto China."*

Abbasid scholars made major contributions by:
- **Translating Greek classics into Arabic** — saving the works of Aristotle and other Greek thinkers that would have otherwise been lost to Western Europe
- **Studying Indian mathematics** and transmitting the knowledge of numerals (0–9, the "Arabic numerals") to Europeans
- **Adopting Chinese paper-making techniques** and teaching Europeans how to make paper
- Making original advances in astronomy, mathematics, medicine, philosophy, and law

This intellectual activity was not just cultural prestige — it was an **economic engine** that attracted scholars, merchants, and knowledge from across Afro-Eurasia.

### The Abbasid Caliphate Under Pressure

By the 1100s and 1200s, the Abbasid Empire faced challenges from four directions:

**1. Egyptian Mamluks**
Arabs often purchased enslaved soldiers — called **Mamluks** — frequently ethnic Turks from Central Asia, to serve as soldiers and bureaucrats. In Egypt, these Mamluks seized power and established the **Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517)**. They prospered by controlling trade in cotton and sugar between the Islamic world and Europe — until Portuguese sea routes bypassed them in the late 15th century. Critically, the Mamluks stopped the Mongol advance in Egypt, one of the few Mongol defeats.

**2. Seljuk Turks**
From the 11th century onward, the **Seljuk Turks** — Central Asian Muslims — conquered parts of the Middle East, extending power nearly to Western China. Their leader called himself **sultan**, reducing the highest Abbasid official from caliph to merely the chief Sunni religious authority — a symbolic but significant demotion.

**3. Crusaders**
The Abbasids had allowed Christian pilgrims easy access to holy sites in and around Jerusalem. When the Seljuk Turks restricted this access, European Christians organized military campaigns called **[[Crusades]]** to reopen the routes. (See Topic 1.6.) This drew Islamic and European civilizations into prolonged contact — and conflict.

**4. The Mongols (the decisive blow)**
In **1258**, the [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire|Mongols]] conquered Baghdad and ended the Abbasid Caliphate. This was one of the most catastrophic events in Islamic history — the House of Wisdom was destroyed, the Caliph was executed, and the city was devastated. The Mongols pushed westward but were stopped in Egypt by the Mamluks.

### Economic Decline

The Abbasids had been the key **commercial intermediary** linking Asia, Europe, and North Africa. Goods and ideas flowed through Baghdad. But as trade routes shifted northward, Baghdad lost its central position. Revenue declined, canals fell into disrepair, agriculture suffered, and the city's infrastructure crumbled. This is a classic AP **causation** chain: trade-route shift → revenue loss → infrastructure decay → political weakness → vulnerability to invasion.

## Cultural and Social Life in the Islamic World

### Political Fragmentation, Cultural Unity

Even as the Abbasid Caliphate fragmented, the Islamic world maintained cultural coherence. The successor states were often led by **Turkic peoples** from Central Asia:
- **Mamluk Sultanate** — North Africa / Egypt
- **Seljuk Turks** — Middle East
- **Delhi Sultanate** — South Asia

By the 16th century, three major Islamic empires would emerge from Turkic roots: the **[[Unit-3.1/Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Empire]]**, the **[[Unit-3.1/Safavid Empire|Safavid Empire]]**, and the **[[Unit-3.1/Mughal Empire|Mughal Empire]]** (see Topic 3.1).

Across all these states, cultural continuity was maintained through:
- **Shariah** — shared legal framework
- **Great universities** in Baghdad, Córdoba, Cairo, and Bukhara
- **Arabic** as the language of scholarship and commerce
- Trade networks that spread goods and ideas

### Islamic Golden Age Scholars

**Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274)** — perhaps the most celebrated Islamic scholar of the period. He contributed to astronomy, law, logic, ethics, mathematics, philosophy, and medicine. His observatory produced the most accurate astronomical charts in the world at the time. His work on triangles laid the groundwork for trigonometry as a distinct discipline. (AMSCO p. 16)

**Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)** — widely acknowledged as a founder of **historiography** (the study of historians' methods) and **sociology**. He analyzed how civilizations rise and fall based on group solidarity (*asabiyyah*), making him centuries ahead of his time.

**'A'ishah al-Ba'uniyyah (1460–1507)** — possibly the most prolific female Muslim writer before the 20th century. A **Sufi** mystic and poet whose work "Clear Inspiration, on Praise of the Trusted One" demonstrates deep learning and the Sufi path toward mystical illumination.

### Sufism

**Sufis** differed from mainstream Muslims in their approach to faith. Most Muslims focused on intellectual engagement with the Quran; Sufis emphasized **introspection and direct mystical experience** of God. Sufism may have originated as a reaction against the perceived materialism of early Umayyad rulers.

Crucially, **Sufi missionaries were the most effective spreaders of Islam** across Africa, Central Asia, and South Asia. Their willingness to adapt to local cultures and incorporate local religious elements won converts that more rigid approaches couldn't reach. This made Sufism a key mechanism of Islamic expansion.

### Commerce and Merchants

Islamic society held merchants in **high esteem** — a sharp contrast to Confucian China, where merchants ranked near the bottom of the social hierarchy. This is explained by Islam's founding story: Muhammad himself was a merchant, as was his first wife Khadijah. As long as they conducted fair dealings and gave to charity (one of the Five Pillars), merchants could grow wealthy and respected.

This pro-merchant culture powered Islamic trade across the Indian Ocean and Central Asia, driving much of the period's global commerce.

### Slavery in the Islamic World

Islam allowed slavery but with significant constraints:
- Muslims could **not** enslave other Muslims
- Jews, Christians, and Zoroastrians were also exempt
- Enslaved people came mainly from sub-Saharan Africa, Kievan Rus, and Central Asia
- **Hereditary slavery** (children automatically enslaved) had not developed
- Many enslaved people converted to Islam and were freed by their owners

Enslaved women sometimes served as **concubines** to Islamic men. They were granted certain freedoms — allowed to go to markets, perform musically — that legal wives did not have. These opportunities sometimes allowed enslaved women to earn enough to buy their own freedom.

## Women in Islamic Society

### Pre-Islamic Context

Some practices now associated with Islam — such as women covering their heads and faces — were already common cultural customs in Central Asia and the Byzantine Empire *before* Muhammad. Islam formalized and codified practices that already had regional precedent.

### Muhammad's Reforms

Muhammad actually **raised women's status** in significant ways compared to pre-Islamic Arabian society:
- Insisted **dowries** (payments by the groom's family) go to the bride, not her father
- **Forbade female infanticide** (killing of newborn girls)
- His first wife Khadijah was educated and ran her own business — a model for women's capability

### Women's Rights Under Islamic Law

Overall, the AP text argues that Islamic women enjoyed **higher status than Christian or Jewish women** of the same era:

| Right | Islamic Women | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Inherit property | ✅ Yes, and retain it after marriage | More rights than many contemporaries |
| Remarry if widowed | ✅ Yes | Not universal in Christian Europe |
| Divorce settlement | ✅ Cash settlement if divorced | Novel provision |
| Initiate divorce | ✅ Under some conditions | Unusual for the era |
| Birth control | ✅ Permitted | Relatively progressive |
| Court testimony | ✅ Protected from retaliation | Worth half a man's testimony |

### The Veil and the Harem

As Islamic cities grew, new restrictions on women emerged. The **veil** and the **harem** (a dwelling set aside for wives, concubines, and their children) became symbols of women's increasing seclusion in urban Islamic society — a pattern of urbanization *restricting* women's public roles seen in multiple cultures.

> [!warning]
> Be careful not to present Islamic women's status as simply "restricted." The AP source emphasizes that Islamic women had *more* legal rights than most women in contemporaneous Christian and Jewish societies. The restrictions that intensified in cities were real, but they don't capture the full picture. **Comparison with precision matters on the AP exam.**

## Islamic Rule in Spain: Al-Andalus

### The Umayyad Conquest

In **711**, after Muslim forces defeated Byzantine armies across North Africa, they invaded Spain from the south and designated **Córdoba** as their Iberian capital. The **Umayyad dynasty** maintained power in Spain long after losing the Middle East.

The Islamic military was stopped in **732** at the **Battle of Tours** — a defeat by Frankish forces that marked the limit of Islamic expansion into Western Europe. Most of the continent remained Christian. But Muslims ruled Spain for **seven centuries**.

### Tolerance and Prosperity in al-Andalus

The Islamic state in Spain, called **al-Andalus**, became one of the great multicultural success stories of the medieval world:
- Muslims, Christians, and Jews coexisted relatively peacefully
- All three were considered **"people of the book"** by Islamic rulers
- Córdoba had the **largest library in the world** at the time
- Trade was promoted; Chinese and Southeast Asian goods entered Europe through Spain

Ships called **dhows** — with long, thin hulls originally developed in India or China — carried much of this trade. Their design made them excellent cargo ships.

### The Chain of Knowledge Transfer

Al-Andalus was the conduit through which **Islamic scholarship transformed European civilization**:

1. Greek texts (Aristotle, etc.) → translated into Arabic → preserved in Islamic libraries
2. **Ibn Rushd (Averroes)** (12th century) → wrote influential commentaries on Aristotle in Arabic
3. His commentaries → influenced the Jewish philosopher **Maimonides (c. 1135–1204)**
4. Maimonides → developed a synthesis of Aristotelian reasoning and biblical interpretation
5. His work → influenced Christian philosopher **St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)**
6. This chain of transmission → laid the groundwork for the **[[Unit-3.3/Renaissance|Renaissance]]** and **[[Unit-4.6/Scientific Revolution|Scientific Revolution]]**

Additionally: **paper-making** (Chinese technology → taught to Europeans by Muslims via al-Andalus) was essential for the later spread of ideas across Europe.

> [!exam-tip]
> This chain of knowledge transfer (China → Islamic world → al-Andalus → Europe) is one of the most exam-tested narratives in AP World History. Know the sequence and the key names: Ibn Rushd → Maimonides → Aquinas.
