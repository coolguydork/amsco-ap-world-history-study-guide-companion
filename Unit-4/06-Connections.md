## 1. Concepts This Unit Builds On (from Earlier Units)

- **[[Indian Ocean Trade Network]]** (Unit 2) — European explorers deliberately sought to tap into the established Indian Ocean commercial world; the Portuguese literally used Arab navigational charts. Unit 4's oceanic expansion is *incomprehensible* without understanding what already existed.

- **[[Mongol Pax and Silk Roads]]** (Unit 2 / Unit 3) — The disruption of overland trade routes following the Black Death and Ottoman expansion *motivated* European maritime exploration as an alternative path to Asian goods.

- **[[Islamic Empires and Trade]]** (Unit 3) — Ottoman control of Eastern Mediterranean trade routes is a direct cause of Iberian exploration. The Ottoman threat to Mediterranean commerce pushed Portugal and Spain to seek oceanic alternatives.

- **[[African Kingdoms and Trans-Saharan Trade]]** (Unit 2) — West African states like Mali and Songhay had long participated in gold and slave trade across the Sahara; the Atlantic slave trade redirected and massively amplified existing African commercial networks.

- **[[Land-Based Empires]]** (Unit 3) — The Aztec and Inca empires' administrative sophistication (road networks, tribute systems, bureaucracies) was not only impressive in its own right but made them *targets* for Spanish conquest — and provided the infrastructure colonizers repurposed for their own extraction.

- **[[Gunpowder Technology]]** (Unit 3) — European use of firearms and cannon gave conquistadors a critical military advantage over Indigenous peoples, even when outnumbered; this advantage was itself borrowed from Chinese and Islamic military innovation.

## 2. Concepts This Unit Sets Up (for Later Units)

- **[[Atlantic Revolutions]]** (Unit 5) — The plantation economy and racial slavery of Unit 4 directly created the conditions for the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) and influenced the American and Latin American independence movements. Creole identity, shaped in Unit 4, fueled Unit 5 revolutions.

- **[[Industrialization]]** (Unit 5–6) — Profits from Atlantic slavery and colonial trade funded early British industrial investment. The plantation complex's demand for cheap, mass-produced textiles also created a market that mechanized textile manufacturing sought to serve.

- **[[Capitalism and Global Inequality]]** (Unit 6) — The joint-stock company, mercantilism, and silver-driven global trade of Unit 4 are the direct ancestors of the capitalist world-system that structures global inequality in Units 6–9. Dependency between periphery and core begins here.

- **[[Abolition Movements]]** (Unit 5) — The massive scale of the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]] in Unit 4 directly provokes the abolitionist movements that emerge in the late 18th and 19th centuries; the moral arguments against slavery draw partly on Enlightenment ideas about natural rights.

- **[[Imperialism in Asia and Africa]]** (Unit 6) — The trading-post empires and joint-stock companies of Unit 4 ([[VOC]], [[British East India Company]]) evolve into the full-scale territorial empires that carve up Africa and Asia in Unit 6.

- **[[Nationalism and Decolonization]]** (Units 7–9) — The racial hierarchies and cultural suppression established in Unit 4 are directly challenged by 20th-century decolonization and anti-colonial nationalism.

## 3. Comparative Civilizations / Contrasting Cases

- **Spanish Empire vs. Portuguese Empire** — Spain focused on territorial conquest and silver extraction (via coerced Indigenous labor); Portugal built a trading-post empire along coasts and relied more on commercial intermediaries. Compare [[Encomienda]]/[[Mita]] vs. Portuguese *feitorias*.

- **Atlantic Slave Trade vs. Indian Ocean Slave Trade** — Both existed, but the Atlantic trade was racially exclusive and resulted in hereditary chattel slavery; the Indian Ocean slave trade (including Ottoman and East African) was older but less racially defined and more varied in the social position of enslaved people.

- **European Maritime Empires vs. Ottoman / Mughal / Qing Land Empires** ([[Unit 3]]) — While European powers expanded overseas, the great land-based empires of Unit 3 expanded overland. By 1750, European maritime empires were beginning to outcompete them economically and militarily — a crucial setup for Unit 6 imperialism.

- **Columbian Exchange vs. Black Death Exchange** ([[Unit 2]]) — Both involved intercontinental disease transfer with massive demographic consequences. The Black Death (Unit 2) killed ~1/3 of Europe; smallpox and other European diseases killed 50–90% of Indigenous Americans. Compare the long-term political and social consequences of each.

- **Coerced labor in the Americas vs. Serfdom in Russia / Eastern Europe** ([[Unit 3]]) — Both intensified in the 16th–17th centuries as responses to labor shortages and commercial agricultural demand. Useful comparison for AP essays on labor systems.
