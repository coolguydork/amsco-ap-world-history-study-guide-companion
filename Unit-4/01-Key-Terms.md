# Key Terms — Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections

#apwh #unit-4 #period-2

## Vocabulary List

1. **Columbian Exchange** — The transfer of plants, animals, diseases, and peoples between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres following Columbus's 1492 voyage. (Transformed global demographics, diets, and economies; caused catastrophic population decline among Indigenous Americans.)

2. **Encomienda** — A Spanish colonial labor system that granted conquistadors the right to Indigenous labor in exchange for "protection" and Christian conversion. (Formalized Indigenous exploitation and laid the groundwork for later plantation slavery.)

3. **Mita** — A coerced rotational labor draft used by the Spanish in Andean mines, particularly at [[Potosí]]. (Extracted enormous silver wealth but decimated Indigenous Andean populations.)

4. **Chattel slavery** — A system in which enslaved people are treated as property to be bought, sold, and inherited. (Became the defining labor system of American plantation economies and the foundation of the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]].)

5. **Atlantic Slave Trade** — The forced transportation of approximately 12.5 million Africans to the Americas between the 16th and 19th centuries. (Largest forced migration in history; devastated West and Central African societies while building Atlantic economic wealth.)

6. **Middle Passage** — The brutal transatlantic voyage endured by enslaved Africans packed into slave ships. (Death rates ranged from 10–20%; a defining symbol of the inhumanity of the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]].)

7. **Triangular Trade** — The three-legged Atlantic commercial circuit in which European goods went to Africa, enslaved Africans went to the Americas, and American commodities returned to Europe. (Integrated Atlantic economies and entrenched racial slavery.)

8. **Potosí** — A city in present-day Bolivia that became the world's largest silver mine under Spanish colonial rule. (Its output financed the Spanish Empire and fueled global price inflation; worked largely through the [[Mita]] labor system.)

9. **Mercantilism** — An economic theory holding that national wealth is built by accumulating bullion through a favorable balance of trade and colonial extraction. (Drove European imperial competition and colonial exploitation throughout the period.)

10. **Joint-stock company** — A business venture in which investors pooled capital and shared profits and risks, enabling large-scale colonial enterprises. (Examples: the Dutch [[VOC]] and British East India Company; key instruments of European commercial expansion.)

11. **VOC (Dutch East India Company)** — The Dutch *Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie*, the world's first publicly traded company, which dominated the spice trade in Southeast Asia. (Model for later joint-stock imperial ventures; exercised quasi-governmental power in Asia.)

12. **Plantation complex** — An agricultural system in the Americas based on large-scale monoculture (sugar, tobacco, cotton) worked by enslaved African labor. (Generated massive profits for European elites while creating the African diaspora.)

13. **Syncretism** — The blending of different cultural, religious, or artistic traditions to form new hybrid practices. (E.g., blending of Catholicism with Indigenous and African spiritual traditions in the Americas; a key outcome of cross-cultural contact.)

14. **Mestizo / Casta system** — A racial hierarchy developed in Spanish colonial America that classified people by degrees of European, Indigenous, and African ancestry. (Formalized racial inequality and shaped social mobility in colonial Latin America.)

15. **Demographic collapse** — The catastrophic population decline of Indigenous Americans — estimated at 50–90% — following European contact due primarily to epidemic disease. (Fundamentally altered the labor supply and accelerated the demand for enslaved African workers.)

16. **Price Revolution** — A period of sustained inflation in 16th–17th century Europe driven largely by the influx of American silver. (Destabilized economies, enriched merchants, and impoverished those on fixed incomes across Eurasia.)

17. **Hacienda** — A large Spanish colonial estate in which Indigenous workers were tied to the land through debt peonage. (Successor to the [[Encomienda]]; persisted as a labor system well into the 19th century.)

18. **Devshirme** — The Ottoman practice of recruiting Christian boys from conquered territories to serve as soldiers (Janissaries) or administrators. (Parallels coercive labor systems elsewhere, though it offered paths to high status.)

19. **Creole** — A person of European descent born in the Americas. (Over time, creoles developed distinct identities that would fuel independence movements in the 18th–19th centuries.)

20. **Maroon communities** — Independent settlements established by escaped enslaved Africans in the Americas. (Evidence of active resistance to slavery; communities existed in Jamaica, Brazil, and Suriname.)

21. **Indentured servitude** — A labor system in which workers contracted to serve a master for a fixed term (typically 4–7 years) in exchange for passage to the Americas. (Used especially in early English colonies before [[Chattel slavery]] became dominant.)

22. **Spice trade** — The highly profitable commerce in cloves, nutmeg, pepper, and other spices from Southeast Asia. (Original driver of European exploration; became dominated by the Portuguese and then the [[VOC]].)

23. **Conquistador** — A Spanish military leader who conquered Indigenous empires in the Americas in the 16th century (e.g., Cortés, Pizarro). (Their campaigns combined military superiority, epidemic disease, and Indigenous allies to bring down the Aztec and Inca empires.)

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the difference between **encomienda**, **mita**, **hacienda**, and **indentured servitude** — the AP exam often asks you to compare coercive labor systems within or across periods.
