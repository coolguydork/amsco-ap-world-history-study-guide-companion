# Chapter 5: Key Terms — State Building in the Americas (1200–1450)

## Vocabulary

**Aztec (Mexica) Empire** — A powerful Mesoamerican empire (c. 1428–1521) centered at Tenochtitlan (present-day Mexico City); the city itself was founded in 1325, but the empire proper emerged with the Triple Alliance of 1428 under Itzcoatl; governed through a confederation of the Mexica, Texcoco, and Tlacopan. (Significance: the dominant political force in Mesoamerica at the time of Spanish contact; its tribute system, religious practices, and urban sophistication astonished European observers.)

**Tenochtitlan** — The capital city of the Aztec Empire, built on an island in Lake Texcoco; by 1500 it had a population of 200,000–300,000, making it one of the world's largest cities. (Significance: demonstrates the scale of Aztec urban achievement; connected to the mainland by causeways; organized around the great Templo Mayor pyramid; destroyed and rebuilt as Mexico City by the Spanish.)

**Triple Alliance** — The political union between the Mexica (Aztec), Texcoco, and Tlacopan that formed the foundation of the Aztec Empire. (Significance: the Aztec Empire was technically a confederation, not a single unified state, which helps explain its vulnerability to internal fragmentation when the Spanish arrived.)

**Tribute System (Aztec)** — The Aztec practice of requiring conquered peoples to provide regular payments of goods (food, textiles, precious materials, and sometimes people for sacrifice) to the imperial capital. (Significance: unlike the Chinese tribute system, the Aztec version was extractive rather than mutual; conquered peoples deeply resented it, providing the Spanish with indigenous allies.)

**Huitzilopochtli** — The Aztec sun god and patron deity of the Mexica people; required regular human sacrifice to sustain the sun's journey across the sky. (Significance: human sacrifice was central to Aztec religious practice and state ideology; the need for sacrificial victims was a driving motivation for the "Flower Wars" — ritualized conflict to capture enemies.)

**Flower Wars (Xochiyaoyotl)** — Ritualized battles conducted between the Aztecs and neighboring states for the primary purpose of capturing warriors for sacrifice rather than conquest or plunder. (Significance: demonstrates how Aztec religious ideology shaped military strategy; also shows the political limits of Aztec expansion — some neighboring states remained permanently hostile.)

**Templo Mayor** — The great double pyramid at the center of Tenochtitlan, dedicated to both Huitzilopochtli (sun/war) and Tlaloc (rain/agriculture); the ritual heart of the Aztec Empire. (Significance: symbolizes the Aztec worldview in which maintaining the cosmic order required regular sacrifice; archaeological excavations have confirmed extensive sacrificial practices.)

**Calpulli** — The basic social unit of Aztec society — a clan-based community group with shared land, temples, and schools; the building block of Aztec social organization. (Significance: provided social cohesion, education, and religious life below the level of the imperial state; demonstrates the complexity of Aztec social organization.)

**Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu)** — The largest pre-Columbian empire in the Americas, stretching 2,500 miles along the Pacific coast of South America from Ecuador to Chile; literally means "Four Quarters of the World." (Significance: a remarkable administrative achievement; governed over 10 million people through roads, relay runners, warehouses, and the *mita* labor system without writing or wheeled vehicles.)

**Pachacuti** — The Inca ruler (r. 1438–1471) who transformed the Inca from a small regional state into the vast Tawantinsuyu empire; his name means "he who shakes the earth." (Significance: one of history's most successful conquerors; responsible for the fundamental architecture of the Inca state; commissioned the construction of Machu Picchu.)

**Mita** — The Inca labor tax system requiring all subjects to contribute a set amount of labor to the state (constructing roads, temples, and terraces; serving in the military; working state-owned mines and farms). (Significance: the economic foundation of the Inca Empire, which had no monetary economy — the state extracted labor rather than taxes, and redistributed goods through state storehouses.)

**Quipu (Khipu)** — A system of knotted strings used by the Inca to record numerical data (tribute, census, military records) and possibly narrative information. (Significance: demonstrates that complex record-keeping and administration can function without conventional writing; the Inca Empire was governed by quipu-readers called *quipucamayoc*.)

**Machu Picchu** — An Inca mountain citadel built c. 1450 by Pachacuti, likely as a royal estate and religious site; located in the Peruvian Andes at 7,970 feet elevation. (Significance: demonstrates Inca architectural mastery — perfectly fitted stone without mortar, built on a steep mountain ridge; now Peru's most famous archaeological site.)

**Inca Road System (Qhapaq Ñan)** — The Inca road network covering over 25,000 miles of paved paths, rope bridges, and tunnels connecting the empire; built and maintained through mit'a labor obligations. (Significance: arguably the greatest infrastructure achievement in the pre-Columbian Americas; allowed the rapid movement of armies, information (via relay runners, *chasquis*), and goods across a vast, geographically diverse empire. Note: the road system and the mit'a are distinct — mit'a was the labor tax used to construct and maintain roads, temples, and other state projects.)

**Maya** — A Mesoamerican civilization with roots before 1000 BCE; by 1200–1450, Classic Maya cities had collapsed, but Maya peoples maintained distinct communities and states in Yucatán and highland Guatemala. (Significance: Aztec civilization built on Mesoamerican foundations laid by the Maya, including the ritual calendar, ballgame, and writing system; Maya continuity after the "collapse" is itself historically significant.)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently compares the Aztec and Inca empires. Key comparison points: **tribute vs. mita** (extraction methods), **urban centralization (Aztec) vs. road network administration (Inca)**, **writing vs. quipu**, and **religious sacrifice (Aztec) vs. ancestor veneration (Inca)**.
