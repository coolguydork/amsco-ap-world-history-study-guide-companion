**AP Period:** Period 3 — c. 1750 to c. 1900
**Unit Theme:** *Consequences of [[Concept Notes/Industrialization\|Industrialization]]* — industrial power created the capacity, the motive, and the ideology for European states to partition most of the non-Western world, reshaping global economies, triggering indigenous resistance, and setting mass migration in motion.

---

## The Big Picture

Unit 6 asks a deceptively simple question: what happened to the rest of the world as Europe industrialized? The answer is imperialism — the most dramatic restructuring of global power since the Columbian Exchange. By 1914, European powers and their offshoots (the United States and Japan) controlled about 84% of the earth's surface. How did this happen in roughly 70 years, and what did it mean for those on the receiving end?

The answer begins in Unit 5. Industrial technology gave European militaries an unprecedented advantage over non-industrial societies. Steamships, the Maxim gun, quinine prophylaxis against malaria, and telegraphic coordination of forces created what historian Daniel Headrick called "the tools of empire." Industrial economies also created insatiable demand: raw cotton for Manchester's mills, rubber for bicycle tires and telegraph insulation, palm oil for machine lubricants, copper for electrical cables, and markets for manufactured goods. These economic pressures turned imperial expansion from occasional to systematic.

But industrialization alone does not explain the *scale* of imperialism or the *ideology* behind it. [[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview|Topic 6.1]] shows that European expansion was also driven by nationalist competition (no great power could afford to let rivals take territory first), religious missionary impulse, and a self-serving ideology — Social Darwinism and the "White Man's Burden" — that cast conquest as a civilizing duty rather than self-interested plunder.

[[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview|Topic 6.2]] traces how these motives translated into action: the Scramble for Africa, the formalization of British India under Crown rule, Russian expansion across Central Asia, and American imperialism in the Pacific and Caribbean. The [[Concept Notes/Berlin Conference|Berlin Conference]] (1884–85) symbolizes the era's logic — European powers divided an entire continent among themselves without consulting a single African leader.

Topics 6.3 and 6.5 complicate the narrative of inevitable European domination. Indigenous peoples resisted — militarily (the Zulu, Ethiopians at Adwa), politically (the Indian National Congress), and culturally. Economic imperialism ([[Unit-6.5/Economic Imperialism Overview|6.5]]) shows that formal colonization was not the only mechanism: Britain controlled China's trade through unequal treaties and the [[Concept Notes/Opium Trade|Opium Trade]], and dominated Latin America through investment and debt without annexing any territory.

Topics 6.6 and 6.7 show the human dimension: imperialism set roughly 55–65 million Europeans and millions more Asian and African laborers in motion across the globe. Migration transformed both sending and receiving societies, creating diaspora communities, ethnic tensions, and the racially charged immigration restrictions of the late 19th century.

The unit's central AP argument — its CCOT thesis — is this: industrialization *changed* the mechanism of global inequality (from tributary and slave systems to cash-crop extraction and "free" labor markets) while *continuing* the fundamental structural reality of European wealth built on non-European labor and resources. The colonial structures established between 1750 and 1900 persisted long enough to shape decolonization movements (Unit 8) and post-colonial global inequality into the present.

## Topic-by-Topic Narrative

### 6.1 — Rationales for Imperialism

European imperialism required not just power but justification. [[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview|Topic 6.1]] examines the ideological framework that made conquest seem natural, even noble, to the Europeans conducting it — and that masked the economic interests driving expansion.

**Social Darwinism** applied Darwin's theory of natural selection to human societies, arguing that "superior" civilizations inevitably and rightfully dominated "inferior" ones. This pseudoscientific racism provided biological cover for what was essentially self-interested plunder. **Scientific racism** — the classification of human "races" in a hierarchy with Europeans at the top — reinforced this ideology with the authority of science.

The **"White Man's Burden"** (Rudyard Kipling, 1899) and the French **mission civilisatrice** ("civilizing mission") cast imperialism as a humanitarian duty. Europeans, in this telling, were bringing education, Christianity, modern medicine, and good governance to peoples incapable of progress on their own. The condescension was absolute and the irony was invisible to its practitioners: the same powers claiming to civilize Africa were extracting its resources, dismantling its political systems, and imposing forced labor.

**Nationalist competition** was equally powerful. After German unification (1871), European great powers competed for global prestige through empire. To fall behind in the colonial race was to lose standing among nations. This competitive dynamic meant that once one power moved, others had to follow — accelerating the pace of partition far beyond what economic rationale alone would have required.

**Economic motivations** were real even when ideological. Industrial economies needed raw materials (cotton, rubber, tin, copper), markets for manufactured goods, and investment outlets. The argument that imperialism was primarily driven by economic need (Lenin's thesis) oversimplifies — many colonies cost more to govern than they generated — but economic logic shaped which territories attracted the most aggressive expansion.

> [!exam-tip]
> [[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview|Topic 6.1]] tests **causation** directly. The AP exam will ask you to explain *why* imperialism happened, and the best answers weigh multiple causes against each other: ideology (Social Darwinism), economics (raw materials and markets), political competition (nationalism), and technology (industrial military advantage). Do not treat any single cause as sufficient.

### 6.2 — State Expansion

The mechanics of empire varied by region and by imperial power, but between 1870 and 1914 the pace was extraordinary. [[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview|Topic 6.2]] traces the "Scramble for Africa," the consolidation of Asian empires, and the emergence of American and Japanese imperialism.

**Africa** was the most dramatic case. In 1870, Europeans controlled about 10% of Africa; by 1914, they controlled about 90%. The [[Concept Notes/Berlin Conference|Berlin Conference]] (1884–85) — convened by Bismarck to regulate the scramble — established two principles: the "effective occupation" doctrine (claiming territory required actual presence, not just a flag) and the principle of free trade access for all European powers in the Congo basin. No African leader was invited. The results on the map were lines drawn through ethnic territories, separating peoples who shared language and culture and joining those who did not — boundaries that persist today.

**British India** transitioned from [[Concept Notes/East India Company|East India Company]] control to Crown rule after the Sepoy Rebellion (Indian Rebellion) of 1857. The Company's mixed record of commercial exploitation and administrative incompetence gave way to the British Raj — direct Crown governance that presented itself as a modernizing force while systematically extracting Indian wealth. The railroad network built across India (by 1900, the largest in Asia) integrated the subcontinent into the British export economy and served as the model for colonial infrastructure development worldwide.

**Russian expansion** moved east and south across Central Asia. Russia's "Great Game" with Britain for influence in Afghanistan and Persia was the Central Asian dimension of imperial competition. Unlike Western European empires separated from their colonies by oceans, Russian expansion was overland and contiguous — making the boundaries between metropole and colony ambiguous.

**American imperialism** emerged as a distinct force after the [[Concept Notes/Spanish-American War\|Spanish-American War]] (1898), which gave the United States control of the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam. American expansion echoed European justifications — "Manifest Destiny" extended to a Pacific and Caribbean sphere — while insisting it was fundamentally different in character. The brutal Philippine-American War (1899–1902) exposed the contradiction.

**Japan** avoided colonization by becoming a colonizer. The Meiji government's rapid industrialization ([[Unit-5.6/Industrialization: Government's Role Overview\|Industrialization: Government's Role Overview]]) produced military capacity that Japan deployed first against China (Sino-Japanese War, 1894–95, winning Taiwan) and then against Russia (Russo-Japanese War, 1904–05 — the first Asian victory over a European power in modern warfare). Japan's imperial expansion into Korea and Manchuria followed Western patterns so closely that it was simultaneously admired and resented by Western powers.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Berlin Conference is a high-frequency DBQ/LEQ topic.** Practice arguing both sides: the conference "formalized" European imperialism (it did not cause it) and it was as much about European powers regulating competition with each other as about Africa itself. Know the "effective occupation" doctrine.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Japan is the AP exam's key comparison case for imperialism.** Japan industrialized *to avoid* colonization, then colonized others using the same tools. This makes Japan evidence for multiple arguments: about the relationship between industrialization and imperialism, about non-Western responses to Western pressure, and about the universality of imperial logic.

### 6.3 — Indigenous Responses to State Expansion

European conquest was never unopposed. [[Unit-6.3/Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview|Topic 6.3]] examines how colonized peoples resisted, accommodated, and adapted to imperial rule — and why some responses were more effective than others.

**Armed resistance** was the first response in most cases, and it was often initially successful before superior European firepower prevailed. The **Zulu** under Cetshwayo defeated the British at Isandlwana (1879) — one of the most dramatic British defeats in colonial history — before being overwhelmed at Ulundi. **Ethiopia** under Emperor Menelik II defeated an Italian invasion at the **Battle of Adwa** (1895), remaining the only African state never colonized by a European power. The victory was made possible by Menelik's diplomatic skill (playing European powers against each other for arms imports) and the Ethiopians' tactical use of terrain. Adwa became a global symbol of African resistance and inspiration for 20th-century Pan-African movements.

The **Sepoy Rebellion** (1857) — also called the Indian Rebellion or India's "First War of Independence" — arose from a combination of religious grievances (the Enfield rifle cartridge controversy), economic exploitation, and political resentment of Company rule. Its suppression led to Crown takeover of India but also to British reforms in governance aimed at co-opting Indian elites. The Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901) in China mobilized popular anger against foreign presence but failed to expel the colonial powers and led to the humiliating Boxer Protocol.

**Political and legal resistance** emerged as colonial systems matured. Educated colonial subjects — trained in European universities and law schools — used Western political ideas (liberalism, rights, nationalism) to challenge colonial rule on its own terms. The **[[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress|Indian National Congress]]** (founded 1885) began as a loyal petition-body seeking reform within the British system before gradually developing into a nationalist independence movement. This pattern — using Western political education to challenge Western imperialism — became the template for 20th-century anti-colonial nationalism across Asia and Africa.

**Accommodation and collaboration** were also real responses. Local elites who cooperated with colonial powers often gained personal advantages: protected economic positions, education access, and entry into colonial bureaucracies. Colonial powers deliberately cultivated collaborating classes — the "indirect rule" model used by the British in much of Africa worked precisely by governing through existing African chiefs and emirs who adapted their authority to serve colonial purposes.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Ethiopia's successful resistance at Adwa (1895) is the AP's canonical example of African agency.** When asked to argue for African resistance, Adwa is your strongest evidence. Know its causes (Menelik's diplomacy and arms acquisition) and significance (the only African defeat of a European colonizer; inspiration for Pan-Africanism).

> [!warning]
> **Do not treat colonial subjects as passive victims.** The AP exam rewards responses that show agency — that colonized peoples made choices, developed strategies, and created organizations that shaped the course of imperialism and laid groundwork for eventual independence. Accommodation and collaboration were strategies, not just submission.

### 6.4 — Global Economic Development

[[Concept Notes/Imperialism\|Imperialism]] fundamentally restructured the global economy, creating a world divided between industrial producers and primary-commodity exporters. [[Unit-6.4/Global Economic Development Overview|Topic 6.4]] examines the economic mechanisms and their consequences.

**Extractive infrastructure** was the visible face of colonial economic development. Railroads penetrated colonial interiors not to connect African or Asian cities to each other, but to carry raw materials from mines and plantations to coastal ports for export. The same railway that made British India legible and taxable made it possible to drain grain from famished provinces for export — contributing to the estimated 12–29 million deaths in the Indian famines of 1876–1879 and 1899–1900 under British Raj policies.

**Cash-crop agriculture** replaced subsistence farming across colonial territories. West African farmers who had grown food crops for local consumption were pushed — by taxation, land alienation, or market prices — into growing cotton, palm oil, groundnuts, or cocoa for export. The Suez Canal (1869) and the transatlantic telegraph network (1866) accelerated global commodity markets, but colonial producers had no control over the prices they received.

**Deindustrialization** was the paradoxical result in formerly artisanal regions. India had been a major exporter of cotton textiles before British rule; the [[Concept Notes/East India Company|East India Company]] systematically dismantled Indian textile production to protect British mills, flooding India with cheap machine-made cloth. This pattern — where colonial policy destroyed existing industry to create markets for metropolitan goods — directly produced the poverty and economic dependency that would define the post-colonial world.

**Environmental consequences** of extractive imperialism included deforestation for rubber and timber, soil exhaustion from monoculture, and displacement of indigenous peoples from their land. The Congo Free State under Belgian King Leopold II became the extreme case: systematic forced rubber extraction, mutilation as punishment for non-production, and a death toll historians estimate between 1 and 10 million.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The AP exam tests whether students can explain *how* imperialism restructured economies, not just *that* it did.** Focus on the mechanisms: cash-crop agriculture, extractive infrastructure, deindustrialization, and the creation of economic dependency. These mechanisms connect 6.4 directly to post-colonial global inequality and to Unit 8's decolonization movements.

### 6.5 — Economic Imperialism

Formal colonization — annexing territory and establishing direct political rule — was only one form of imperial control. [[Unit-6.5/Economic Imperialism Overview|Topic 6.5]] examines "economic imperialism": domination through trade, investment, debt, and unequal treaties that achieved the same economic extraction without the administrative costs of colonization.

**China** was the paradigmatic case. The **Opium Wars** (1839–42, 1856–60) were triggered by British insistence on the right to sell opium — a narcotic — in China over Chinese government objections. When China resisted, Britain used military force to impose **unequal treaties** that: opened "treaty ports" to foreign trade, granted extraterritoriality (foreign citizens immune from Chinese law), imposed war indemnities, and eventually created "spheres of influence" dividing China into economic zones controlled by different powers. China was never formally colonized — it remained nominally sovereign — but by 1900 it had lost control of its trade policy, its ports, and much of its tariff revenue. The [[Concept Notes/Opium Trade|Opium Trade]] link traces this across the broader period.

**Japan** faced similar pressure when Commodore Perry's "black ships" forced the opening of Japanese ports in 1853–54. Japan's response — rapid state-directed industrialization (the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]]) — allowed it to escape the fate of China by becoming an industrial power before Western economic imperialism could take hold. This contrast between Chinese and Japanese responses to the same Western pressure is one of the AP exam's most tested comparisons.

**Latin America** after independence faced British economic imperialism without political colonization. Britain invested heavily in Argentine and Brazilian infrastructure (railways, port facilities) and dominated trade. The terms of trade systematically disadvantaged Latin American primary exporters: their commodity prices fluctuated while the prices of British manufactures were more stable. By the late 19th century, US firms like the United Fruit Company were replicating this pattern in Central America — controlling enough economic infrastructure to effectively dictate government policy ("banana republics").

> [!exam-tip]
> **Economic imperialism vs. political imperialism is a key compare question.** The mechanisms differ (trade and debt vs. conquest and administration) but the economic outcomes are similar: extraction of primary commodities, deindustrialization, and long-term dependency. The AP exam rewards students who can compare China and Latin America as examples of economic imperialism against India and Africa as examples of political imperialism.

> [!exam-tip]
> **China–Japan comparison is high-frequency.** Both faced Western pressure in the mid-19th century. China responded slowly, maintained traditional governance structures, and was subjected to unequal treaties. Japan responded with rapid, top-down industrialization and modernization. By 1905, Japan had defeated Russia militarily; China was partitioned into spheres of influence. The key variable: depth and speed of state-directed reform.

### 6.6 — Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World

Imperialism set the world in motion. Between c. 1815 and 1914, more than 55 million people left Europe for the Americas, Australasia, and southern Africa. Millions more moved within and between colonial territories as laborers. [[Unit-6.6/Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World Overview|Topic 6.6]] examines the push factors that expelled and the pull factors that attracted these migrants.

**European mass emigration** was driven by push factors at home and pull factors abroad. Push factors included: population growth without corresponding agricultural employment, the Irish Famine (1845–52) that killed approximately 1 million and drove 1–2 million more to emigrate, Jewish pogroms in Russia (particularly after 1880) that drove hundreds of thousands to flee, and economic disruption from industrialization itself (displaced artisans, agricultural workers losing to mechanization). Pull factors included: available land in the Americas and Australasia, industrial labor demand in the United States, and active recruitment by receiving governments (the United States, Canada, Argentina, and Australia all advertised for settlers).

**Labor migration within empires** took a more coercive form. After Britain abolished the transatlantic slave trade (1807) and then slavery itself (1833–38) across its empire, plantation economies in the Caribbean, South Africa, Fiji, and Mauritius faced labor shortages. The response was **indentured labor** — contracts that brought Indian and Chinese workers to these colonies for fixed terms (typically 5 years) in conditions that critics called "a new system of slavery." Workers signed away freedom of movement and were legally bound to their employer. Though technically temporary, many indentured laborers remained permanently, creating South Asian diaspora communities across the British Caribbean, East Africa, and the Pacific.

**"Coolie" labor** — the term applied (usually pejoratively) to Chinese contract workers — built railroads across the American West, worked mines in Peru and Cuba, and staffed plantations across Southeast Asia. These workers faced dangerous conditions, racial discrimination, and legal barriers to settlement in the societies their labor built.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Distinguish voluntary from coerced migration.** European emigration was largely voluntary (though economic desperation was a major push). Indentured and coolie labor was coerced — contracts signed under duress, with limited information about conditions, and legal mechanisms that removed exit options. The AP exam rewards students who understand this spectrum from free to unfree labor rather than treating all migration as equivalent.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Indentured labor directly replaces enslaved labor.** The indentured system was created explicitly to fill the labor vacuum left by emancipation. Linking 6.6 back to [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and to Unit 4's discussion of slavery shows how coercive labor systems *transformed but persisted* across the period — a classic continuity argument.

### 6.7 — Effects of Migration

Mass migration transformed both the societies migrants left and the societies they entered. [[Unit-6.7/Effects of Migration Overview|Topic 6.7]] examines the cultural, economic, and political consequences of this demographic upheaval.

**Receiving societies** were fundamentally changed. The United States went from approximately 23 million people in 1850 to 76 million in 1900, absorbing tens of millions of European migrants. Argentina's population quadrupled between 1870 and 1914. Australia's gold rushes drew Chinese migrants whose presence triggered racist legislation. New ethnic neighborhoods — Chinatowns in San Francisco, Little Italy in New York, Jewish enclaves in Chicago — formed as migrants clustered with others who shared language, religion, and culture. These communities maintained ties to home regions (through letters, remittances, and return migration) while also adapting to new environments.

**Cultural diffusion** worked in multiple directions. Migrants brought foodways, religious practices, musical traditions, and political ideas into new contexts. Irish Catholic immigrants transformed American urban politics. Chinese immigrants introduced new agricultural techniques to California. Indian diaspora communities carried South Asian religious practices (Hinduism, Islam, Sikhism) to Trinidad, Guyana, Fiji, and Natal. These cultural transfers created hybrid identities that were neither purely "home" nor purely "host."

**Nativist backlash** was pervasive. In the United States, the **Chinese Exclusion Act** (1882) was the first federal law to restrict immigration by race — prohibiting Chinese laborers from entering the country and barring Chinese immigrants already there from naturalization. Australian states enacted similar restrictions. Anti-immigrant movements emphasized racial and cultural "purity," economic competition for jobs, and national identity. These tensions foreshadowed the immigration restriction regimes of the 20th century.

**Effects on sending societies** were also significant. **Remittances** — money sent home by migrants — sustained families and sometimes transformed village economies in Ireland, Italy, China, and the Punjab. Out-migration created labor shortages in some sending regions, driving up wages for those who stayed. But migration also drained human capital: the most adventurous, youngest, and most economically ambitious members of communities were often those who left, depressing long-term development in places with very high emigration rates.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) is the AP's go-to example of anti-immigrant discrimination.** Know its context (competition with Chinese workers on the transcontinental railroad and in California gold mines), its mechanism (racial restriction on labor immigration), and its significance (first racially targeted immigration restriction in US history).

### 6.8 — Causation in the Imperial Age

The synthesis topic asks students to apply AP historical reasoning skills to the unit as a whole. [[Unit-6.8/Causation in the Imperial Age Overview|Topic 6.8]] focuses on **causation** — explaining *why* industrialization and imperialism happened, and *why* their consequences were distributed so unequally.

**The causal chain of imperialism** runs: industrial technology creates military advantage → national competition drives expansion → ideology (Social Darwinism, mission civilisatrice) provides justification → extractive colonial economies destroy indigenous production → migration fills labor gaps created by abolition → diaspora communities and nationalist movements emerge → anti-colonial resistance escalates. Each link in this chain connects topics within Unit 6 and extends forward into Units 7 and 8.

**The CCOT argument** for the unit asks: what changed and what continued between c. 1750 and c. 1900 in terms of migration, living standards, imperial expansion, and political structures?

Major *changes*: the mechanisms of labor exploitation shifted from chattel slavery to indentured service and cash-wage labor; European empires shifted from coastal trading posts and plantation colonies to systematic territorial partition; industrial technology transformed the pace and scale of global integration; new ideologies (Social Darwinism, racial science) provided new justifications for old hierarchies.

Major *continuities*: the fundamental structure of non-European labor producing resources for European wealth persisted; social hierarchy (now organized by race and "civilization" rather than birth and religion alone) continued to justify extraction; political exclusion of the colonized from governance remained absolute; violence as the ultimate enforcer of colonial control never disappeared.

**Historical perspectives** on this unit divide along the question of whether imperialism ultimately benefited colonized societies. Modernization theorists argued that colonial infrastructure (railroads, telegraphs, medicine, law) produced long-term economic development. Dependency theorists (André Gunder Frank, Walter Rodney) argued that colonialism *underdeveloped* Africa and Asia by systematically draining wealth, destroying local industry, and integrating colonial economies as raw-material appendages of industrial Europe — with consequences that persist into the present.

> [!exam-tip]
> **For any Unit 6 LEQ or DBQ, the strongest thesis argument connects industrial technology as the enabling cause to economic extraction as the primary motive, mediated by nationalist competition.** Do not list causes — *weigh* them: which was most significant, and why?

> [!tip]
> **Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972)** is the key historiographical reference for arguing that colonialism had long-term negative economic consequences. Modernization theory (W.W. Rostow) is the opposing view. Knowing this debate allows you to write a sophisticated Historical Perspectives argument on any Unit 6 question.

## Key Comparisons and Arguments for the AP Exam

> [!exam-tip]
> **Most Important CCOT Argument (Unit 6):** The defining *change* is the mechanism of labor control — chattel slavery replaced by indentured service and wage labor, which maintained economic extraction while claiming to honor post-abolition "freedom." The defining *continuity* is the structural inequality between industrial economies (wealth accumulators) and colonial economies (raw-material exporters). Frame any Unit 6 CCOT around this axis.

> [!exam-tip]
> **China vs. Japan — The Essential Comparison:** Both faced Western economic pressure in the 1850s. China maintained traditional governance and responded defensively; Japan undertook rapid, state-directed industrialization. By 1905, Japan had defeated Russia; China had been partitioned into spheres of influence. The variable: depth and speed of state-directed reform. Use this comparison in any LEQ about responses to imperialism or about why some regions industrialized while others did not.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Political vs. Economic Imperialism:** India and Africa (formal colonization — direct political rule) vs. China and Latin America (economic imperialism — unequal treaties, foreign investment, debt). The mechanisms differ, the economic outcomes are similar: extraction of primary resources, destruction of local industry, long-term dependency.

> [!tip]
> **Contextualization for any Unit 6 essay:** The best contextualization connects to Unit 5's Industrial Revolution — the *technological gap* created by industrialization made European conquest feasible and the *economic demand* of industrial economies made it necessary. You can also contextualize with Unit 4's colonial trade networks: European imperial expansion in Unit 6 builds on patterns established in Unit 4 but operates at a different scale and with different mechanisms.

> [!warning]
> **Common misconception — Colonialism as economic development:** The AP exam frequently presents documents arguing that colonial infrastructure (railroads, telegraph, law) modernized colonized societies. Do not accept this framing uncritically. The dependency theory counter-argument — that this infrastructure served extraction, not development, and that colonial policies actively deindustrialized previously manufacturing regions — is equally testable and often the stronger historical argument.

> [!warning]
> **Common misconception — European expansion as inevitable:** Students sometimes write as if colonized peoples had no choices or no agency. The AP exam rewards responses that show Ethiopian resistance at Adwa, Indian National Congress organizing, and Chinese self-strengthening movements as real historical forces — even when they ultimately failed to prevent colonization. Agency exists even in defeat.

## Cross-Unit Connections

**Back to Unit 5 (Revolutions and Industrialization):**
Unit 6 cannot be understood without Unit 5. The Industrial Revolution (5.3–5.5) created the technological tools (steamships, the Maxim gun, [[Concept Notes/Quinine|Quinine]] for malaria prevention) and the economic motive (raw material demand, market hunger) that made the scale of 19th-century imperialism possible. The uneven *spread* of industrialization ([[Unit-5.4/Industrialization Spreads Overview|5.4]]) directly created the power gap between industrial and non-industrial societies that imperialism exploited. Nationalist competition born in 5.2's nationalist movements drove the Scramble for Africa. See [[Unit-5.4/Industrialization Spreads Overview|Industrialization Spreads Overview]] and [[Unit-5.5/Technology in the Industrial Age Overview|Technology in the Industrial Age Overview]].

**Back to Unit 4 (Transoceanic Interconnections):**
The patterns of Unit 6 build on Unit 4's colonial structures. European coastal trading posts became the launching points for interior conquest. The plantation economies of the Caribbean — established in Unit 4 — created the demand for indentured labor after emancipation ended enslaved labor. The [[Concept Notes/East India Company|East India Company]] links Unit 4's merchant capitalism to Unit 6's formal empire. The fundamental continuity of non-European resource extraction for European enrichment runs from Unit 4 through Unit 6. See [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview|Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview]] and [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]].

**Forward to Unit 7 (Global Conflict):**
The rivalries created by imperial competition directly caused World War I. German unification and industrial growth threatened British naval and economic supremacy; competition for African and Asian territory created flash points; the alliance system built to manage imperial competition made any regional conflict global. The **[[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism|Pan-Africanism]]** and Indian nationalist movements born in Unit 6's resistance phase ([[Unit-6.3/Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview|6.3]]) become the organized independence movements of Unit 8. The anti-colonial nationalism that the Berlin Conference so thoroughly ignored was already germinating by 1900.

**Forward to Unit 8 (Cold War Origins and Decolonization):**
The structural inequalities created by Unit 6 — colonial extractive economies, racial hierarchies embedded in law and governance, suppressed indigenous political organization — define the conditions from which Unit 8's decolonization movements emerge. The Indian National Congress (1885) becomes Gandhi's independence vehicle. Pan-Africanism (rooted in diaspora communities created by Unit 6 migration) becomes the intellectual foundation for African independence. The post-colonial poverty of Unit 8 and 9's developing world is the direct economic legacy of the extractive structures [[Unit-6.4/Global Economic Development Overview|Topic 6.4]] describes.
