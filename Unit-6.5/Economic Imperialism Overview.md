# Economic Imperialism

**Unit 6 | Consequences of Industrialization | AP Period 3 | AMSCO pp. 407–416**

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam distinguishes **economic imperialism** (control without formal colonization) from **political imperialism** (direct colonial rule). Britain's role in Argentina and Latin America is the go-to example of the former. Know both Opium Wars, the Treaty of Nanking, and the Culture System for free-response evidence.

## Essential Question
How did economic imperialism differ from political imperialism, and what were its effects?

## What Was Economic Imperialism?

Economic imperialism describes a situation in which foreign businesses and industrialized states gain dominant economic power over weaker nations **without necessarily establishing formal political control**. It emerged as a direct consequence of industrialization: factories needed raw materials and new markets, and industrial military technology—steamships, railroads, superior weapons—gave industrial powers the leverage to reshape economies across Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

The mechanism was consistent: colonial or semi-colonial powers forced subject regions to become **export economies**, producing raw materials (cotton, opium, rubber, palm oil, cocoa) for European or American factories, then buying back finished goods at inflated prices. This restructured local agriculture away from food production, created dangerous dependency on single commodities, and extracted wealth systematically without distributing it locally (AMSCO p. 407–408).

## Economic Imperialism in Asia

**India** was the most consequential case. The [[Concept Notes/East India Company|East India Company]] (est. 1600) first dominated the textile trade, then pivoted to forcing Indian farmers to grow [[Concept Notes/Opium Trade|opium]] for export to China. By the late 19th century, India was supplying raw cotton to British mills—exactly reversing its earlier role as a manufacturer of finished textiles (p. 408).

**China** became the center of the most dramatic confrontation. Chinese goods (silk, porcelain, tea) were in high demand in Britain, but China wanted little from Britain in return. To offset this imbalance, the East India Company flooded China with opium. When Chinese Commissioner **Lin Zexu** destroyed British opium stocks in Canton (1839), the First **Opium War** (1839–1842) followed. British industrial military technology—steamships and superior firearms—overwhelmed Chinese defenses. The **Treaty of Nanking** (1842) forced China to open five ports, cede Hong Kong, and pay reparations: the first of the "unequal treaties." The Second Opium War (1856–1860) produced the Treaty of Tientsin, legalizing opium and opening additional ports. Other powers—France, Germany, Russia, Japan, the United States—then pressed China for their own **spheres of influence**, exclusive trading zones within Chinese territory (pp. 408–409).

**Dutch East Indies** (present-day Indonesia): The Dutch East India Company monopolized the **Spice Islands** trade before pivoting to agriculture. In 1830 the Dutch government introduced the **Culture System**, forcing Javanese farmers to devote one-fifth of their land to export crops (sugar, coffee, indigo) or perform **corvée labor** for 66 days per year. Food shortages resulted. The system was abolished in 1870 under humanitarian pressure (p. 408).

> [!warning]
> The Open Door Policy (US, 1899) proposed that all foreign powers have equal trading access to China—it was *not* altruistic. It was designed to prevent any single power from monopolizing China and cutting the US out of the market.

## Economic Imperialism in Africa

Before colonization, African farmers grew primarily food crops. Colonizers restructured land use around **cash crops** for export: cotton in Egypt, Sudan, and Uganda; cocoa on the Gold Coast; palm oil across West Africa. The exchange was deeply unequal—Africans received cotton textiles, canned food, and alcohol in return (p. 409).

- **Egypt** embraced cotton so thoroughly that by century's end it accounted for 93% of exports—leaving the economy dangerously exposed to price fluctuations.
- **Kenya**: The Kikuyu and other indigenous groups were displaced to infertile reserves while fertile Rift Valley land was transferred to white settlers. Africans were legally barred from growing export cash crops.
- **Gold Coast**: Became the world's largest cocoa producer after missionaries introduced the crop in the 1880s.
- **Slavery persisted**: Abolished in British colonies in 1833 but continued elsewhere in Africa until the early 20th century. Slave labor was used to produce palm oil, coffee, and cocoa across French and Portuguese colonies.

## Economic Imperialism in Latin America

Britain replaced Spain as Latin America's dominant trading partner in the 19th century. European investors poured over $10 billion into Latin America between 1870–1914, primarily in **Argentina**, Mexico, and Brazil. British capital built railroads, telegraph systems, and port infrastructure across the continent—exerting economic dominance without colonization (informal empire) (pp. 412–413).

**Argentina** became the richest country in Latin America, with 10% of all British foreign investment directed there. The **Pampas** grasslands were developed into large-scale farming and ranching.

**Central America**: The **United Fruit Company** (US) controlled banana production and allied with local landowners to pressure governments—giving rise to the term **"banana republics."** When corporate interests were threatened, US and European governments intervened militarily, demonstrating how economic imperialism could slide into political control.

**Hawaii** (Pacific): American sugar planters overthrew the constitutional monarchy in 1893. Hawaii was annexed as a US territory in 1898.

> [!tip]
> **Comparing forms of imperialism:** Political imperialism = direct colonial rule (British India, French West Africa). Economic imperialism = control through investment, trade, and debt (British Argentina, US Central America). Many regions experienced both simultaneously.

## Why It Mattered

Economic imperialism deepened global inequality and created structural dependency that outlasted formal empire. Regions locked into single-commodity export economies were vulnerable to global price swings, unable to industrialize, and stripped of resources that might have funded local development. The Great Famine in India (c. 1876), which killed millions while Britain continued exporting grain, is the starkest illustration of how economic extraction could override human welfare.
