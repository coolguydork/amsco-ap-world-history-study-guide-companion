# People, States & Movements

**Topic 6.5 — Economic [[Concept Notes/Imperialism\|Imperialism]] | AMSCO pp. 407–416**

---

## Key Figures

**Lin Zexu (Lin Tse-hsü)**
Chinese Imperial Commissioner in Canton, appointed in 1839 to enforce the emperor's ban on opium. His famous letter to Queen Victoria argued that Britain could not justify selling a drug it had banned in its own country. When diplomatic pressure failed, he confiscated and destroyed 20,000+ chests of British opium. Britain responded with the First Opium War (1839–1842). Lin Zexu's actions were morally unimpeachable but strategically catastrophic for China—his stand revealed China's military vulnerability to industrialized powers. He is remembered in China as a national hero and symbol of principled anti-colonial resistance (AMSCO pp. 407, 409).

> [!exam-tip]
> Lin Zexu's letter to Queen Victoria is a high-frequency SAQ/DBQ source on the AP exam. His main argument: opium is banned in Britain because it causes harm; therefore Britain has no moral right to sell it to China. Be ready to identify his perspective and explain why it failed to change British policy.

**Muhammad Ali (Egypt, r. 1805–1848)**
Modernizing ruler of Egypt who attempted to industrialize the country through state-directed development—building textile factories, reforming the military, and developing cotton agriculture. His cotton-export economy was initially a success story but made Egypt dependent on global cotton prices and European creditors. Subsequent rulers borrowed heavily, giving Britain and France financial leverage that eventually led to British military occupation of Egypt in 1882. Muhammad Ali's career illustrates how a reforming leader's economic modernization program could unintentionally set the stage for colonial domination (p. 410).

**John D. Rockefeller / Standard Oil**
American industrialist whose Standard Oil Company dominated global petroleum production in the late 19th century. Though less directly tied to the AMSCO 6.5 narrative than Lin Zexu, Rockefeller appears in the ref file as a symbol of US corporate imperialism—the model for the United Fruit Company and other American enterprises that used wealth and government backing to control foreign economies. The Standard Oil model (monopolistic control of a global commodity, backed by US political power) is the template for later US economic imperialism in Latin America.

## Key States and Colonial Holdings

**China ([[Concept Notes/Qing Dynasty\|Qing Dynasty]])**
By 1839 the Qing Dynasty ruled a massive empire that had long viewed itself as the center of civilization. The Opium Wars revealed a fatal gap: China had not developed industrialized military technology, and it could not resist British steamships and rifles. The result was the "century of humiliation"—a series of unequal treaties that carved China into spheres of influence without formally colonizing it. China's experience is the AP's primary example of a powerful non-Western state losing sovereignty through economic imperialism (pp. 408–409).

**India (British East India Company, then British Crown)**
India underwent the full spectrum from economic to political imperialism. The East India Company first dominated trade, then forced India into a raw-material economy (cotton, opium), and eventually became the colonial government. After the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the British Crown took direct control (the British Raj). India's transformation from the world's leading textile manufacturer to a raw cotton exporter for British mills is one of the AP's central examples of economic imperialism's destructive effects on local industry (pp. 407–408).

**Dutch East Indies (Indonesia)**
The [[Concept Notes/Dutch East India Company\|Dutch East India Company]] established colonial control over present-day Indonesia through the spice trade, then the Dutch government imposed the Culture System (1830), forcing farmers to grow export crops. The system generated massive profits for the Netherlands while causing food shortages in Java. Dutch Indonesia is the clearest case study of a colonial power using forced labor systems to restructure an agricultural economy (p. 408).

**Argentina**
The wealthiest nation in Latin America by WWI, built through British investment rather than colonization. British capital built Argentina's railroads, telegraph system, and port infrastructure. British entrepreneurs developed the Pampas for large-scale cattle ranching and farming. Argentina exemplifies "informal empire"—economic dominance without political control. Its prosperity was real but dependent: Argentina's economy was structured to serve British needs, not Argentine development (pp. 412).

**Egypt (under Muhammad Ali and successors)**
Cotton became 93% of Egyptian exports by the late 19th century. Egypt borrowed heavily from British and French banks to finance infrastructure. When Egypt defaulted, Britain occupied it militarily in 1882 and governed it as a de facto colony until 1952. Egypt is the textbook case of informal economic imperialism converting into formal political control through debt dependency (pp. 409–410).

**Central American "Banana Republics"**
Small countries including Guatemala, Honduras, and Costa Rica became economically dominated by the United Fruit Company (US) in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The company controlled vast landholdings, built private railroads, and used US government support to pressure local governments. The term "banana republic" entered the language as a descriptor for states where a foreign corporation has more power than the national government (p. 413).

## Key Companies

**[[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC)**
British joint-stock company (1600–1858) that began as a trading enterprise and became the colonial government of India. It commanded a private army, collected taxes, and waged wars. Its use of Indian-grown opium to balance the China trade created one of history's most harmful drug distribution systems. Dissolved after the 1857 rebellion; a model for later corporate imperialism (pp. 407–408).

**Dutch East India Company (VOC)**
Dutch trading company (1602–1799) that monopolized the Southeast Asian spice trade and established Dutch colonial control over Indonesia. One of the world's first corporations to issue stock to the public. At its peak commanded more ships than any European nation-state. Dissolved in 1799 when the Dutch government took direct colonial control (p. 408).

**United Fruit Company**
American corporation (est. 1899) that came to control banana production, railroads, and telegraph systems across Central America. Allied with large landowners to pressure governments; worked with the US State Department to intervene militarily when its interests were threatened. The clearest US example of a private company functioning as an agent of economic imperialism (p. 413).

> [!tip]
> **Company comparison:** East India Company (1600s–1850s) = corporate imperialism that became territorial colonization. United Fruit Company (1900s) = corporate imperialism that used political leverage without formal colonization. Both show how private corporations can drive imperial expansion.
