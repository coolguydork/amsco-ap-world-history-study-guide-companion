# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 6.5 — Economic [[Concept Notes/Imperialism\|Imperialism]] | AMSCO pp. 407–416**

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves asking you to distinguish economic from political imperialism. Know the definitions cold, and have a specific example ready for each region (Asia, Africa, Latin America).

---

**economic imperialism**
Control of a weaker nation's economy by a stronger nation without establishing formal political colonial rule. Foreign business interests dominate natural resources, labor, and markets through investment, trade agreements, and debt. British investment in Argentina and US corporate control in Central America are the clearest examples. Economic imperialism often preceded—and sometimes prevented the need for—formal colonization (AMSCO p. 407).

**cash crop**
An agricultural product grown primarily for export and sale rather than local consumption. Colonial powers systematically shifted subject economies from food crops to cash crops: cotton in Egypt and India, cocoa on the Gold Coast, palm oil in West Africa, rubber in Brazil. Single-commodity dependence left colonies vulnerable to global price swings and undermined food security, causing famines even while crops were being exported (pp. 409–411).

**Opium War**
Two conflicts between Britain and China—First (1839–1842) and Second (1856–1860)—fought over the illegal opium trade. The British East India Company had been selling opium to China to offset a massive trade deficit. When Lin Zexu destroyed British opium stocks (1839), Britain responded militarily. Chinese defeat exposed the military gap between industrialized and non-industrialized states, and forced the "unequal treaties" (pp. 408–409).

**Treaty of Nanking**
The 1842 treaty ending the First Opium War. China was forced to open five treaty ports to foreign trade, cede Hong Kong to Britain, and pay large war reparations. The first of China's "unequal treaties," it established the template: industrialized powers could use military force to extract trading privileges from non-industrialized states (p. 409).

**spheres of influence**
Zones within a nominally independent country where a foreign power holds exclusive trading rights. After the Opium Wars, Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, and the United States each carved out spheres of influence in China. China remained technically independent but had ceded effective economic sovereignty to multiple imperial powers simultaneously (p. 409).

**[[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]]**
British joint-stock company (chartered 1600) that evolved from a trading firm into a de facto colonial government controlling India. Began in the spice trade, shifted to textiles, and then forced Indian farmers to grow opium for export to China. Its private army grew to 20,000+ Indian soldiers. Dissolved in 1858 after the Indian Rebellion of 1857, when the British Crown took direct control. The historical model of corporate imperialism becoming territorial conquest (pp. 407–408).

**Spice Islands**
The Maluku Islands (Moluccas) of present-day Indonesia, source of nutmeg, cloves, and mace. The primary prize in early European competition for Asian trade. The [[Concept Notes/Dutch East India Company\|Dutch East India Company]] gained a monopoly over the spice trade here, establishing Dutch colonial control over what became Indonesia. Their value drove both Dutch and English colonial expansion into Asia (p. 408).

**Dutch East India Company**
Dutch trading company (VOC; chartered 1602) that dominated the Southeast Asian spice trade and established colonial control over present-day Indonesia. One of history's first multinational corporations. By the late 18th century it had shifted from shipping to agricultural production. The Dutch government dissolved it in 1799 and took direct control of the Dutch East Indies, later imposing the Culture System.

**Culture System**
Dutch colonial policy imposed on Java (Indonesia) in 1830. Farmers had to devote one-fifth of their rice land to government-mandated export crops (sugar, coffee, indigo) or work government fields for 66 days per year if they held no land. Crop failures were the farmers' problem. Generated massive profits for the Dutch government while causing food shortages and famine in Java. Abolished in 1870 under humanitarian pressure (p. 408).

**corvée labor**
Compulsory unpaid labor required by colonial authorities as a form of taxation. Under the Dutch Culture System, Javanese villagers without land had to work government fields for 66 days per year. Similar forced labor systems operated across the colonial world—French corvée in West Africa, road-building in Belgian Congo. A key mechanism of economic extraction without wages.

**opium**
Addictive narcotic derived from poppy plants grown in South Asia. The British East India Company sold opium to China to offset the trade imbalance created by British demand for Chinese goods. Millions of Chinese became addicted. China's repeated attempts to suppress the trade triggered both Opium Wars. Britain's use of a drug to destabilize a rival civilization is one of the AP exam's clearest examples of economic imperialism's human costs (pp. 407–409).

**banana republics**
Term coined by writer O. Henry to describe small Central American countries whose governments were effectively controlled by foreign corporations like the United Fruit Company. Characterized by political instability, single-commodity dependence, and corporate manipulation of local governments using bribery and US military backing. A shorthand for the most aggressive form of US economic imperialism in Latin America (p. 413).

**Pampas**
The vast fertile grasslands of Argentina. British investors developed the Pampas for large-scale cattle ranching and cereal farming—improving breeding stock, building railroads, and financing infrastructure. Made Argentina one of the world's wealthiest nations by WWI. Represents the potential of British informal empire to create prosperity—though the profits flowed primarily to British investors (p. 412).

**Egypt**
Northeast African country whose economy was restructured around cotton as a cash crop. By the late 19th century cotton made up 93% of Egyptian exports. Dependence on cotton revenues made Egypt vulnerable to British and French creditors, ultimately triggering British military occupation in 1882. Egypt is the clearest example of how economic dependency under informal empire converted into formal colonization (pp. 409–410).

**Sudan**
Northeast African territory where cotton became the leading cash crop under British influence. The British-controlled Plantation Syndicate (a group of weaving companies) effectively dictated land use to Sudanese farmers. Combined with the Mahdist resistance movement (see [[Unit-6.3/Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview|Topic 6.3]]), Sudan illustrates how economic extraction and political resistance often overlapped.

**Uganda**
East African territory colonized by Britain in the 1890s. The British encouraged cotton as a cash crop, which quickly replaced enslaved people and ivory as the chief export. A clear example of how colonizers systematically restructured African economies around single commodities for European industrial needs (p. 410).

**Kenya**
East African British colony where the indigenous Kikuyu and other groups were displaced from fertile land to reserves with poor soil. Fertile Rift Valley land was given to white settlers, and Africans were legally prohibited from growing export cash crops like coffee and tea. The racial economic hierarchy of Kenya was among the most explicit in the colonial world (p. 410).

**Gold Coast**
British crown colony (1874) in West Africa, in present-day Ghana. After missionaries introduced cocoa in the 1880s, the Gold Coast became the world's largest cocoa producer. Its resources (cocoa, gold, palm oil) made it one of Britain's most profitable West African colonies. The Asante Empire resisted British control until 1901.

**Argentina**
South American country that became the richest nation in Latin America by WWI, largely through British investment. British entrepreneurs built the railroad network, telegraph system, and port of Buenos Aires. Up to 10% of all British foreign investment flowed to Argentina. Represents the model of economic imperialism without formal colonization—Britain controlled Argentina's economy without ever making it a colony (pp. 412).

> [!tip]
> **Key comparison for the exam:** India = political + economic imperialism (formal colonization + economic extraction). Argentina = economic imperialism only (investment and trade dominance without colonization). The AP may ask you to explain the difference or argue which was more damaging.
