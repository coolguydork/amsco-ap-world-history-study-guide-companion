## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: Lord Palmerston, British Foreign Secretary, Letter to the Minister of the Emperor of China, 1840*

> "It appeared that the Laws of the Chinese Empire forbid the importation of opium into China and declare that all Opium which may be brought into the country is liable to confiscation. The Queen of England desires that Her Subjects who may go into foreign countries should obey the Laws of those countries; and Her Majesty does not wish to protect them from the just consequences of any offenses which they may commit in foreign parts. But, on the other hand, Her Majesty cannot permit that Her Subjects residing abroad should be treated with violence, and be exposed to insult and injustice; and when wrong is done to them, Her Majesty will see that they obtain redress."

**1. What is the context of the letter above?**

A. Britain is seeking to gain permission for Christian missionaries to enter China.
B. Britain is upset that the Chinese government has denied British ships free trade privileges.
C. Britain has just defeated China in the First Opium War.
D. There is a civil war going on in China.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Palmerston wrote this letter in 1840, at the start of the First Opium War—before Britain's military victory. His complaint is that China seized British opium (illegally imported goods, from Britain's perspective) and arrested British subjects. The letter is Britain's formal protest of Chinese interference with what Britain considered its right to trade. Option C is wrong because the war had not yet been decided at the time of writing; A and D are not referenced in the passage.

**2. How did the Chinese government react to this letter?**

A. They continued to prevent the importation of opium.
B. They acquiesced and agreed to all its demands.
C. They allowed only Dutch and French ships to enter their ports.
D. They declared all European ships quarantined.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> China continued to resist—this letter did not resolve the dispute. The First Opium War (1839–1842) followed, and only after military defeat did China make concessions in the Treaty of Nanking. China never voluntarily agreed to Palmerston's demands; it was forced to accept them through military defeat.

**3. Which of the following is an accurate reflection of the relationship between China and Britain at the time of the passage?**

A. Britain knew China was a first-rate military power.
B. Britain viewed China as an economic superpower.
C. China and England both sought the support of Japan.
D. China was unaware of Britain's military power.

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The Qing Dynasty had not anticipated the military capability that industrialization had given Britain. China had not followed the Industrial Revolution closely and did not understand how dramatically British steamships and industrial weapons had shifted the military balance. Britain understood this gap; China did not—which is why China resisted militarily rather than negotiating. Options A and B contradict Britain's confident stance; C is anachronistic (Japan was not a relevant military power in 1840).

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: William Dalrymple, "The East India Company: The Original Corporate Raiders," The Guardian, 2015*

> "It appeared that the East India Company (EIC) ceased to be a conventional corporation, trading in silks and spices, and became something much more unusual. Within a few years, 250 company clerks backed by the military force of 20,000 locally recruited Indian soldiers had become the effective rulers of Bengal. An international corporation was transforming itself into an aggressive colonial power. . . . We still talk about the British conquering India, but that phrase disguises a more sinister reality. It was not the British government that seized India at the end of the 18th century, but a dangerously unregulated private company headquartered in one small office, five windows wide, in London, and managed in India by an unstable sociopath—[Robert] Clive. In many ways the EIC was a model of corporate efficiency: 100 years into its history, it had only 5 permanent employees in its head office. Nevertheless, that skeleton staff executed a corporate coup unparalleled in history: the military conquest, subjugation and plunder of vast tracts of southern Asia. It almost certainly remains the supreme act of corporate violence in world history."

**SAQ 1.**

**A. Identify Dalrymple's main point about British presence in India in the period 1750–1900.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Argumentation/Sourcing
> Dalrymple's main point is that British colonization of India was carried out not by the British government but by a private corporation—the East India Company—and that this corporate conquest was more ruthless and less accountable than traditional state colonialism. He argues that calling it "the British conquering India" obscures the more disturbing reality: a for-profit company with minimal oversight used private military force to plunder a continent. His implicit argument is that economic imperialism, when carried out by corporations rather than governments, lacks even the limited accountability of state colonialism.

**B. Explain ONE way in which the East India Company affected global economies in the period 1750–1900.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The East India Company restructured the Indian economy to serve British industrial needs. It forced Indian farmers to grow opium for export to China (to redress Britain's trade deficit with China), which created widespread addiction in China and triggered the Opium Wars. Simultaneously, it undermined India's domestic textile industry by flooding Indian markets with cheaper British machine-made cloth, transforming India from a manufacturing exporter into a raw-material supplier. Both effects demonstrate how a single corporation could reshape the economies of multiple continents.

**C. Explain what Dalrymple meant when he said that the East India Company committed a "supreme act of corporate violence" in India.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Sourcing and Contextualization
> Dalrymple means that the EIC's conquest of India combined corporate profit motive with military brutality in a way that was qualitatively different from ordinary state warfare. A private company—answerable to shareholders, not citizens—used military force to subjugate hundreds of millions of people, extract resources, and restructure an entire civilization to generate profits. Because the company was "dangerously unregulated," it faced no accountability to British voters or Parliament; it could use violence in pursuit of profit in ways a democratic government could not. The "violence" was not just physical but economic: the deliberate destruction of Indian manufacturing, the forced drug economy, and the extraction of wealth that impoverished the subcontinent.

**SAQ 2. (No Source)**

**A. Identify ONE way in which economic imperialism in the period 1750–1900 contributed to native resistance.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Economic imperialism's forced conversion of subsistence food economies into cash-crop export economies generated direct material grievances that fueled resistance. In the Dutch East Indies, the Culture System forced Javanese farmers to grow sugar and coffee for Dutch profit rather than rice for their families, causing food shortages. This exploitation, combined with the absence of any political voice, drove resistance movements. Similarly, in China, the British-forced opium trade—which was both economically draining (silver leaving China) and socially destructive (mass addiction)—generated the moral outrage that motivated Lin Zexu's resistance and later Chinese nationalist sentiment.

**B. Explain ONE way in which the relationship between companies and industrialized states impacted colonized states.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The partnership between private corporations and industrialized governments gave companies the military backing they needed to operate in unwilling markets, while governments used companies to project power at minimal cost. In Central America, the United Fruit Company leveraged the US government's Monroe Doctrine and its own lobbying power to pressure local governments into maintaining favorable conditions—low taxes, land grants, suppression of labor organizing. When governments resisted, US military intervention followed. Colonized and semi-colonized states thus faced not just a corporation's economic power but the full military-diplomatic apparatus of a major industrial state operating on the corporation's behalf.

**C. Explain ONE reason for the shift in agricultural influence from Asia or Latin America to industrialized states.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The Industrial Revolution gave industrialized states the technological and financial tools to restructure agricultural production in other regions to serve their needs. Specifically, the combination of railroads, steamships, and access to capital markets allowed British investors to penetrate the interior of Latin America, build the transportation infrastructure needed to bring commodities to port, and connect those commodities to global markets. Without railroads, Argentine beef and grain could not reach Buenos Aires efficiently; without steamships, they could not reach Britain profitably. The infrastructure investment that shifted agricultural power was itself a form of economic imperialism: it integrated Argentine agriculture into the British economy while creating permanent dependency on British capital and technology.
