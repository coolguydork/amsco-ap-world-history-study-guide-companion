# Themes and Causation — Chapter 20 (4.7): Changing Social Hierarchies

#apwh #unit-4 #period-4

---

## Social Structures

The most significant social change of this period was the emergence of race as a primary organizing principle of social hierarchy in the Atlantic world. Pre-colonial societies had social hierarchies based on class, religion, ethnicity, and gender — but the systematic, heritable, legally codified racialization of slavery was new. The casta system formalized dozens of racial categories in Spanish colonial law, creating a hierarchy in which a person's ancestry determined their legal rights, occupational opportunities, tax obligations, and social status. This system was not merely descriptive — it was actively enforced through church records, legal documents, and social practice. Its legacy shaped Latin American social structures well into the 20th century.

---

## Economic Systems

Social hierarchies in this period were inseparable from economic systems. The casta system existed to organize labor: peninsulares and creoles managed enterprises; mestizos and Indigenous peoples worked in agriculture, mining, and domestic service; enslaved Africans labored on plantations. Each category had different labor obligations and economic rights. The racial character of chattel slavery — in which enslaved status was permanent and heritable — served a specific economic function: it created a self-reproducing labor force for plantation agriculture without requiring constant fresh imports. Social hierarchy was, at its core, a labor management system.

---

## Cultural Developments

Colonial social hierarchies generated both cultural suppression and unexpected creativity. Indigenous and African cultural practices were officially suppressed but survived in syncretic religious and artistic forms — colonial baroque art, the cult of the Virgin of Guadalupe, Candomblé, and Vodou all reflect creative responses to oppression. Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's poetry navigated the constraints of colonial gender hierarchy while producing literature of enduring significance. The social hierarchy also produced distinctive literary genres: casta paintings (visual representations of racial mixing), *relaciones* (colonial administrative reports), and hybrid Indigenous-Spanish chronicles.

---

## Gender

Colonial encounters transformed gender hierarchies in complex ways. Indigenous and African women were subject to new forms of sexual exploitation and domestic service that colonial systems did not officially protect against. At the same time, the social disruption of conquest created some spaces where women exercised unusual authority — doña Marina as diplomatic interpreter, Queen Nzinga as military and political leader, Sor Juana as intellectual. Gender hierarchy interacted with racial hierarchy: elite creole women had more options than enslaved African women, but both were constrained by colonial patriarchy in ways that European men were not.

> The **intersection of race and gender** in colonial society is a sophisticated analytical point that earns AP complexity credit. Practice explaining how enslaved African women, Indigenous women, and creole women experienced the same colonial system differently based on their racial classification.
