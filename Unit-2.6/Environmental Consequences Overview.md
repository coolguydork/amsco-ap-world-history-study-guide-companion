# Environmental Consequences of Connectivity — Overview

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 2.6 has **one AP Learning Objective**: explain the environmental effects of exchange networks in Afro-Eurasia, c. 1200–1450. Every exam question on this topic will revolve around **agricultural diffusion**, **environmental degradation**, or **disease spread** — usually the [[Concept Notes/Black Death\|Black Death]].

## What This Topic Is About

The same trade networks that carried silk, spices, and ideas across Afro-Eurasia also carried seeds, soil degradation, and infectious disease. Topic 2.6 examines three interconnected environmental consequences of increased commercial connectivity:

1. **Agricultural diffusion** — crops migrating along trade and tribute routes, boosting food production and population growth
2. **Environmental degradation** — overgrazing, deforestation, soil erosion, and climate change straining the land
3. **Epidemic disease** — the Black Death traveling trade corridors to devastate Eurasian populations

## The Big Picture

Between c. 1200 and c. 1450, trade integration reached new heights — but connectivity is a double-edged sword. The same routes that brought Champa rice to China and bananas to sub-Saharan Africa also transmitted bubonic plague from Central Asia to Europe. The environmental consequences of this era reordered populations, economies, and political systems across the globe. (AMSCO pp. 121–123)

## Agricultural Diffusion

Merchants and conquerors moved crops into new regions, transforming land use and supporting population growth.

**Champa rice** — Originating in Vietnam (and possibly India given the Hindu influence on Champa), this drought-resistant, flood-resistant rice variety was introduced to China as a tribute good. It could yield two crops per year, enabling terraced farming in uplands and paddy farming in lowlands. It drove China's population boom and southward urban migration. (AMSCO p. 121)

**Bananas** — Indonesian seafarers introduced bananas to sub-Saharan Africa during Indian Ocean voyages. Rich in nutrition, bananas allowed Bantu-speaking peoples to migrate into regions where yams — their traditional staple — could not grow, supporting population growth across equatorial Africa. Indonesians also settled Madagascar. (AMSCO p. 122)

**Sugar, cotton, and citrus** — As caliphs expanded the Dar al-Islam, they spread cultivation of these crops across the Islamic world. The markets of Samarkand distributed citrus and rice products from Southwest Asia into Europe. Demand for sugar would later fuel the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] of the 1500s. (AMSCO p. 122)

## Environmental Degradation

Population growth fueled by agricultural expansion also pushed resources past their limits:

- **Great Zimbabwe** — overgrazing outside the city was so severe that the population had to abandon it in the late 1400s. (See [[Unit-1.5/Africa Overview|Topic 1.5]] for Great Zimbabwe's rise.)
- **Feudal Europe** — overuse of farmland and deforestation led to soil erosion, steadily reducing agricultural output. (AMSCO p. 123)
- **Little Ice Age (c. 1300–c. 1800)** — a period of cooling that decreased agricultural yields, compounding population stress across Eurasia.
- **Maya collapse** — environmental degradation was also a factor in the decline of the Maya in the Americas.

## Spread of the Black Death

No environmental consequence of connectivity was more dramatic than epidemic disease. Bubonic plague (the **Black Death**) originated in southern China and spread via [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongol]] trade corridors into Central Asia, then west to Southeast Asia, Persia, Egypt, and Europe. Historians believe caravanserais — where travelers and animals sheltered together — helped transmit the infected fleas that carried the disease.

| Region | Impact | Dates |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Asia (China & Central Asia) | ~25 million dead | 1332–1347 |
| Europe | ~1/3 of population killed | 1347–1351 |
| North Africa | Severe loss of life | 1340s–1350s |
| Sub-Saharan Africa & South Asia | Largely spared — few trading ports | — |

The Black Death's demographic collapse had profound economic consequences: with fewer workers, labor became more valuable. Workers could demand higher wages, helping erode the [[Concept Notes/Feudalism|feudal system]]. (AMSCO p. 123)

> [!tip]
> The Black Death traveled along the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]] and maritime routes — the same networks that carried the [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]]'s goods and ideas. Connectivity enabled both prosperity and catastrophe.
