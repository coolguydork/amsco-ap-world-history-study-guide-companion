# Unit 2.6: Key People and Groups — Environmental Consequences of Connectivity

#apwh #unit-2 #period-1

## People and Groups

### Mongol Armies and Administrators (as a group)
**Who:** The military and administrative forces of the Mongol Empire, which by the mid-13th century controlled territory from China to Eastern Europe.
**AP Significance:**
- Mongol armies and the trade networks they protected (the Pax Mongolica) created the conditions for plague to travel rapidly from its Central Asian reservoir westward.
- The siege of Caffa (Crimea) in 1346 is a famous — though debated — early account of biological warfare: Mongol forces allegedly catapulted plague-infected corpses over the city walls, infecting Genoese merchants who then carried the disease back to Europe.
- The Mongol Empire's inadvertent role in transmitting the Black Death illustrates how the same infrastructure that enables prosperity can enable catastrophe — a key AP theme of unintended consequences.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Mongols did not *cause* plague — they did not create the pathogen. But their road networks and the commercial activity they encouraged created the conditions for rapid, wide-scale transmission. This is an important distinction for AP causal analysis.

---

### Venetian and Genoese Merchants (as a group)
**Who:** Italian merchant communities from Venice and Genoa who dominated Mediterranean and Black Sea trade in the 13th–14th centuries.
**AP Significance:**
- Genoese merchants operating at the Black Sea port of Caffa are credited with bringing plague to the Mediterranean in 1347 when their ships returned to Sicily and other Italian ports.
- Italian merchants' extensive trade networks meant that once plague arrived in Italian port cities, it spread rapidly through the dense urban populations and onward to the rest of Europe.
- Their role illustrates how the merchant class — the engine of trade network prosperity — was also the primary vector of plague transmission into Europe.

---

### Giovanni Boccaccio (1313–1375)
**Who:** Italian author who survived the Black Death in Florence and wrote the *Decameron* (c. 1353), a collection of 100 stories framed as tales told by young Florentines fleeing the plague.
**AP Significance:**
- The *Decameron*'s introduction is one of the most vivid primary source accounts of the Black Death's social impact: the breakdown of family ties, the abandonment of the sick, the collapse of social norms, and the scale of death.
- Boccaccio's account illustrates how demographic catastrophe disrupts social structures and cultural norms — a key AP theme.
- His perspective as an educated Italian man witnessing plague in a major commercial city gives his account particular value for understanding urban impacts of the Black Death.

**Key Quote (paraphrase):** "Father abandoned child, wife abandoned husband... people died in the streets for lack of care... the plague showed no mercy to the rich or the powerful."

---

### Flagellant Movements (as a group)
**Who:** Religious zealots who publicly whipped themselves in processions across Europe during the Black Death, believing that self-inflicted suffering would appease a God who was punishing humanity.
**AP Significance:**
- Flagellants represent the extreme end of European religious responses to the Black Death — the belief that the plague was divine punishment for human sin.
- Their movement spread rapidly across Germany and the Low Countries (1349–1350), partly because it offered a sense of human agency in the face of uncontrollable catastrophe.
- Church authorities ultimately condemned the flagellant movement as heretical, illustrating how the Black Death strained the relationship between populations and established religious institutions.
- The movement's spread itself was an inadvertent vector of plague transmission, as large gatherings of flagellants from different regions mixed populations and potentially spread disease.

---

### Surviving Peasants and Urban Workers (as a group)
**Who:** The laborers — agricultural serfs, urban artisans, servants — who survived the Black Death and found themselves in radically changed labor markets.
**AP Significance:**
- Labor scarcity following the death of 30–50% of the European workforce gave surviving workers unprecedented economic leverage: they could demand higher wages, better conditions, or freedom from serfdom.
- In England, the Statute of Laborers (1351) attempted to cap wages at pre-plague levels — the government's recognition that workers were demanding more — but was largely unenforceable.
- The Peasants' Revolt in England (1381) and similar uprisings across Europe were partly driven by tensions between laborers' new expectations and lords' attempts to restore pre-plague conditions.
- This group represents how demographic change creates social and political change — a key AP causal argument.

---

### Chinese Rice Farmers (as a group)
**Who:** Agricultural communities in southern China who cultivated and spread new strains of fast-ripening rice (Champa rice, originally from Southeast Asia) along trade and communication routes.
**AP Significance:**
- Chinese farmers' adoption and spread of Champa rice (which arrived via Southeast Asian trade routes before our period) represents the positive side of ecological exchange: a crop diffusion that dramatically increased food production, supported population growth, and enabled urbanization.
- The rapid population growth supported by agricultural intensification in China before the Mongol conquests and plague made subsequent demographic losses even more catastrophic.
- Rice cultivation's spread via trade routes illustrates intentional crop diffusion — a contrast to the *unintentional* disease transmission that defines the Black Death story.

> [!exam-tip]
> When asked about environmental consequences of trade, remember to cover BOTH positive (crop diffusion, agricultural expansion) and negative (disease transmission, deforestation) consequences. One-sided answers lose complexity points on the AP exam.
