# Key Terms & Concepts

> [!exam-tip]
> AMSCO groups these terms under three ENV sub-themes: **Crop Diffusion**, **Degradation**, and **Disease**. Know which category each term belongs to — AP MCQs frequently test you on cause-and-effect chains within these categories.

## Crop Diffusion

**Champa rice**
A quick-ripening, drought-resistant, flood-resistant rice variety originating in Vietnam (influenced by Hindu Champa states) and offered to China as tribute. It could yield two crops per year, enabled farming on previously unusable upland terraces and lowland paddies, and fueled China's population boom. (AMSCO p. 121)

**Bananas**
Introduced to sub-Saharan Africa by Indonesian seafarers crossing the Indian Ocean. Nutritious and easy to cultivate in equatorial climates, bananas allowed [[Concept Notes/Indian Ocean Trade Network|Bantu-speaking peoples]] to migrate into regions where their traditional food staple (yams) could not grow, boosting population and agricultural land use. (AMSCO p. 122)

**Sugar**
Spread across the Islamic world and into Europe via Dar al-Islam trade networks. European demand for sugar grew so high it became a driving force behind the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] of the 1500s. (AMSCO p. 122)

**Citrus crops**
Citrus fruits (lemons, oranges, limes) spread via Islamic trade networks from Southwest Asia into the Mediterranean basin and Europe, diversifying diets and trade goods. (AMSCO p. 121)

**Cotton**
Also diffused through Islamic networks, cotton cultivation expanded textile production across the Mediterranean and Middle East. (AMSCO p. 122)

**Agricultural diffusion**
The process by which crops, farming techniques, and agricultural knowledge spread across regions through trade, tribute, conquest, and migration. One of the most significant environmental consequences of increased commercial connectivity in this era.

## Environmental Degradation

**Overgrazing**
Excessive livestock grazing that strips vegetation from land, causes soil compaction, and leads to desertification. Most notable AP example: overgrazing around **Great Zimbabwe** forced abandonment of the city in the late 1400s. (AMSCO p. 123)

**Deforestation**
Clearing of forests for agricultural land and fuel, which destabilizes soil and leads to erosion. A chronic problem in feudal Europe as population pressure pushed farming onto marginal lands. (AMSCO p. 123)

**Soil erosion**
Loss of fertile topsoil caused by deforestation and overuse of farmland. Reduced agricultural productivity and contributed to food shortages in Europe. (AMSCO p. 123)

**Little Ice Age (c. 1300–c. 1800)**
A prolonged cooling period that reduced agricultural output across Europe and other parts of Eurasia, compounding the population stress caused by the [[Concept Notes/Black Death\|Black Death]]. (AMSCO p. 123)

> [!warning]
> The Little Ice Age and the Black Death are distinct but overlapping stresses on Europe, c. 1300–1350. Don't conflate them — the Little Ice Age is climate-driven; the Black Death is disease-driven. Both reduced agricultural output, but through different mechanisms.

## Disease

**Bubonic plague (Black Death)**
A bacterial disease caused by *Yersinia pestis*, transmitted by fleas living on rodents. Originated in southern China, spread along [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongol]] trade corridors through Central Asia, and reached Europe via the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]] and Mediterranean sea routes in 1347. Killed an estimated 75–200 million people across Eurasia. In Europe alone, it killed roughly one-third of the population between 1347 and 1351. (AMSCO pp. 121, 123)

**Caravanserai**
Rest stops along overland trade routes where merchants and animals sheltered together overnight. Historians believe caravanserais contributed to plague transmission because animals likely carried the infected fleas that spread *Y. pestis*. (AMSCO p. 123)

**Demographic collapse**
A sharp decline in population caused by epidemic disease, famine, or other catastrophic events. The Black Death's demographic collapse in Europe had a transformative economic effect: fewer workers made labor scarce, driving up wages and weakening the [[Concept Notes/Feudalism|feudal system]]. (AMSCO p. 123)
