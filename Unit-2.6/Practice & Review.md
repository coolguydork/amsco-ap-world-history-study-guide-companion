# Practice & Review

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO AP World History, pp. 124–125*

The following questions refer to the map: **Spread of the Black Death in Europe, 1346–1353**. The map shows plague reaching Western Europe from the east along maritime and overland trade routes, with dated progressions from 1346 (Crimea/Black Sea) through 1353 (northeastern Europe).

---

**Question 1.** Which explanation for the cause of the rapid spread of the Black Death is best supported by this map?

(A) The migrations of Central Asians into eastern Europe
(B) The spread of rodents through trade
(C) Pollution caused by growing concentrations of people in cities
(D) Poverty among Europeans resulting from feudalism

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The map shows plague spreading along **trade routes** (both maritime and overland), which carried the rats and fleas that transmitted *Y. pestis*. (A) is wrong — the map shows disease movement, not migration of peoples. (C) is wrong — urban pollution is not shown and doesn't explain the geographic pattern. (D) is wrong — feudal poverty is internal to Europe and can't explain why plague entered from the east via trade routes.

---

**Question 2.** One significant long-term impact of the Black Death was

(A) the end of the Indian Ocean as a viable trade network
(B) the decline of the feudal system in Europe
(C) the increased use of camels in the Silk Roads trade
(D) the rise of the Ottoman Turks

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Black Death killed ~1/3 of Europe's population, creating labor scarcity. Workers could demand higher wages, weakening lords' economic control over serfs — the core of the feudal system. (A) is wrong — the Indian Ocean network was not destroyed; it actually recovered and expanded. (C) is wrong — camel use predates the plague and is not a consequence of it. (D) is wrong — Ottoman rise is a separate development not directly caused by the Black Death.

---

**Question 3.** Which of the following statements does the map support most clearly?

(A) Muscovy traded extensively with Western Europe.
(B) Africa never experienced the Black Death.
(C) The Black Death entered Western Europe through the Mediterranean Sea.
(D) The strain of the Black Death that moved along the Indian Ocean was not fatal.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The map shows 1347 reaching Italy (Sicily, Genoa) and spreading northward — consistent with maritime entry through the Mediterranean. (A) is not clearly supported by the map. (B) is too absolute — North Africa did experience the plague; the map simply focuses on Europe. (D) cannot be inferred from a map of European spread.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO AP World History, p. 125*

**Using the excerpts below (Ibn Khaldun, Ibn Battuta, Gabriele de Mussis, Gilles li Muisis):**

**(A)** Identify ONE pattern the excerpts show about the Black Death.

> [!tip] Model Response — Pattern Identification
> A pattern across all four excerpts is that the Black Death caused catastrophic mortality across multiple regions and cultures. Whether written by a North African historian (Ibn Khaldun), a Moroccan traveler in Damascus (Ibn Battuta), an Italian lawyer at Caffa (de Mussis), or a French churchman (li Muisis), all accounts describe mass death on a scale that overwhelmed societies — suggesting the plague's devastation was universal along trade-connected regions.

---

**(B)** Explain ONE way the Black Death spread during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The Black Death spread through Mongol-controlled trade corridors along the Silk Roads. The Mongol conquests of the 1200s–1300s created an integrated overland trade network from China to the Black Sea. As merchants and their animals traveled these routes, they carried the fleas hosting *Yersinia pestis*. Caravanserais — rest stops where humans and animals sheltered together overnight — likely accelerated transmission. De Mussis's account of plague at Caffa (a Black Sea trading port) illustrates this: the disease arrived with Mongol forces on the steppe and entered European maritime networks through that port.

---

**(C)** Compare ONE report of the plague's impact from an outside source to one of the passages above.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Boccaccio's *Decameron* (Italy, 1353) describes the same scale of horror that Ibn Khaldun records from North Africa: "Dead bodies filled every corner," Boccaccio wrote, echoing Khaldun's observation that "cities and buildings were laid waste." Both emphasize civilizational disruption, not just individual deaths. The similarity across a Christian Italian and a Muslim North African source — writing independently — strengthens the argument that the plague's devastation was a genuinely global phenomenon along trade-connected zones, not a regional or culturally specific experience.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

**(1)** In 1–3 paragraphs, explain some of the environmental effects of trade in Afro-Eurasia from c. 1200 to c. 1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> Trade networks in Afro-Eurasia between c. 1200 and c. 1450 produced significant environmental consequences through three main mechanisms: crop diffusion, environmental degradation, and disease transmission.
>
> Agricultural diffusion was perhaps the most positive consequence. Champa rice, introduced to China from Vietnam through the tribute system, transformed Chinese land use: terraced upland farms and lowland paddies expanded rice production dramatically, fueling population growth. Indonesian seafarers introduced bananas to sub-Saharan Africa, enabling Bantu-speaking peoples to settle regions where yams could not grow. Islamic merchants spread sugar, cotton, and citrus from Southwest Asia into the Mediterranean world. In each case, trade moved crops across ecological zones, boosting food production and population.
>
> However, population growth driven by agricultural diffusion also accelerated environmental degradation. Overgrazing around Great Zimbabwe became so severe the city was abandoned in the late 1400s. Deforestation and soil erosion reduced agricultural output in feudal Europe, compounded by the onset of the Little Ice Age (c. 1300). Most dramatically, the same trade routes that spread crops also spread bubonic plague. The Black Death traveled Mongol trade corridors from southern China westward, killing ~25 million Asians between 1332–1347 and roughly one-third of Europe's population between 1347–1351. The demographic collapse weakened feudalism as labor scarcity raised wages — demonstrating that the environmental consequences of connectivity could reshape political and economic systems, not just landscapes.

## Think As a Historian — Causation Cycle

*AMSCO prompt (p. 126): Describe a cycle of cause and effect related to increased **demand for goods** (rather than food). Then analyze cause-and-effect connections between cultures in transregional exchange networks, c. 1200–1450.*

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation Cycle (Demand for Goods)
> Increased demand for luxury goods — silk, spices, and porcelain — drove expansion of the Silk Roads and Indian Ocean networks. More trade routes required more caravanserais, more ports, and more ships, which increased the movement of people and animals across regions. This connectivity enabled the transmission of diseases like bubonic plague. The resulting demographic collapse reduced consumer populations and disrupted demand, temporarily contracting trade. However, surviving merchants and the newly wage-earning working class eventually rebuilt demand, and trade networks expanded again — repeating the cycle at a new scale. Between cultures: Chinese demand for spices connected East Asia to Southeast Asia and South Asia via the Indian Ocean; Islamic merchants redistributed goods into Europe; European demand for sugar would eventually fuel Atlantic colonization — each link creating new environmental pressures.
