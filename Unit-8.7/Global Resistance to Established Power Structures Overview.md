# Global Resistance to Established Power Structures

**AMSCO pp. 599–607 | AP Period 4 | Themes: GOV, CDI, SIO**

**Essential Question:** What were differing reactions to existing power structures after 1900?

## The Big Picture

The 20th century was not only an age of world wars and superpower rivalry — it was an age of resistance. People around the globe challenged established power structures through starkly different means: Gandhi's nonviolent fasts in India, MLK's marches in Alabama, Hungarian students tearing down Stalin's statue in Budapest, and Shining Path guerrillas bombing Peruvian cities. What unites these disparate movements is the AP exam's central question: **How and why did individuals and groups challenge authority, and with what results?**

Three broad patterns emerge. First, **nonviolent resistance** proved remarkably effective when leaders could mobilize mass public support and shame colonial or authoritarian regimes internationally (Gandhi, MLK, Mandela). Second, **armed resistance and terrorism** — from the IRA in Northern Ireland to al-Qaeda globally — destabilized states but rarely achieved lasting political goals through violence alone. Third, **state repression** by militarized governments (Franco's Spain, Idi Amin's Uganda) often intensified rather than resolved conflicts, exposing the limits of authoritarian control.

The **1968 global wave of protest** is a high-frequency exam moment: student uprisings in France, Czechoslovakia, the US, Mexico, Japan, and Poland all erupted simultaneously, driven by shared grievances about authoritarianism, war, and inequality — evidence of global interconnection in political culture.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves comparing **methods of resistance**: nonviolent (Gandhi, MLK, Mandela) vs. violent/terrorist (IRA, ETA, Shining Path, al-Qaeda). Be able to explain why different contexts produced different strategies. For SAQs, connect 8.7 to 8.5 (decolonization movements) and 8.3 (Cold War proxy conflicts).

## Nonviolent Resistance

The most celebrated resistance movements of the 20th century succeeded through mass civil disobedience. **[[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]]** (covered in Topic 8.5) pioneered the model: marches, boycotts, and hunger strikes against British colonial rule led to Indian independence in 1947. His model directly influenced **Martin Luther King Jr.**, who applied it in the US Civil Rights Movement of the 1950s–1960s. King organized bus boycotts (Montgomery, 1955–56), massive marches (March on Washington, 1963), and voter registration drives, ultimately winning the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965. **Nelson Mandela** led the African National Congress's nonviolent campaign against apartheid in South Africa — though he also endorsed limited sabotage early in his career — and spent 27 years in prison before becoming South Africa's first democratically elected Black president in 1994.

> [!tip]
> **Nonviolent resistance worked best when:** (1) the resistance could generate international media attention and moral pressure; (2) the oppressive regime was democratic or cared about its international image; (3) leaders maintained discipline and mass participation. Compare Gandhi vs. IRA — same colonial grievance structure, very different tactics and outcomes.

## Challenges to Soviet Power in Eastern Europe

Eastern European reform movements tested the limits of Soviet tolerance. In **Poland (1956)**, workers' protests brought Wladyslaw Gomulka to power — he won limited autonomy from Moscow but maintained communist rule and kept Soviet troops in Poland. In **Hungary (1956)**, the more radical Imre Nagy declared Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact and neutrality in the Cold War. Soviet forces invaded, captured and executed Nagy, and crushed the revolt. In **Czechoslovakia (1968)**, Alexander Dubček's "Prague Spring" liberalized speech and the press — the Soviets again invaded, applying the **Brezhnev Doctrine**: Moscow would intervene militarily in any socialist country where communism was threatened. These failures defined the ceiling of reform possible within the Soviet bloc until Gorbachev abandoned the doctrine in the late 1980s.

> [!warning]
> Students often confuse the **1956 Hungarian Revolution** (crushed by Soviets; Nagy executed) with the **1968 Prague Spring** (also crushed; Dubček removed but not killed). Both demonstrate the Brezhnev Doctrine in practice, though the doctrine was not formally articulated until 1968.

## 1968: The Year of Global Revolt

1968 was the single most explosive year of resistance in the 20th century. Simultaneously: Paris (massive student strikes + 10 million workers' general strike, the largest in French history), Prague (Prague Spring crushed by Warsaw Pact), Mexico City (military suppresses student protests two months before the Olympics), the US (antiwar protests, Kent State shootings in 1970), Japan, Poland, Brazil, Yugoslavia, and Northern Ireland. The common thread was **youth radicalization** — post-WWII expansion of university education created large, politically engaged student populations who channeled frustrations over civil rights, Vietnam, and authoritarianism into collective action.

## Terrorism as Resistance

In the post-Cold War era, state-vs-state conflicts declined while non-state terrorism rose. Three key examples:

**Northern Ireland (The Troubles, c. 1968–1998):** Catholic nationalists (IRA) vs. Protestant unionists (Ulster Defence Association) produced ~3,500 deaths. The IRA used bombings in England to pressure Britain. A ceasefire was reached in 1994; the IRA renounced violence after the Good Friday Agreement (1998).

**Spain — ETA:** The Basque Homeland and Freedom organization (founded 1959) sought Basque independence through bombings and assassinations (800+ dead), including the 1973 killing of Franco's designated successor. Declared a ceasefire in 2011; dissolved 2018.

**Peru — Shining Path:** Founded by philosophy professor Abimael Guzmán on Maoist ideology, Shining Path waged a brutal insurgency from 1980, killing an estimated 70,000 people. Guzmán was captured in 1992; the movement gradually collapsed.

**Islamic terrorism:** Groups including al-Qaeda (financed by Osama bin Laden) carried out attacks globally, most dramatically the September 11, 2001 attacks in the US (nearly 3,000 killed). Other groups — ISIS/ISIL, Boko Haram, al-Shabaab, Taliban — used fundamentalist interpretations of Islam, though most victims were fellow Muslims.

> [!warning]
> The AP does not frame terrorism as legitimate resistance — frame it as a **method of political violence** used by non-state actors to destabilize governments. Focus on causes (political grievances, ideological frameworks) and effects (cycles of repression, domestic security responses, international coalitions).

## Militarized State Responses

Authoritarian states typically intensified conflicts. **Francisco Franco** (Spain, 1939–1975) executed, imprisoned, or sent hundreds of thousands of political dissidents to labor camps — yet opposition persisted, and Spain democratized after his death. **Idi Amin** (Uganda, 1971–1979) expelled 60,000 Asians, worsened ethnic tensions, and killed up to 500,000 people before being forced into exile by Tanzanian forces + Ugandan nationalists. Both cases show that pure repression fails to eliminate resistance and often delegitimizes regimes internationally.

## The Military-Industrial Complex

President Dwight Eisenhower coined the term **military-industrial complex** in 1961 to describe the growing interdependence of the US defense establishment and private weapons manufacturers. As defense industries expanded, so did political pressure to maintain spending — a structural driver of Cold War arms races. The international weapons trade expanded as states without domestic arms production purchased weapons from superpowers, intensifying regional conflicts worldwide.
