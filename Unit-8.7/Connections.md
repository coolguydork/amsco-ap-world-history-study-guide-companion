# Connections

**AMSCO pp. 599–607**

## Builds On

**[[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview\|Decolonization after 1900 Overview]]**
Gandhi's nonviolent model and Mandela's anti-apartheid campaign are introduced here as anticolonial / anti-racist resistance. Topic 8.7 extends those cases into a broader comparative framework of global resistance methods. The same leaders appear in both topics — 8.5 focuses on the outcome (independence, democracy); 8.7 focuses on the method (nonviolent vs. violent, and the conditions that favored each).

**[[Unit-8.2/Cold War Overview\|Cold War Overview]]**
The Brezhnev Doctrine and the crushing of Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968) are direct consequences of the superpower rivalry established in 8.2. Eastern European resistance to Soviet domination is a case study in the limits of Cold War containment from within the Soviet bloc. The military-industrial complex connects to Cold War arms buildup.

**[[Unit-8.3/Effects of the Cold War Overview\|Effects of the Cold War Overview]]**
Cold War proxy conflicts supplied weapons, funding, and ideological frameworks to resistance and terrorist movements. The international arms trade that intensified conflicts in the developing world is a direct effect of the military-industrial complex covered in 8.7.

**[[Unit-7.6/Causes of World War II Overview\|Causes of World War II Overview]]**
Francisco Franco came to power through the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), which is covered in Topic 7.6. His dictatorship provides the political context for ETA's emergence in 8.7. The interwar fascist movements that produced Franco connect 8.7 back to earlier authoritarian patterns.

## Leads To

**[[Unit-8.8/End of the Cold War Overview\|End of the Cold War Overview]] **
Gorbachev's abandonment of the Brezhnev Doctrine in the late 1980s directly enabled the 1989 revolutions in Eastern Europe — the same countries that had been crushed in 1956 and 1968. The long history of resistance documented in 8.7 finally succeeded when Moscow stopped sending tanks. The Prague Spring's Dubček was rehabilitated after the 1989 Velvet Revolution.

**Topic 9.x — Contemporary Challenges**
Post-9/11 terrorism, the "War on Terror," and ongoing global extremism are the direct legacy of al-Qaeda's emergence, covered in 8.7. The tools states developed to fight terrorism (surveillance, military intervention, international coalitions) define 21st-century geopolitics.

## Compare With

**[[Unit-6.8/Causation in the Imperial Age Overview\|Causation in the Imperial Age Overview]]**
The labor movement and socialist resistance to industrial capitalism (19th century) parallels the ideological resistance to political power structures in 8.7. Both involve non-elite groups organizing against established hierarchies, though the methods and targets differ. Compare also: Luddite machine-breaking (violent) vs. trade union organizing (legal/institutional) — similar violence-vs-legal-means spectrum.

**Topic 4.3 — Columbian Exchange / Topic 5.x — [[Concept Notes/Industrialization\|Industrialization]]**
Earlier periods also feature resistance to established power — slave revolts, anticolonial uprisings (Haitian Revolution), indigenous resistance. Compare methods and outcomes across periods: what changed about resistance strategies in the 20th century? (Answer: mass media, international law, decolonization framework, Cold War patron funding.)
