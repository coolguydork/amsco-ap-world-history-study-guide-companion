# Practice & Review

**AMSCO pp. 605–607**

## Multiple-Choice Practice

*Source: Bobby Sands, who died in a prison hunger strike in Belfast in 1981:*

> "I am a political prisoner. I am a political prisoner because I am a casualty of a perennial war that is being fought between the oppressed Irish people and an alien, oppressive, unwanted regime that refuses to withdraw from our land. . . . I believe and stand by the God-given right of the Irish nation to sovereign independence, and the right of any Irishman or woman to assert this right in armed revolution. That is why I am incarcerated, naked and tortured. . . . Foremost in my tortured mind is the thought that there can never be peace in Ireland until the foreign, oppressive British presence is removed, leaving all the Irish people as a unit to control their own affairs and determine their own destinies as a sovereign people, free in mind and body, separate and distinct physically, culturally and economically."

**1. How did the author of the above passage view himself?**

A. As a terrorist
B. As a freedom fighter
C. As a victim of terrorism
D. As a political theorist

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Sands frames himself as a "casualty" of war fighting for Irish independence — the language of a freedom fighter, not a terrorist. He explicitly invokes the "God-given right" of Irish self-determination and calls British rule "alien" and "oppressive," constructing a nationalist liberation narrative. This illustrates how the same individual can be framed very differently depending on point of view — a key AP historical thinking skill (HAPP/POV).

**2. What organization was the author most likely affiliated with?**

A. Ulster Defence Association
B. Basque Homeland and Freedom (ETA)
C. Irish Republican Army (IRA)
D. Royal Irish Constabulary Special Reserve

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Sands advocates for removing the "British presence" from Ireland and Irish unification — the IRA's stated goal. The Ulster Defence Association was Protestant/loyalist (opposite side). ETA is Spanish/Basque. The Royal Irish Constabulary was a British colonial police force.

**3. Was the conflict in Northern Ireland ever resolved?**

A. A ceasefire was reached, and the IRA renounced violence.
B. The British withdrew from Northern Ireland.
C. The violence continues but less intensely.
D. A wall was built between Northern Ireland and the Irish Republic.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The Good Friday Agreement (1998) established a ceasefire and power-sharing government. The British did NOT withdraw — Northern Ireland remains part of the United Kingdom. The IRA renounced violence and decommissioned weapons. Answer B is the IRA's goal, not the outcome; C and D are incorrect.

## Short-Answer Practice

*Source: The 16 Points, Hungarian Students National Policy Demands, October 22, 1956 (excerpts):*

> "1. We demand the immediate evacuation of all Soviet troops...
> 4. We demand general elections by universal, secret ballot... We demand that the right of workers to strike be recognized.
> 5. We demand revision and re-adjustment of Hungarian-Soviet and Hungarian-Yugoslav relations...
> 9. We demand complete recognition of freedom of opinion and of expression, of freedom of the press and of radio...
> 10. We demand that the statue of Stalin, symbol of Stalinist tyranny and political oppression, be removed as quickly as possible..."

**Part A:** Identify ONE element of the historical context in which the passage was written.

> [!tip] Model Response — Historical Context
> The passage was written in October 1956, during a period of de-Stalinization following Soviet leader Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" (February 1956) denouncing Stalin's cult of personality. Khrushchev's speech signaled a possible relaxation of Soviet control, emboldening reform movements across Eastern Europe. In Poland, workers' protests had already forced the return of Gomulka as a more nationally-independent communist leader. Hungarian students and workers saw this as an opening to demand withdrawal of Soviet troops, free elections, and an end to Stalinist repression — hence the call to remove Stalin's statue.

**Part B:** Explain ONE way in which the 1956 Hungarian Revolution was similar to uprisings in Czechoslovakia during the Cold War era.

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Both the Hungarian Revolution (1956) and Czechoslovakia's Prague Spring (1968) were reform movements that challenged Soviet domination from within communist-governed states, and both were crushed by Soviet military intervention. In Hungary, Soviet forces invaded after Imre Nagy declared neutrality and withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact. In Czechoslovakia, Warsaw Pact forces invaded after Alexander Dubček's liberal reforms threatened Soviet control. In both cases, the Soviet Union used overwhelming military force to restore hardline communist governments, demonstrating that Moscow would not tolerate meaningful political independence within its satellite states.

**Part C:** Explain ONE way in which the 1956 Hungarian Revolution differed from the conflict in Northern Ireland during the Cold War era.

> [!tip] Model Response — Difference
> The Hungarian Revolution of 1956 was a mass popular uprising against a foreign imperial power (Soviet Union) that sought national self-determination and neutrality in the Cold War — it was resolved militarily within weeks when Soviet forces invaded. The conflict in Northern Ireland (the Troubles, c. 1968–1998) was a protracted ethno-religious civil conflict within an existing state, pitting Catholic nationalists (IRA) against Protestant unionists (Ulster Defence Association) over whether Northern Ireland should remain part of the United Kingdom or join the Irish Republic. Unlike Hungary — which ended in military defeat — the Northern Ireland conflict was ultimately resolved through a political negotiation (the Good Friday Agreement, 1998), with neither side achieving its maximum goal but both accepting a power-sharing compromise.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

*In one to three paragraphs, explain the differing reactions to existing power structures after 1900.*

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> After 1900, individuals and groups resisted established power structures through a spectrum of methods ranging from disciplined nonviolence to outright terrorism, with the choice of method shaped by the nature of the regime being challenged and the resources available to resisters. Nonviolent resistance proved most effective against regimes that depended on international legitimacy — colonial powers like Britain and democratic states like the United States responded to mass civil disobedience by Gandhi, King, and Mandela because the moral weight of peaceful protesters being beaten or imprisoned damaged their reputations globally. These movements ultimately won independence, civil rights legislation, and the end of apartheid.
>
> By contrast, authoritarian regimes that did not depend on international legitimacy suppressed nonviolent movements more easily, pushing resistance groups toward armed tactics. The IRA, ETA, and Shining Path all operated in contexts where the state could use military force without international accountability. Yet violent resistance rarely achieved its stated goals — the IRA won power-sharing rather than unification; ETA and Shining Path ultimately failed. The exception was the Eastern European reform movements, which also failed militarily (Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968) under the Brezhnev Doctrine, only succeeding decades later when the Soviet Union withdrew its military guarantee in 1989.
>
> States responded to resistance with a range of approaches: some (like post-apartheid South Africa) negotiated and transformed; others (like Franco's Spain and Idi Amin's Uganda) used repression that intensified conflict rather than resolving it; and the United States responded to al-Qaeda's terrorism with the "War on Terror" — a massive global military response that reshaped geopolitics for the 21st century. The common thread across all these cases is that resistance and state response entered feedback cycles, with the effectiveness of each approach depending on the political, economic, and international context in which it occurred.
