# People, States & Movements

**AMSCO pp. 599–607**

## Nonviolent Resistance Leaders

**Martin Luther King Jr.**
Baptist minister and the most prominent leader of the American Civil Rights Movement in the 1950s–1960s. King employed Gandhi's strategy of nonviolent civil disobedience: bus boycotts (Montgomery, 1955–56), sit-ins, the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom (1963, 250,000 attendees), and voting rights marches from Selma to Montgomery (1965). His leadership produced landmark legislation — the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 — and demonstrated that nonviolent mass mobilization could defeat institutionalized discrimination. Assassinated in 1968. His legacy influenced resistance movements globally.

**Nelson Mandela**
Lawyer, ANC leader, and anti-apartheid activist in South Africa. Mandela initially endorsed limited sabotage against infrastructure but became globally associated with nonviolent resistance and moral authority. Imprisoned on Robben Island from 1964 to 1990 — 27 years — his imprisonment became an international symbol of apartheid's injustice. Released in 1990, he led negotiations to dismantle apartheid and won the Nobel Peace Prize (1993) alongside F.W. de Klerk. Elected South Africa's first Black president in 1994. His life arc — from prisoner to president — is one of the most powerful stories in 20th-century history.

**[[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]]**
Though covered primarily in [[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview|Topic 8.5]], Gandhi appears here as the originator of the nonviolent resistance model that inspired King and Mandela. His campaigns of satyagraha (nonviolent resistance) against British colonial rule in India combined moral authority, mass mobilization, and strategic use of media attention. Indian independence in 1947 was the proof of concept for nonviolent decolonization.

> [!tip]
> **The "holy trinity" of nonviolent resistance**: Gandhi (India/anticolonialism), King (USA/racial equality), Mandela (South Africa/apartheid). AP exam loves comparing these three — their shared method, different contexts, and different outcomes. All three faced imprisonment or assassination; all three ultimately prevailed.

## Eastern European Resistance Figures

**Imre Nagy**
Hungarian communist reformer who became Prime Minister during the 1956 Hungarian Revolution. Under popular pressure, he declared Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact — an act of defiance Moscow could not tolerate. Soviet forces invaded, removed him from power, and later executed him in 1958 after a secret trial. Nagy became a posthumous symbol of Hungarian national sovereignty. His reburial in 1989 was a catalyzing event in Hungary's transition away from communism.

**Alexander Dubček**
Slovak communist politician who led Czechoslovakia as First Secretary of the Communist Party during the Prague Spring (January–August 1968). His program of "socialism with a human face" expanded press freedom, civil liberties, and political pluralism. After the Soviet-led invasion crushed the Prague Spring in August 1968, he was stripped of power and eventually worked as a forestry official. Returned to prominence after the 1989 Velvet Revolution and served as Speaker of the Czech Parliament.

**Wladyslaw Gomulka**
Polish communist leader who navigated a middle path after the 1956 Poznań protests. He secured limited domestic autonomy from Moscow — ending forced collectivization of farms, allowing the Catholic Church more freedom — while keeping Poland loyal to the Warsaw Pact and allowing Soviet troops to remain. His compromise illustrates the narrow space Eastern European states had for reform within the Soviet system.

## Terrorist and Armed Resistance Movements

**Irish Republican Army (IRA)**
Founded in the early 20th century as an Irish nationalist paramilitary organization, the IRA became most active during "the Troubles" in Northern Ireland (c. 1968–1998). The IRA's Provisional wing used bombings, assassinations, and armed attacks to pressure Britain to withdraw from Northern Ireland. Primary targets included British security forces, but civilian deaths were frequent. After failed military campaigns, the IRA ultimately achieved a political settlement — the Good Friday Agreement (1998) — which established power-sharing in Northern Ireland rather than Irish unification.

**Ulster Defence Association (UDA)**
The largest loyalist paramilitary organization in Northern Ireland, founded in 1971 to defend Protestant and unionist communities from IRA attacks. The UDA committed sectarian murders of Catholics and Irish nationalists. Like the IRA, its violence represented a cycle of communal reprisal that killed thousands of civilians. Also eventually participated in the peace process.

**Basque Homeland and Freedom (ETA)**
Basque separatist organization (Euskadi Ta Askatasuna) founded in 1959 in Francoist Spain, where Basque language and culture were suppressed. ETA used bombings, kidnappings, and assassinations over five decades; its most dramatic act was killing Admiral Luis Carrero Blanco — Franco's handpicked successor — with an explosives-triggered car bomb in 1973. ETA's campaign ultimately failed to achieve independence. Declared a final ceasefire in 2011 and dissolved in 2018 after decommissioning its weapons.

**Shining Path (Sendero Luminoso)**
Maoist guerrilla movement founded in Peru in the 1970s by philosophy professor Abimael Guzmán. Launched its armed campaign in 1980 with the burning of ballot boxes on the first day of Peru's first democratic elections in years — a symbolic act of contempt for the political system. Shining Path targeted government officials, development workers, and rival leftists with extreme violence. Unlike other terrorist organizations, Shining Path had no ethnic or religious identity — its ideology was purely ideological Maoism. Guzmán's capture in 1992 fatally weakened the movement.

**al-Qaeda**
International Sunni Islamist terrorist organization founded by Osama bin Laden in the late 1980s. Financed by Saudi wealth, al-Qaeda conducted a global campaign of terrorism against Western interests and "apostate" Muslim governments. Most infamous for the September 11, 2001 attacks in the United States, in which nearly 3,000 people died. The US response — the "War on Terror" — reshaped global security policy for decades. Bin Laden was killed in a US raid in Abbottabad, Pakistan in 2011.

## Militarized States

**Francisco Franco (Spain)**
Military dictator who ruled Spain from 1939 to 1975 after overthrowing the elected Republican government in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939, [[Unit-7.6/Causes of World War II Overview|Topic 7.6]]). His anti-communism made him a US Cold War ally despite being a fascist dictator. His government executed, imprisoned, or sent hundreds of thousands of political opponents to labor camps. ETA emerged partly as a response to Franco's suppression of Basque identity. After Franco's death, Spain transitioned to democracy — suggesting that authoritarian suppression merely deferred rather than eliminated opposition.

**Idi Amin (Uganda)**
Military dictator who seized power in Uganda in 1971 and ruled until 1979. Known as the "Butcher of Uganda," Amin expelled 60,000 Asians (mainly Indian-descended) in 1972, devastating Uganda's economy, and oversaw the killing of an estimated 300,000–500,000 people from targeted ethnic groups. He declared himself President for Life and held numerous absurd titles. His aggression against Tanzania ultimately led to his ouster when Tanzania's military — alongside Ugandan exiles — invaded and drove him into exile in 1979.

> [!warning]
> Idi Amin is an example of a **post-colonial state** that descended into authoritarianism after independence — a pattern common in newly independent African nations. Connect to [[Unit-8.6/Newly Independent States Overview|Topic 8.6]] (Newly Independent States): many post-colonial governments became military dictatorships rather than liberal democracies.
