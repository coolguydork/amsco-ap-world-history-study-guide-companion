# Key Terms & Concepts

**AMSCO pp. 599–607**

**Wladyslaw Gomulka**
Polish communist leader who returned to power after the 1956 Poznań protests. He pursued limited domestic autonomy from Moscow while maintaining communist rule and allowing Soviet troops to remain in Poland. His compromise — maximum reform without threatening Soviet interests — set the ceiling for what Eastern European states could achieve in the 1950s.

**Imre Nagy**
Hungarian Prime Minister who in 1956 declared Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact and neutrality in the Cold War, going further than Moscow would tolerate. Soviet forces invaded and crushed the revolution; Nagy was arrested, tried in secret, and executed in 1958. He became a martyr symbol of anti-Soviet resistance and national sovereignty.

**Prague Spring**
The 1968 Czechoslovak reform movement under Alexander Dubček, characterized by liberalization of speech, press freedom, and political pluralism — "socialism with a human face." Ended when Soviet and Warsaw Pact forces invaded in August 1968 and reversed all reforms. Together with Hungary 1956, it established the Brezhnev Doctrine as a hard limit on Eastern bloc autonomy.

**Alexander Dubček**
First Secretary of the Czechoslovak Communist Party who led the Prague Spring (1968). He attempted to democratize communism from within — expanding freedoms while remaining nominally socialist. After the Soviet invasion, he was removed from power and later worked as a forest official. He was rehabilitated after the Velvet Revolution (1989).

**Brezhnev Doctrine**
Soviet foreign policy doctrine (1968) asserting that the USSR and its Warsaw Pact allies would intervene militarily in any socialist country where communist rule was threatened. Used to justify the invasions of Hungary (1956, retroactively) and Czechoslovakia (1968). It effectively subordinated satellite-state sovereignty to Soviet security interests and was only abandoned under Gorbachev, enabling the 1989 revolutions.

> [!exam-tip]
> The **Brezhnev Doctrine** is the single most testable concept in 8.7 for multiple-choice. Know it as: Soviet right to military intervention in any socialist country. Contrast with Gorbachev's abandonment of it — a direct cause of the 1989 Eastern European revolutions [[Unit-8.8/End of the Cold War Overview|End of the Cold War Overview]].

**Irish Republican Army (IRA)**
Irish Catholic nationalist paramilitary organization seeking to end British rule in Northern Ireland and unify the island of Ireland. During the Troubles (c. 1968–1998), the IRA conducted bombings and assassinations in Northern Ireland and on the British mainland, killing approximately 1,800 people. Renounced violence and disarmed following the Good Friday Agreement (1998).

**Ulster Defence Association**
Protestant loyalist paramilitary organization in Northern Ireland that fought to maintain union with Britain. Committed sectarian violence against Catholics during the Troubles, often in retaliation for IRA attacks. Represents the unionist side of the ethno-religious conflict and illustrates how terrorism can emerge from both sides of a political dispute.

**Basque Homeland and Freedom (ETA)**
Separatist organization founded in 1959 seeking independence for the Basque region of northern Spain and southern France. Used bombings and assassinations over five decades, killing 800+ people, including Franco's designated successor Admiral Carrero Blanco in 1973. Declared a ceasefire in 2011 and formally dissolved in 2018 — one of the few terrorist organizations that ended through political negotiation.

**Abimael Guzmán**
Peruvian philosophy professor who founded the Shining Path (Sendero Luminoso) Maoist guerrilla movement. Inspired by Mao Zedong and the Khmer Rouge, he launched an insurgency in 1980 to overthrow the Peruvian state and establish a communist government. Captured in 1992 and sentenced to life in prison. His movement killed an estimated 70,000 people, mostly rural Peruvian civilians.

**Shining Path**
Maoist revolutionary movement in Peru (active 1980s–1990s), one of the most brutal insurgencies in Latin American history. Founded by Abimael Guzmán, it combined revolutionary communist ideology with extreme violence against civilians. Killed an estimated 70,000 people before Guzmán's capture in 1992. Represents the persistence of communist revolutionary ideology in the developing world even after Cold War rivalry declined.

**Nelson Mandela**
South African anti-apartheid lawyer and ANC leader who was imprisoned for 27 years (1964–1990) on Robben Island. Became a global symbol of peaceful resistance to racial oppression. After his release, he led negotiations that dismantled apartheid and became South Africa's first democratically elected president (1994–1999). His life exemplifies how nonviolent moral authority can outlast and ultimately defeat institutionalized racism.

> [!tip]
> **Mandela connection:** apartheid and South Africa's democratic transition are covered more fully in [[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview|Topic 8.5]] (Decolonization). In 8.7, Mandela represents the comparison case for nonviolent resistance alongside Gandhi and MLK. All three: globally famous, imprisoned or harassed by the state, ultimately victorious through moral authority + international pressure.

**Kent State University**
Site of a May 4, 1970 massacre where Ohio National Guard troops shot and killed four unarmed student protesters at an antiwar demonstration against the US invasion of Cambodia. The killings triggered student strikes at hundreds of US colleges and universities and intensified domestic opposition to the Vietnam War. Symbolizes the domestic political costs of Cold War military intervention and the generational divide it created.

**Military-Industrial Complex**
Term coined by President Dwight Eisenhower in his 1961 farewell address to describe the growing interdependence of the US military establishment and private defense contractors. Eisenhower warned that this powerful economic-political alliance could distort national priorities and threaten democratic governance. The concept applies globally: the international arms trade expanded throughout the Cold War as states purchased weapons from superpowers, intensifying regional conflicts.

> [!exam-tip]
> Eisenhower coining the term "military-industrial complex" **in 1961** — his farewell address, just before leaving office — is a testable fact. A decorated WWII general warning about military overreach is a compelling primary source for SAQ/LEQ arguments about government change after 1900.

**Terrorism**
The use of violence against civilians to achieve political ends, typically by non-state actors. Distinguished from conventional warfare by its deliberate targeting of civilians for psychological effect. The 20th century saw terrorism employed by nationalist groups (IRA, ETA), communist insurgencies (Shining Path), and religious extremists (al-Qaeda, ISIS). Most terrorist campaigns ultimately failed to achieve their stated political goals through violence alone.
