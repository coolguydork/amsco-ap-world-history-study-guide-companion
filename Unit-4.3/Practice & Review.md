## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO AP World History, printed pp. 215–216*

Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage:

> "And so at the rumor of the rich deposits of mercury… in the years 1563 and 1571, they started the construction of the town of Huancavelica de Oropesa in a pleasant valley at the foot of the range. It contains 400 Spanish residents, as well as many temporary shops of dealers in merchandise and groceries, heads of trading houses, and transients, for the town has a lively commerce.… Up on the range there are 3,000 or 4,000 Indians working in the mine.… The ore was very rich black flint… and when they have filled their little sacks, the poor fellows, loaded down with ore, climb up those ladders or rigging, some like masts and others like cables, and so trying and distressing that a man empty-handed can hardly get up them."
>
> — Antonio Vázquez de Espinosa, *Compendium and Description of the West Indies*, 1622

---

**Question 1.** The excerpt implies that Espinosa felt

A. sympathy for those working in the mine
B. loyalty to the Spanish government
C. concern for the souls of the indigenous population
D. interest primarily in making profits from the mine

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Espinosa describes the Indian laborers as "poor fellows" climbing exhausting ladders "loaded down with ore" — language of pity, not indifference or disapproval. He notes the difficulty of the climb even for an empty-handed person, emphasizing the hardship. The passage contains no evidence of explicit loyalty to Spain (B), religious concern (C), or profit motive (D).

---

**Question 2.** The conditions faced by the Indian laborers described in the passage were most similar to those of

A. enslaved Africans in North America
B. bureaucrats serving in Song China
C. guild members in European cities
D. merchants involved in the trans-Saharan trade

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The Indian laborers described are forced workers in dangerous conditions with no apparent freedom — directly comparable to chattel slavery. Song bureaucrats (B) were educated elites; guild members (C) were free artisans; trans-Saharan merchants (D) operated voluntarily for profit. Only enslaved Africans in North America faced similar coerced, dangerous, and physically brutal labor conditions.

---

**Question 3.** What impact did the products of mines described in the passage, along with metals extracted from other mines, have on Spain and the rest of the world?

A. It enabled the Spanish navy to defeat the English navy in 1588.
B. It resulted in inflation and a worldwide devaluation of silver.
C. It caused gold to become the new form of worldwide currency.
D. It allowed many South Americans to move to Europe.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The massive influx of American silver into European markets caused the Price Revolution — sustained inflation as the purchasing power of silver fell. Spain's navy was in fact defeated by England in 1588 (A is wrong — the Armada lost). Gold did not replace other currencies (C). South Americans did not migrate to Europe in significant numbers (D). The silver glut and resulting inflation is the classic "unintended consequence" of colonial mining.

**Question 1.** Use the passages below to answer all parts of the question that follows.

> "On the evening of October 11, [1492]… The two worlds [Old and New], which God had cast asunder, were reunited, and the two worlds, which were so very different, began on that day to become alike. >

## Model SAQ Responses

> [!tip] Model Response — SAQ 1A (Crosby's Argument)
> Alfred Crosby argues that the biological reunification of the Eastern and Western Hemispheres following Columbus's 1492 voyage was among the most transformative events in the history of life on Earth. In the first passage he argues that the two hemispheres, previously isolated, began to homogenize biologically — sharing diseases, crops, and organisms. In the second, he extends this argument to show that American Indian crops (especially maize) are now so globally distributed that their loss would be catastrophic for humanity. His overarching claim is that the biological exchange initiated in 1492 was not a European achievement but a planetary transformation with lasting consequences.

> [!tip] Model Response — SAQ 1B (How Biological Impact Differed)
> The biological impact of the Columbian Exchange differed from other 1500–1650 encounters in the catastrophic *asymmetry* of disease transfer. When Europeans and Africans introduced smallpox, measles, and influenza to the Americas, they encountered populations with no prior exposure — and therefore no immunity — to these pathogens. Indigenous mortality rates of 50–90 percent were unlike anything experienced in other encounters of the period, where populations typically had some pre-existing immunity from earlier epidemic exposure. The biological homogenization Crosby describes was profoundly one-sided in its human cost.

> [!tip] Model Response — SAQ 2C (European Environmental Practices)
> European agricultural practices significantly altered American environments in the period 1500–1650. Unlike indigenous peoples who often used mixed cultivation, forest management, and rotational practices, European colonists cleared large tracts of forest for monoculture plantations (particularly sugarcane and tobacco). This deforestation led to soil depletion, erosion, and the destruction of ecosystems that indigenous peoples had managed sustainably for generations. European livestock (cattle, sheep, goats) introduced to cleared land caused further overgrazing and soil erosion. These environmental transformations were unintended consequences of the pursuit of commercial agriculture.

## Historical Reasoning: Identify Evidence in an Argument

*Think As a Historian — AMSCO p. 217*

Using Crosby's second passage (the maize quotation above):

1. **What claims does Crosby make?**
Crosby claims that (a) maize was already the most important grain of American Indians before contact; (b) it has since become a global staple across four continents; (c) the world's dependence on this single American Indian crop is now so great that its hypothetical disappearance would constitute an "ecological tragedy" and mass human death. His implicit claim is that American Indian agricultural knowledge has been one of history's most consequential contributions to humanity.

2. **Five pieces of evidence Crosby uses:**
   - "Maize was the most important grain of the American Indians in 1491"
   - "It is a standard crop of people not only throughout the Americas, but also southern Europe"
   - "It is a staple for the Chinese"
   - "It is a staple in Indonesia, throughout large areas of Africa"
   - "If suddenly American Indian crops would not grow in all of the world, it would be an ecological tragedy… the slaughter of a very large portion of the human race"

> [!exam-tip]
> "Identify evidence in an argument" is a core AP historical reasoning skill. On the exam, this means distinguishing between a historian's **claims** (what they assert) and their **evidence** (what they use to support those assertions). Crosby's *claim* is that maize is globally transformative; his *evidence* is the enumeration of regions where it is now a staple.
