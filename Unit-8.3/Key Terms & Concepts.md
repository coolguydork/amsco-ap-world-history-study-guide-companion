## Military Alliances

**[[Concept Notes/NATO\|North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)]]**
Western military alliance formed in 1949 under US leadership; collective defense commitment (attack on one = attack on all); primary military counterweight to Soviet power in Europe; the first permanent US peacetime military alliance and cornerstone of Western Cold War strategy. Original 12 members included Belgium, Canada, Denmark, France, Great Britain, Iceland, Italy, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, and the US.

**Warsaw Pact**
Soviet-led military alliance (1955) among Eastern European communist states, formed in direct response to NATO. Members: Albania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, Poland, Romania, and the USSR. Combined forces were headquartered in Moscow; member states effectively placed their militaries under Soviet command. Albania withdrew in 1968; the Pact dissolved in 1991.

**communist bloc**
The coalition of Soviet-aligned communist states including the USSR and its Eastern European satellite states; bound together by ideology, Soviet military dominance, and COMECON economic integration. Distinguished from the Western (US-led) bloc and from non-aligned nations.

**Southeast Asia Treaty Organization (SEATO)**
1954 US-led collective defense alliance for Southeast Asia modeled on NATO; members included US, UK, France, Australia, New Zealand, Philippines, Thailand, and Pakistan. Less effective than NATO due to diverse membership and limited automatic defense commitment; dissolved in 1977.

**Central Treaty Organization (CENTO)**
1955 collective defense alliance covering the Middle East (Britain, Turkey, Iran, Pakistan; US as associate member); created to contain Soviet expansion southward. Largely ineffective; collapsed after Iraq withdrew in 1959.

> [!exam-tip]
> For the AP, the key point about all these alliances is their shared purpose: **containment** — surrounding the Soviet Union and communist China with US-aligned military partners to prevent further communist expansion.

## International Conflicts (Proxy Wars)

**proxy war**
Conflict in which superpowers supported opposing sides without direct confrontation; e.g., Korean War, Vietnam War, Angola, Contra War. The armies of smaller countries served as proxies (stand-ins) for the two superpowers. Because nuclear weapons made direct superpower conflict unthinkable, proxy wars became the primary mode of Cold War military competition.

**Berlin Airlift**
1948–1949 US and British operation supplying West Berlin by air after the Soviet Union blockaded all land routes into the city; successfully broke the blockade after nearly a year; demonstrated Western resolve and confirmed the Cold War division of Germany.

**Berlin Wall**
Wall built by East Germany in 1961 to seal its border with West Berlin and stop mass emigration of East Germans to the West; the most powerful symbol of Cold War division; approximately 150 people were killed trying to cross it between 1961 and 1989; torn down in November 1989, marking the effective end of the Cold War.

**Korean War**
1950–1953 conflict in which communist North Korea invaded South Korea; US-led UN forces intervened under the containment doctrine; China entered when UN forces approached its border; ended in stalemate at the 38th parallel with ~4 million casualties. The first major military test of containment and established the pattern for Cold War proxy conflicts.

**Vietnam War**
Longest Cold War proxy conflict (1954–1975) in which the US supported South Vietnam against communist North Vietnam; US involvement escalated under the domino theory; US withdrew in 1973 and South Vietnam fell in 1975. Profoundly divided American society and shaped US foreign policy for decades.

**domino theory**
Cold War US strategic doctrine that if one country fell to communism, neighboring countries would follow like falling dominoes; used by Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Johnson to justify US military intervention in Vietnam and elsewhere in Southeast Asia.

**Bay of Pigs**
1961 failed US-sponsored invasion of Cuba by Cuban exiles attempting to overthrow Fidel Castro's communist government; collapsed within days; major embarrassment for President Kennedy; emboldened the Soviets and contributed directly to the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962).

**Cuban Missile Crisis**
1962 standoff when Soviet nuclear missiles were discovered in Cuba; the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war; resolved when Khrushchev withdrew Soviet missiles in exchange for a US pledge not to invade Cuba and the quiet removal of US missiles from Turkey. Led directly to the Hot Line and Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty.

**Angola**
African nation that became a Cold War proxy battleground after Portuguese decolonization in 1975; the US backed the Bankongo/UNITA faction, the Soviets and Cubans backed the Mbundu/MPLA government, and South Africa backed the Ovimbundu; civil war lasted until 2002. Exemplifies Cold War competition extending into newly decolonized Africa.

**Contra War**
1981–1990 conflict in Nicaragua in which US-backed Contra rebels fought the Sandinista socialist government; part of the Reagan Doctrine of supporting anti-communist movements; raised sharp ethical questions about US covert intervention in Latin America.

## Treaties and Arms Control

**Hot Line**
Direct communication link established in 1963 between the White House and the Kremlin; created in the aftermath of the Cuban Missile Crisis to enable rapid leader-to-leader communication and prevent accidental nuclear war; first formal US-Soviet crisis management mechanism.

**Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty**
1963 agreement between the US, USSR, and UK banning nuclear weapons tests in the atmosphere, underwater, and in space; first nuclear arms control agreement; signed in the aftermath of the Cuban Missile Crisis. Did not ban underground tests; France and China did not sign.

**Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty**
1968 international treaty in which non-nuclear states pledged not to acquire nuclear weapons and nuclear states pledged not to help others develop them; cornerstone of international non-proliferation efforts; demonstrates Cold War-era cooperation on the most dangerous technology.

> [!exam-tip]
> Arms control agreements are evidence of **Cold War cooperation** alongside competition. The AP frequently asks students to explain why rivals who were building more weapons simultaneously signed treaties to limit them — mutual assured destruction made both sides want guardrails.

## Society & Activism

**antinuclear weapons movement**
Mass public movement that emerged in Japan in the 1950s and spread to Western Europe and the US by the late 1970s–early 1980s; opposed nuclear weapons buildup; organized massive demonstrations including 1 million people in New York City (June 1982). Reflects transnational civil society organizing against state military policy.
