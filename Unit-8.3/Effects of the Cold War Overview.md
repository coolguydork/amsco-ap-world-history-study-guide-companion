# Effects of the Cold War

**Unit 8 — Cold War and Decolonization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 562–570**

> [!tip] Essential Question
> In what ways did both the Soviet Union and the United States seek to maintain influence during the Cold War?

## The Big Picture

The Cold War never became a direct military confrontation between the superpowers — nuclear deterrence made that unthinkable. Instead, the US and USSR competed through alliances, arms buildups, and proxy wars: conflicts where smaller nations fought on behalf of the great powers. From Korea to Vietnam, Cuba to Angola, Nicaragua to Angola, the Cold War's "hot" violence fell on the developing world. Meanwhile, both superpowers constructed rival military alliance systems — NATO and the Warsaw Pact — that divided Europe into armed camps for four decades.

The most dangerous moment came in 1962, when Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba brought the world to the brink of nuclear war. The Cuban Missile Crisis ultimately led to the first arms control agreements (the 1963 Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty and the 1963 Hot Line), demonstrating that even enemies can cooperate when mutual destruction is the alternative.

## Allied Occupation of Germany

After World War II, the Allies divided Germany into four occupation zones (US, British, French, Soviet). When the Western Allies moved to unify their zones into a democratic state, the Soviets blockaded West Berlin (1948), cutting off land access to the Western-controlled enclave deep inside Soviet-occupied territory. The Western response — the **Berlin Airlift** — flew in supplies for nearly a year until the Soviets backed down. Germany then formally split into **West Germany** (Federal Republic) and **East Germany** (German Democratic Republic).

The most visible symbol of Cold War division was the **Berlin Wall**, built in August 1961 to stop the hemorrhage of East Germans fleeing to the West. Between 1961 and 1989, approximately 150 people were killed trying to cross it.

> [!warning] Common Misconception
> The Berlin Wall was not built to keep Westerners out — it was built to keep East Germans in. The mass emigration (2.5 million between 1945–1961) was both an embarrassment to communist ideology and an economic drain on East Germany.

## Military Alliances

**[[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]]** (1949): Western collective defense alliance led by the US. An attack on any member was treated as an attack on all. Original 12 members included most of Western Europe plus the US and Canada.

**Warsaw Pact** (1955): Soviet-led counterpart to NATO. Albania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, Poland, Romania, and the USSR. Combined forces were headquartered in Moscow.

Two communist nations resisted Soviet control: **Albania** eventually withdrew from the Warsaw Pact (1968) and aligned with China; **Yugoslavia** under **Tito** never joined the Warsaw Pact and pursued an independent socialist path.

Additional US-led alliances targeted specific regions:
- **SEATO** (1954): Southeast Asia — included Australia, France, UK, New Zealand, Pakistan, Philippines, Thailand, US
- **CENTO** (1955): Middle East — Britain, Iran, Iraq, Pakistan, Turkey; US as associate member

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks why the US formed so many alliances. The answer is **containment** — surrounding the USSR and its allies with military partners to prevent further communist expansion. By the mid-1950s, the US had alliances with more than 40 states.

## Proxy Wars

Because direct superpower conflict risked nuclear war, the Cold War produced a pattern of **proxy wars** — conflicts where the US and USSR supported opposing sides without fighting each other directly. These wars were real wars, killing millions; they just weren't fought between the two superpowers themselves.

**Korean War (1950–1953):** North Korea (Soviet-backed) invaded South Korea. The UN (led by the US under General Douglas MacArthur) intervened. China entered when UN forces approached the Chinese border. Result: stalemate at the 38th parallel; the peninsula remained divided. Casualties: ~4 million civilian and military.

**Vietnam War (1954–1975):** The US supported South Vietnam against communist North Vietnam, guided by the **domino theory** — the belief that a communist victory would topple neighboring states like falling dominoes. President Kennedy increased military advisers to 16,000; President Johnson sent 500,000 troops. The US ultimately withdrew in 1973; South Vietnam fell in 1975. The war profoundly divided American society and reshaped US foreign policy.

**Angola (1975–2002):** After Portuguese decolonization, three ethnic factions — backed by the USSR/Cuba, South Africa, and the US respectively — fought a civil war lasting nearly three decades.

**Contra War (1981–1990):** In Nicaragua, the US backed Contra rebels against the socialist Sandinista government, raising sharp ethical questions about covert US intervention in Latin America.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse proxy wars with decolonization wars — they often overlapped. Vietnam's war against France [[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview|Decolonization after 1900 Overview]] was a decolonization struggle; the American phase was a Cold War proxy conflict. Local causes (land reform, anti-colonialism) mixed with superpower ideology in almost every proxy conflict.

## Cuba and the Nuclear Brink

**Bay of Pigs (1961):** CIA-trained Cuban exiles attempted to invade Cuba and overthrow Castro. The invasion collapsed within days — a major embarrassment for President Kennedy. It emboldened Khrushchev and led directly to the next crisis.

**Cuban Missile Crisis (1962):** Khrushchev shipped nuclear missiles to Cuba (partly in response to US missiles in Turkey). US intelligence discovered the missiles; Kennedy ordered a naval "quarantine." For 13 days, the superpowers stood at the nuclear brink. Resolution: the Soviets withdrew missiles from Cuba; the US quietly removed missiles from Turkey and pledged not to invade Cuba.

The crisis produced the first concrete arms control steps:
- **Hot Line** (1963): Direct communication link between Washington and Moscow to prevent accidental war
- **Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty** (1963): Banned atmospheric, underwater, and space nuclear tests
- **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty** (1968): Nuclear states pledged not to help others acquire nuclear weapons

> [!exam-tip]
> The Cuban Missile Crisis is the AP's go-to example for Cold War nuclear tension AND for superpower cooperation. The post-crisis arms control agreements are equally important — they show that even adversaries can negotiate when both face annihilation.

## Antinuclear Weapons Movement

The nuclear arms race generated a popular backlash. Japan, as the only country ever attacked with nuclear weapons, led early antinuclear activism — in 1955, one-third of Japan's population signed a petition against nuclear testing. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the movement spread to Western Europe and the US, culminating in a 1 million-person demonstration in New York City in June 1982.

> [!tip] AP Connections
> The antinuclear movement is an example of **transnational activism** (Theme: SIO) — civil society organizing across borders to challenge state military policy. It connects to broader patterns of protest movements in the late 20th century (Topics 8.7, 9.7).

## AP Exam Strategy

This topic is high-yield for both MCQ and SAQ. The AP most commonly tests:

1. **Proxy wars as a Cold War pattern** — Korea and Vietnam as case studies; be able to compare them (SAQ comparison skill)
2. **Alliance systems** — NATO vs. Warsaw Pact; why the US built SEATO and CENTO
3. **Cuban Missile Crisis** — causes, resolution, consequences (arms control)
4. **Domino theory** — as ideological justification for US intervention
5. **Arms control** as evidence of Cold War détente/cooperation despite rivalry

For LEQ/DBQ, this topic provides strong evidence for arguments about: US containment policy, superpower competition in the developing world, and the role of nuclear deterrence in preventing direct war.
