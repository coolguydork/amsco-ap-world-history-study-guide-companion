# Key Terms & Concepts
*Topic 5.3 — Industrial Revolution Begins | AMSCO pp. 297–300*

---

## Technology: Textiles

**Spinning jenny**
Invented by James Hargreaves in the 1760s, this machine allowed a single weaver to spin multiple threads simultaneously. It was the first major mechanization of textile production and a precursor to the factory system (AMSCO p. 298).

**Water frame**
Patented by Richard Arkwright in 1769, it used waterpower to drive the spinning wheel — far more efficient than human labor. Because of the machine's size and power requirements, it had to be housed in large buildings, creating the first factories and dooming the cottage industry (AMSCO p. 298).

**Factory system**
The organization of production in centralized, mechanized buildings where workers operated machines owned by an entrepreneur. Pioneered by Arkwright, the factory system replaced the putting-out system and restructured labor relations around wages rather than piece-work (AMSCO p. 298).

## Technology: Agriculture

**Agricultural revolution**
Beginning in the early 1700s in Britain, a set of improvements in farming practices that dramatically increased food output per acre and per worker. The result was a larger population, lower food prices, and a surplus rural labor force available for factory work (AMSCO p. 297).

**Crop rotation**
The practice of alternating different crops (e.g., wheat, turnips, clover) in the same field across successive growing seasons to maintain soil fertility and increase yields. A key driver of the agricultural revolution (AMSCO p. 297).

**Seed drill**
A device that placed seeds at controlled depths in rows, improving germination rates and reducing waste compared to broadcast sowing. Part of the agricultural revolution toolkit (AMSCO p. 297).

## Economy: Manufacturing

**Industrial Revolution**
The broad transformation of economies from agrarian and craft-based to mechanized and factory-based production, beginning in Britain c. 1750 and spreading globally through the 19th century. Characterized by coal-powered machinery, urbanization, wage labor, and expanded commodity production (AMSCO p. 297).

**Industrialization**
The process of developing manufacturing industries and mechanized production at scale, accompanied by urbanization, capital investment, and wage-labor systems. Britain industrialized first; the pattern then spread globally (Topic 5.4).

**Cottage industry (putting-out system)**
A preindustrial production arrangement in which merchants supplied raw materials (typically raw cotton) to rural women who spun or wove them into finished cloth in their homes. It provided supplemental income but was too slow to compete with mechanized factories (AMSCO p. 298).

**Interchangeable parts**
A manufacturing system developed by Eli Whitney (~1798) in which components of a product are standardized so that any given part can replace any identical broken part. This reduced dependence on skilled craftsmen and enabled mass production (AMSCO p. 298).

**Division of labor**
An organizational principle in which each worker performs only one specialized task in a production process rather than crafting an entire product. Directly enabled by interchangeable parts; Adam Smith's pin factory is the classic example. Increased speed and lowered costs but reduced worker skill (AMSCO p. 298).

**Specialization of labor**
The assignment of workers to particular tasks based on skill or efficiency. Closely related to division of labor; together they form the foundation of industrial mass production (AMSCO p. 298).

**Assembly line**
A system in which a product moves along a production line and workers at each station add one component. Evolved from Whitney's interchangeable parts; formalized by Henry Ford in the early 20th century for automobile production (AMSCO p. 298).

**Enclosure movement**
The process by which the British government fenced off common farmland that had traditionally been open for use by small farmers. Dispossessed and landless, many rural families migrated to cities, forming the wage-labor workforce for the new factories. The enclosure movement thus forcibly created an industrial proletariat (AMSCO p. 300).

**Capital**
Money or assets available to invest in new businesses or enterprises. British capitalists accumulated surplus capital through the slave trade and colonial commerce, making them able to fund the construction of factories, canals, and machinery (AMSCO p. 300). See also [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]].

**Laissez-faire economics**
From the French "let it be" — the economic doctrine, associated with Adam Smith, that markets function best without government interference, that self-interest drives efficient allocation of resources, and that free trade benefits all participants. The theoretical framework underlying industrial capitalism and a sharp contrast to [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|Mercantilism]] (AMSCO pp. 297, 301).

## Environment: Britain

**Coal (fossil fuel)**
A carbon-based energy source formed from ancient plant and animal remains. Britain's extensive coal deposits (in South Wales, Yorkshire, Lancashire) were essential to industrialization — coal powered steam engines and smelted iron ore. The burning of coal to power machines marks the beginning of a fossil-fuel-based economy and long-term environmental change (AMSCO p. 299).

**Seaways and river network**
Britain's Atlantic coastline, natural rivers, and government-funded canals enabled cheap transport of bulk raw materials (coal, iron, cotton) and finished goods. Geographic access to waterways was a structural advantage over landlocked competitors (AMSCO p. 300).

**Urbanization**
The large-scale migration of people from rural areas to cities. In Britain, the enclosure movement and factory jobs drove urbanization; cities like Manchester and Liverpool grew rapidly. Urbanization produced new public health challenges, class formations, and political pressures (AMSCO p. 300).

**Raw materials**
Unprocessed natural resources — cotton, coal, iron ore, timber — that are transformed into finished goods by industrial processes. Britain's colonies supplied raw materials (e.g., slave-produced cotton from the Americas); factories converted them into exportable commodities (AMSCO p. 299).
