# Timeline & Chronology
*Topic 5.3 — Industrial Revolution Begins | AMSCO pp. 297–300*

> [!tip]
> Dates in this note were verified against the curated Clean Key Dates in ref_5_3.md. The Unit 5+ narrative text frequently truncates years (e.g., "177" for 1776); all dates here reflect the corrected values.

## Chronological Table

| Date | Event | AP Significance |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| Early 1700s | Agricultural revolution in Britain (crop rotation, seed drill, potato) | Surplus food → population growth + rural labor surplus freed for factory work |
| 1760s | Spinning jenny (James Hargreaves) | First multi-thread spinning; begins mechanization of textile production |
| 1765 | James Watt improves steam engine | Coal-powered, mobile energy source; foundational for factory, rail, and ship power |
| 1769 | Water frame patented (Richard Arkwright) | Factory system begins; cottage/putting-out system becomes economically unviable |
| 1776 | Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* | Theoretical foundation for industrial capitalism; laissez-faire over mercantilism |
| c. 1798 | Eli Whitney's interchangeable parts | Division of labor + mass production possible; deskilled factory work |

## Cause-and-Effect Chain

The events above are not simply sequential — they form a reinforcing web:

Agricultural revolution (early 1700s) → surplus labor available → workers move to cities when enclosure movement also pushes them off land → factories have a workforce.

Colonial trade and [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] (ongoing 1600s–1800s) → British capitalists accumulate surplus capital → can fund factory construction, canals, machinery.

Spinning jenny (1760s) + water frame (1769) → cottage industry collapses → centralized factory production → urbanization accelerates.

Watt steam engine (1765) → reliable, portable, coal-powered energy → factories no longer tied to rivers → industrial geography expands.

Interchangeable parts (~1798) + division of labor → mass production possible → factory costs fall → industrial output increases → Britain becomes dominant global manufacturer.

## Periodization Note

Topic 5.3 covers the **origins** of industrialization (c. 1750–1800), focused on Britain. Topics 5.4 and 5.5 then trace the spread of industrialization globally and the new technologies (railroads, steamships, telegraph, steel) of the mid-to-late 1800s. Topic 5.3 is the "why Britain, why now" foundation.

The AP exam period for this content is **Period 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900)**, though most 5.3 events fall in the second half of the 1700s.
