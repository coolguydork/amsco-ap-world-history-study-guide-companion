# Key Terms — Unit 5.3: Industrial Revolution Begins

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Industrial Revolution** — The transformation from agrarian, hand-production economies to machine-powered factory production; began in Britain c. 1760 and spread globally through the 19th century. (The most consequential economic transformation in human history since the Neolithic agricultural revolution.)

2. **Factory system** — The organization of production in large buildings (factories) where workers operate machines under employer supervision, replacing cottage industries and artisanal workshops. (Created new patterns of work, time discipline, and social organization; produced the modern urban working class.)

3. **Steam engine** — A device that converts heat from burning fuel (coal) into mechanical work; James Watt's improved version (1769, 1782) made it efficient enough to power factories, mines, and eventually locomotives. (The defining technology of the early Industrial Revolution; its portability made industrial power available anywhere coal could be delivered.)

4. **James Watt (1736–1819)** — Scottish inventor who made crucial improvements to the steam engine (separate condenser, rotary motion) in the 1760s–1780s, transforming it from a pump into a general-purpose power source. (In partnership with businessman Matthew Boulton, Watt commercialized the steam engine — illustrating the importance of the inventor-entrepreneur relationship.)

5. **Spinning jenny** — A multi-spindle spinning machine invented by James Hargreaves (c. 1764) that allowed one worker to spin multiple threads simultaneously. (First major labor-saving device in textile production; dramatically increased output per worker.)

6. **Water frame** — A water-powered spinning machine invented by Richard Arkwright (1769) that produced stronger thread than the spinning jenny; required a factory setting near rivers. (Arkwright's factories became models of the factory system; he is sometimes called "the father of the factory.")

7. **Power loom** — A mechanized weaving device (Edmund Cartwright, 1785) that automated the weaving process, dramatically reducing the cost of cloth. (Completed the mechanization of textile production from fiber to finished cloth; devastated the handloom weaving trade.)

8. **Enclosure movement** — The process by which British landlords fenced (enclosed) common agricultural lands that peasants had traditionally shared, forcing rural workers off the land and into cities. (Created the landless urban labor force — the proletariat — that industrial factories required; an essential precondition of British industrialization.)

9. **Cottage industry / putting-out system** — A pre-industrial system in which merchant capitalists distributed raw materials to rural workers who produced goods at home (spinning, weaving) and returned finished products for payment. (The factory system replaced this; the transition was often violent for workers who lost control of their time and workspace.)

10. **Proletariat** — The industrial working class: people who own no productive property and must sell their labor for wages. (The term was later theorized by Marx; but the *reality* was created by the enclosure movement and the factory system.)

11. **Coke smelting** — The process of using coke (purified coal) instead of charcoal to smelt iron ore, developed by Abraham Darby (1709) and refined throughout the 18th century. (Enabled large-scale iron production by eliminating dependence on forests for charcoal; iron was the structural material of the Industrial Revolution.)

12. **Bessemer process** — A method of converting iron into steel by blasting air through molten iron (Henry Bessemer, 1856); dramatically reduced the cost of steel production. (Steel — stronger and more flexible than iron — made railroads, skyscrapers, and large-scale machinery possible; key to the Second Industrial Revolution.)

13. **Capital** — Money, machinery, and other resources invested in productive enterprises. (Britain's advantage was accumulated capital from Atlantic trade, slave economy profits, and an advanced banking and credit system — without capital, technology cannot be commercialized.)

14. **Laissez-faire** — The economic doctrine that government should not interfere in the market; dominant ideology of early British industrialization. (Justified both the enclosure movement and the refusal to regulate factory conditions — with devastating consequences for workers that eventually produced the reform movement.)

15. **Luddites** — British textile workers (1811–1816) who smashed new machinery that threatened their livelihoods, protesting industrialization's destruction of traditional craft work. (Their movement was violently suppressed; "Luddite" later became a dismissive term for anyone opposing technology, but the original movement was a rational response to economic displacement.)

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the **"why Britain first" argument** for the AP exam: coal + iron deposits (near navigable rivers) + capital (from empire and slave trade) + enclosure movement (labor supply) + stable government (property rights) + colonial markets (demand). Each factor addresses a specific bottleneck that other countries couldn't overcome simultaneously.
