# Themes and Causation — Unit 5.3: Industrial Revolution Begins

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## Governance

Britain's political stability and property rights were essential enabling conditions for industrialization. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 had established parliamentary supremacy and a constitutional monarchy that protected property from arbitrary seizure. Patent law incentivized inventors by granting temporary monopolies on new technologies. The enclosure movement was enabled by Parliamentary Acts — illustrating that "free market" industrialization depended heavily on state power to dispossess peasants and redistribute common lands to private landlords. Conversely, the laissez-faire ideology of the industrial bourgeoisie insisted the state should not *regulate* factory conditions — a position that eventually provoked the reform legislation (Factory Acts) of the 1830s–1870s as conditions became politically untenable.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the relationship between government and industrialization. For Britain, emphasize: government *enabled* industrialization (enclosure acts, patent law, stable property rights) while adopting laissez-faire ideology *against* regulating its consequences. This is a complex and nuanced position — not simply "Britain had no government role."

## Economic Systems

The Industrial Revolution represented the decisive transition from **commercial capitalism** (trade-based wealth accumulation) to **industrial capitalism** (production-based wealth accumulation). The key shift was in the *source of value*: industrialists extracted profit not primarily from trading price differences but from the productivity of machine labor operating on raw materials. This required new economic institutions: **investment banking** to mobilize capital at scale, **factory accounting** to track costs and productivity, and **insurance** to spread production risk. Britain's advantage lay in having all these institutions already developed from centuries of commercial activity. The shift to factory production also created new economic problems: **business cycles** of boom and bust, structural unemployment during technological transitions, and the systematic depression of wages as machine output increased.

## Cultural Developments and Religion

The Industrial Revolution produced powerful cultural reactions both for and against it. **Romanticism** — the dominant cultural movement of the early 19th century — reacted against industrial rationalism with a celebration of nature, emotion, and the pre-industrial past. Poets like Blake ("dark Satanic mills"), Wordsworth, and Keats expressed horror at the transformation of the English landscape. Simultaneously, a powerful **Protestant work ethic** culture (Max Weber's later argument) reinforced industrial discipline: punctuality, sobriety, thrift, and hard work were virtues in both the counting-house and the chapel. Evangelical Christianity drove many of the reform movements (abolition, factory reform, education) that sought to mitigate industrialization's worst effects on the poor.

## Social Structures

No development transformed social structures more completely than the Industrial Revolution. It created two new classes — the **industrial bourgeoisie** and the **urban proletariat** — that gradually displaced the old hierarchy of aristocracy and peasantry as the defining social relation of modern life. The bourgeoisie's wealth came from capital ownership and factory production; the proletariat's survival depended on selling labor for wages. This created a fundamentally adversarial economic relationship — the basis of Karl Marx's later analysis. Within the working class, industrialization also transformed **gender and family** relations: the factory separated workplace from home (previously united in cottage industry), enforcing a new division between male "breadwinner" and female "domestic sphere" — at least for the middle class. Working-class women and children continued to labor in factories and mines, often under even worse conditions than men.

## Technology and Environment

The Industrial Revolution's environmental impact was immediate and severe. Coal-burning factories produced **air pollution** that blackened buildings and caused respiratory disease in industrial cities. Manchester's air was described by contemporaries as permanently brown; the Thames River became an open sewer. Deforestation had been a problem before industrialization but coke smelting reduced pressure on forests — substituting geological resources (coal) for biological ones (trees) at enormous environmental cost. Mining scarred landscapes and contaminated water supplies. The key point for AP World History is that the Industrial Revolution began the process of **fossil fuel combustion** at planetary scale — the origin point of the modern climate crisis.

---

## Human Migration

Industrialization triggered the largest internal migration in British history. The enclosure movement drove millions from the countryside to industrial cities — Manchester grew from ~25,000 in 1772 to 316,000 in 1850. Cities lacked adequate housing, clean water, or sewage systems, producing epidemic disease (cholera outbreaks 1832, 1848, 1854). International migration also accelerated: Irish famine refugees (1840s–1850s) flooded into Manchester and Liverpool, providing cheap labor for further industrial expansion. The urban proletariat's concentration in dense cities had a political consequence the rural poor had lacked: it made collective organizing possible. The factory floor became the organizing site for trade unions, socialist movements, and eventually successful labor reform.
