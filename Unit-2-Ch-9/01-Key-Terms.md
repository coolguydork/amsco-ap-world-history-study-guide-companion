# Chapter 9: Key Terms — Indian Ocean Exchange

> [!exam-tip]
> Indian Ocean trade is one of the AP exam's highest-frequency topics. Know the **mechanism** (monsoons + dhows + diasporas), the **goods** (spices, silk, gold, ivory, enslaved people), the **religion spread** (Islam via merchants), and the **states** that depended on trade (Swahili city-states, Malacca, Calicut).

---

## Vocabulary

**Indian Ocean trade network** — The system of maritime commerce connecting East Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, Persia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and China, sustained by monsoon winds and merchant diasporas. (Significance: the world's most active pre-modern maritime trading system; the primary vehicle for the spread of Islam, spices, and commercial wealth in the period c. 1200–1450.)

**Monsoon winds** — Seasonal wind patterns of the Indian Ocean that blow from the southwest in summer (driving ships northeast, toward India and Southeast Asia) and from the northeast in winter (driving ships southwest, toward Arabia and East Africa). (Significance: the natural infrastructure of Indian Ocean trade; sailors could plan voyages around the monsoon calendar; predictability made long-distance commerce reliable without state control.)

**Dhow** — A type of sailing vessel used by Arab and Indian Ocean merchants, characterized by a triangular (lateen) sail and a hull sewn together with ropes rather than nails, making it flexible in rough water. (Significance: the primary merchant vessel of the Indian Ocean; its design was optimized for the monsoon wind patterns of the region.)

**Swahili coast** — The East African coastal region (modern Kenya, Tanzania, Mozambique) where a series of prosperous city-states developed as nodes in the Indian Ocean trading network. (Significance: the Swahili city-states — Kilwa, Mombasa, Zanzibar, Malindi — were commercial hubs that exported African gold and ivory and imported Indian textiles and Chinese porcelain; their culture blended Bantu African traditions with Arabic and Islamic influences.)

**Kilwa** — The most powerful and prosperous Swahili city-state on the East African coast during the period c. 1200–1500, serving as a hub for the gold trade from Great Zimbabwe. (Significance: Ibn Battuta visited Kilwa and described it as one of the most beautiful cities in the world; its prosperity was entirely based on Indian Ocean commerce; visited by the Portuguese in 1498 as Vasco da Gama opened direct European access to Indian Ocean trade.)

**Malacca (Melaka)** — A sultanate and port city on the Malay Peninsula, founded c. 1400, that became the dominant commercial hub of Southeast Asian maritime trade. (Significance: Malacca controlled the strait between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra — the chokepoint through which all ship traffic between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea passed; its ruler converted to Islam, making the city a center of Islamic diffusion into Southeast Asia.)

**Strait of Malacca** — The narrow sea passage between the Malay Peninsula and the island of Sumatra, through which almost all maritime trade between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea traveled. (Significance: whoever controlled this strait could tax or disrupt the entire maritime spice trade; Malacca's power derived from its position here.)

**Calicut (Kozhikode)** — A major port city on the Malabar Coast (southwestern India) that served as a key hub in the Indian Ocean spice trade. (Significance: the source of much of the pepper that flowed to the Islamic world and eventually Europe; Vasco da Gama arrived here in 1498, establishing direct European access to the spice trade and bypassing Islamic middlemen.)

**Spice trade** — The commerce in high-value aromatic and culinary plants — especially pepper, nutmeg, mace, cloves, and cinnamon — that were indigenous to South and Southeast Asia and commanded extraordinary prices in the Islamic world and Europe. (Significance: the primary economic driver of Indian Ocean commerce; European desire to access the spice trade directly, without Islamic middlemen, was a primary motivation for the Age of Exploration.)

**Lateen sail** — A triangular sail that, unlike a square sail, allows a ship to sail at an angle into the wind rather than only downwind. (Significance: critical technology that enabled Indian Ocean sailors to navigate monsoon winds in multiple directions; later adopted by Portuguese and other European explorers.)

**Diasporic merchant communities** — Permanent settlements of merchants from one culture in a foreign port city, creating trust networks for long-distance trade. (Significance: Indian Ocean trade was sustained by overlapping diasporas — Arab merchants in Indian ports, Indian merchants in East African cities, Chinese merchants in Southeast Asian ports; these communities spread culture and religion as well as goods.)

**Ibn Battuta's Indian Ocean travels** — The Moroccan traveler's journeys along East African and South Asian Indian Ocean ports (c. 1330s), which provide primary source documentation of Swahili city-states, Indian commercial culture, and the reach of Islamic civilization via maritime trade. (Significance: the most important primary source for Indian Ocean trade in this period; see also [[../Unit-2-Ch-7/02-Key-People-and-Groups|Ibn Battuta]] in Ch7.)

**Zheng He's voyages** — Seven massive Chinese naval expeditions (1405–1433) dispatched by the Ming Emperor Yongle, reaching Southeast Asia, the Indian subcontinent, the Persian Gulf, and East Africa. (Significance: the largest naval fleet in the world at the time; demonstrated Chinese technological capability and political ambition; but ended abruptly when the Ming court reversed policy — their discontinuation is as significant as the voyages themselves.)

**Tributary system (Chinese maritime)** — The Chinese practice of demanding symbolic tribute from foreign rulers in exchange for trade access and diplomatic recognition. (Significance: the mechanism by which China participated in the Indian Ocean network — not through private commerce but through state-managed diplomatic trade; Zheng He's voyages served this system.)

**Malacca Sultanate** — The Muslim-ruled state at Malacca (c. 1400–1511) that dominated Southeast Asian trade and spread Islam throughout the region. (Significance: a model for how Islam spread in Southeast Asia — through royal conversion rather than conquest; Malacca's rulers converted to Islam, and trade partners followed suit to gain commercial advantages.)

**East African gold trade** — The commerce in gold mined in the interior of Africa (particularly from Great Zimbabwe and surrounding regions) that flowed to the Swahili coast and from there into the Indian Ocean network. (Significance: East African gold was essential to Indian Ocean commerce; it connected the interior of Africa to the maritime world and funded the prosperity of Swahili city-states like Kilwa.)
