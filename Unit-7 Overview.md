**AP Period:** Period 4 — c. 1900 to the present
**Unit Theme:** *Global Conflict* — industrialized nationalism and economic instability produced the two most destructive wars in human history, killed approximately 85 million people in 45 years, ended European global dominance, and forced the international community to create new institutions against genocide and war.

---

## The Big Picture

Unit 7 covers 45 years that broke the 19th-century world and built the 20th. Between 1900 and 1945, two world wars killed more people than any conflict in recorded history, destroyed four empires, redrew every major map, and forced a fundamental rethinking of what states could do to their own citizens and to each other.

The story begins where Unit 6 ends: with a world divided into industrial empires competing for global dominance. The forces that drove imperialism — nationalism, militarism, alliance competition, economic rivalry — became lethal when combined with the industrial technologies of the factory age. Machine guns, poison gas, submarines, and artillery capable of firing shells twenty miles made WWI (1914–1918) the first truly industrial war. No previous war had killed millions on this scale; no previous war had required entire civilian economies to be mobilized for production.

WWI is the hinge of the unit. It caused the Russian Revolution (7.1), which produced the Soviet Union. It destroyed the Ottoman, Austro-Hungarian, and German empires, reshaping the map of Europe and the Middle East (7.2–7.3). The Treaty of Versailles (1919) settled the war in ways that created conditions for the next one: war guilt, reparations, and territorial humiliation produced the economic instability and political extremism of the 1920s–1930s (7.4–7.6). And the mandate system, which handed former Ottoman and German colonies to Britain and France as "trusteeships," radicalized anti-colonial movements across Asia and Africa (7.5) — a direct cause of the decolonization movements in Unit 8.

WWII (1939–1945) was, in important respects, a continuation of WWI fought with even more destructive technology: aerial bombing, armored divisions, and ultimately the atomic bomb. But WWII was also different in kind: it was the first war fought deliberately against civilian populations as a strategic objective. The [[Concept Notes/Holocaust|Holocaust]] — Nazi Germany's systematic murder of 6 million Jews and millions of others — was not a byproduct of the war but a policy implemented using the administrative and industrial capacity of a modern state (7.8). The international response — the Nuremberg Trials, the [[Concept Notes/Genocide|Genocide]] Convention, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights — created the framework of modern international human rights law.

For the AP exam, Unit 7's central argument is causation: understanding not just *what* happened but *why*, and how WWI's unresolved legacies directly caused WWII. The unit also asks students to think about the relationship between economic instability and political extremism — the Depression did not make fascism inevitable, but it made it possible. And it asks students to recognize that "global conflict" was never just a European story: Japan's imperial expansion, anti-colonial movements in India and Africa, and the decision to use atomic weapons in Asia all remind students that the world wars were fought and felt worldwide.

## Topic-by-Topic Narrative

### 7.1 — Shifting Power After World War I

The 20th century opened with empires — but several of those empires were already under internal stress by 1900. Topic 7.1 traces the revolutions and political upheavals that reshaped global power before and during WWI, producing the new political map that WWII would redraw again.

**Russia** experienced revolution twice. The Revolution of 1905 — triggered by military defeat against Japan (Russo-Japanese War, 1904–05) and "Bloody Sunday" — forced Tsar Nicholas II to create a parliament (the Duma) but did not fundamentally change the autocracy. The February Revolution of 1917 toppled the Tsar; the October Revolution brought the [[Concept Notes/Bolsheviks|Bolsheviks]] under Lenin to power. The Bolsheviks withdrew from WWI (Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, 1918), fought a brutal civil war, and by 1922 had established the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics — the world's first Communist state.

**China** overthrew the Qing Dynasty in 1911, establishing the Republic of China under Sun Yat-sen. The new republic was immediately weak and fragmented, setting the stage for decades of warlordism, Japanese invasion, and civil war that would culminate in the Communist victory of 1949 (Unit 8).

**The Ottoman Empire** collapsed during and after WWI. Arab nationalist movements, supported by Britain with promises of independence, rose against Ottoman rule. But Britain and France had already secretly agreed to divide Ottoman territories between themselves (the Sykes-Picot Agreement, 1916). The result — British and French mandates over the Middle East — generated resentments that shaped the region through the 20th century and beyond.

**Mexico** experienced its own revolution (1910–1920), overthrowing the Porfirio Díaz dictatorship in a complex, multi-faction conflict that produced a new constitution (1917) enshrining land reform and labor rights — one of the most radical constitutional documents of the era.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The Russian Revolution is a top-5 exam topic in Unit 7.** Know both 1905 (caused by military defeat; result was the Duma — a limited concession) and 1917 (caused by WWI strain; result was Bolshevik seizure and eventual Soviet Union). The AP loves to ask you to compare or connect these two events.

> [!tip]
> These revolutions share a pattern: **external military pressure + internal economic strain + weak government legitimacy = revolution**. Russia (1905 and 1917), China (1911), and Mexico (1910) all fit this template. Recognizing the pattern makes causation arguments easier to construct on SAQs and LEQs.

### 7.2 — Causes of World War I

WWI had no single cause — the AP exam tests whether you can explain how multiple long-term structural factors combined with a short-term trigger to produce catastrophe.

**Long-term causes (MAIN):** The AP textbook uses the MAIN acronym — Militarism, Alliance systems, Imperialism, [[Concept Notes/Nationalism|Nationalism]] — to organize the structural causes. European powers had been engaged in an arms race since the 1870s; Britain and Germany competed for naval supremacy. The alliance system meant that a conflict between any two powers could draw in all the others: the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) faced the Triple Entente (France, Russia, Britain). Imperial rivalries in Africa and Asia (Moroccan Crises of 1905 and 1911) had already nearly produced war. Nationalism — particularly in the multi-ethnic Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires, where Slavic, Greek, and Arab peoples sought self-determination — made the Balkans a powder keg.

**The trigger:** On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand (heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne) was assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist. Austria-Hungary issued an ultimatum to Serbia; Serbia's partial acceptance was deemed insufficient; Austria-Hungary declared war. The alliance system activated: Russia mobilized to support Serbia; Germany declared war on Russia and France; Germany invaded Belgium (the Schlieffen Plan); Britain declared war on Germany.

**Why did Europe *stay* at war?** Equally important for AP causation questions is understanding why the war lasted four years once it started. Governments found it politically impossible to accept a compromise peace after such sacrifice. Industrial mobilization locked economies into war production. And the military logic of the trenches — where offensive attacks against machine guns produced mass casualties without territorial gain — produced a stalemate neither side could break until American entry in 1917 shifted the balance.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The most common mistake on WWI causation questions is treating the assassination as "the cause."** The assassination was the trigger; the MAIN structural factors are the causes. An exam answer that explains only the assassination will score in the 1–2 range on a rubric. An answer that uses the assassination as a short-term trigger while explaining how the alliance system, nationalism, and imperial rivalry created the *conditions* for escalation will score much higher.

> [!warning]
> **Italy switched sides in WWI (1915), joining the Triple Entente against its treaty partners.** The Triple Alliance did not fight together. Italy's defection (motivated by territorial promises from Britain and France) is a good example of the limits of alliance systems and of the role of self-interest in wartime diplomacy.

### 7.3 — Conducting World War I

WWI introduced the world to industrial-scale killing. Topic 7.3 examines how the war was actually fought — the technologies, tactics, and human costs — and its transformative effects on societies worldwide.

**Trench warfare** defined the Western Front. From the English Channel to Switzerland, a line of trenches extended 400 miles, barely moving for four years. Machine guns made frontal assaults suicidal; the Battles of Verdun and the Somme (both 1916) each killed over a million men combined for territorial gains measured in miles. Artillery barrages preceded every offensive, achieving surprise at the cost of warning the enemy. The stalemate produced not just casualties but psychological trauma — "shell shock" (PTSD) on a mass scale.

**New technologies** appeared on WWI battlefields for the first time: poison gas (chlorine and mustard gas), tanks, aircraft (initially for reconnaissance, then for fighting and bombing), and submarines. Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare — sinking neutral shipping including the British liner *Lusitania* (1915) and attempting to cut Britain's Atlantic supply lines — was a key cause of American entry in 1917.

**Total war** meant the mobilization of entire civilian economies. Women entered industrial workforces in large numbers across Europe; colonial subjects were recruited as soldiers and laborers (African, Indian, and Southeast Asian troops fought for European powers); agricultural production was redirected toward military needs. The British naval blockade of Germany caused hundreds of thousands of civilian deaths from malnutrition by 1918.

**Colonial participation** was significant and often overlooked. France recruited ~600,000 soldiers from Africa; Britain deployed Indian, Australian, New Zealand, Canadian, and South African troops. The promise of recognition and self-determination made to colonial subjects during the war — never fully honored — radicalized anti-colonial movements afterward.

**The end:** American entry (April 1917), the collapse of the Russian front (Bolshevik withdrawal, 1917), and German military exhaustion combined to end the war. The Armistice was signed November 11, 1918. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) imposed war guilt, reparations of 132 billion gold marks, and territorial losses on Germany.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Total war is the key concept for 7.3.** The AP defines it as a war in which a nation mobilizes all available resources — economic, industrial, civilian, colonial — for the war effort. Know both the military dimension (new weapons, mass casualties) and the home front dimension (women's labor, rationing, propaganda, colonial recruitment).

### 7.4 — Economy in the Interwar Period

The interwar period (1919–1939) was defined by economic instability that destroyed the political center in many countries and made extremist movements possible. Topic 7.4 traces three connected economic stories: postwar instability, the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression|Great Depression]], and the rise of [[Concept Notes/Fascism|Fascism]] and communism as alternative economic models.

**Postwar instability** struck Germany first. The Versailles reparations burden, combined with postwar disruption, produced hyperinflation in the early 1920s — the German mark became worthless. A brief stabilization followed (the "Golden Twenties"), but Germany remained structurally dependent on American loans.

**The Great Depression** (1929–c. 1939) began with the Wall Street Crash (October 1929) and spread globally through bank failures, trade contraction, and mass unemployment. By 1932, a quarter of American workers were unemployed; German unemployment reached 30%. The Depression devastated international trade, as countries imposed protective tariffs in a self-defeating spiral. It also discredited liberal capitalism as an economic model, opening political space for alternatives.

**Fascism** emerged as one alternative. Benito Mussolini had taken power in Italy in 1922 (before the Depression); Adolf Hitler became German Chancellor in 1933 on a platform that combined nationalism, anti-communism, anti-Semitism, and a promise to restore German greatness. [[Concept Notes/Nazism|Nazism]] offered a totalitarian solution: the state directed the economy for national power, suppressed political opposition, and channeled popular grievance into ethnic scapegoating. Japan's military government pursued a similar nationalist-expansionist model.

**The Soviet alternative** was Stalin's command economy. Stalin's Five-Year Plans (beginning 1928) rapidly industrialized the USSR through state direction of all economic activity. The human cost was catastrophic — the collectivization of agriculture caused the [[Concept Notes/Holodomor|Holodomor]] (Ukrainian famine, 1932–33), killing millions — but Soviet industrial output grew dramatically. To Depression-era observers, the Soviet model seemed to prove that central planning worked where capitalism had failed.

**The [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic|Weimar Republic]]'s collapse** is the AP's central case study for how economic crisis enables authoritarianism. The Depression turned Germany's political crisis into catastrophe: the Nazi party went from 2.6% of the vote in 1928 to 37% in 1932. Hitler was appointed chancellor through constitutional means in January 1933, then dismantled the constitution within months.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The Depression → fascism causal chain is the unit's most tested argument.** Economic instability alone did not produce fascism — many countries experienced the Depression without turning fascist. The AP rewards answers that identify *why Germany specifically* was vulnerable: WWI humiliation, Weimar legitimacy deficit, hyperinflation trauma, and the particular appeal of Nazi scapegoating to specific social groups (the middle class, veterans, rural populations).

### 7.5 — Unresolved Tensions After World War I

Topic 7.5 examines the peace that failed — the Treaty of Versailles and the mandate system — and the anti-colonial movements that the war's broken promises energized.

**The Treaty of Versailles** (1919) settled WWI but planted the seeds of WWII. The "war guilt clause" (Article 231) assigned sole responsibility for the war to Germany, justifying massive reparations. Germany lost 13% of its territory and 10% of its population. The settlement humiliated Germany without destroying its capacity for recovery — a classic example of a peace harsh enough to generate resentment but not thorough enough to prevent remilitarization. John Maynard Keynes, the British economist who attended the Paris Peace Conference, predicted this outcome in *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919).

**The mandate system** was the peace settlement's treatment of former Ottoman and German colonies. Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points had promised national self-determination — but this principle was applied in Europe while colonies were simply redistributed among the victors. Britain and France received League of Nations "mandates" over territories in the Middle East, Africa, and the Pacific, exercising colonial control under a nominally international framework. The gap between the rhetoric of self-determination and the reality of the mandate system radicalized colonial subjects worldwide.

**Anti-colonial movements** intensified in the war's aftermath:
- In **India**, the Amritsar Massacre (1919) — where British troops killed ~400 unarmed protesters — galvanized the independence movement. Gandhi's non-violent resistance campaigns drew on Thoreau's civil disobedience and Hindu tradition; the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress|Indian National Congress]] became a mass movement rather than an elite petition body.
- In **China**, the May Fourth Movement (1919) erupted when news arrived that the Versailles settlement had awarded German concessions in Shandong to Japan rather than returning them to China. Intellectuals and students rejected both Western imperialism and Confucian tradition, seeking a "third way" — a context in which Marxism became attractive to Chinese reformers.
- **[[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism|Pan-Africanism]]** and the **Négritude movement** articulated a shared identity and solidarity among African and African-diaspora peoples against colonial rule and racism. Marcus Garvey's "Back to Africa" movement mobilized the African-American diaspora; W. E. B. Du Bois organized Pan-African Congresses.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The mandate system is the AP's primary connection between WWI and decolonization.** When asked to explain causes of anti-colonial movements in the 20th century, always include the mandate system's betrayal of self-determination promises and the Versailles settlement's application of different standards to European and non-European peoples.

> [!tip]
> **Gandhi's non-violent resistance method** directly influenced Martin Luther King Jr.'s Civil Rights Movement — a cross-topic connection the AP tests in Long Essay Questions about reform movements. Know the method (satyagraha — civil disobedience, non-cooperation, boycotts) and the reasoning (expose the moral contradiction of colonial rule by accepting suffering without retaliating).

### 7.6 — Causes of World War II

Topic 7.6 traces the decade of crises (1931–1939) that led to WWII — and asks why democratic governments failed to stop fascist aggression before it became a world war.

**Japanese expansion** in Asia preceded WWII in Europe by nearly a decade. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and established the puppet state of Manchukuo; the League of Nations condemned the action but took no military steps to reverse it. Japan invaded China proper in 1937, committing the Nanjing Massacre (killing ~200,000 Chinese civilians). Japan's imperial logic was explicitly anti-Western: the **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere** framed Japanese expansion as Asian liberation from European colonialism, even as Japan imposed its own brutal colonial rule.

**Nazi aggression** in Europe followed a pattern of testing and exceeding Western tolerance:
- 1935: Germany reintroduced conscription (banned by Versailles)
- 1936: Germany remilitarized the Rhineland (demilitarized by Versailles)
- 1938: Germany annexed Austria (Anschluss)
- 1938: Germany seized the Sudetenland (Czechoslovakia's German-speaking region)
- 1939: Germany occupied all of Czechoslovakia, then invaded Poland

**Appeasement** — the policy of making concessions to aggressive powers to avoid war — is the period's defining political failure. The Munich Agreement (September 1938), in which Britain and France allowed Hitler to annex the Sudetenland in exchange for a promise of no further territorial demands, is the canonical example. British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain returned to London claiming "peace for our time." Six months later, Germany occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia.

**Why appeasement?** The AP expects students to explain the appeasers' reasoning, not just condemn their failure. Britain and France were genuinely unprepared for war militarily; their populations had been traumatized by WWI and were deeply averse to another conflict; there was real (if mistaken) hope that Hitler's goals were limited; and some British conservatives saw Nazi Germany as a useful buffer against Soviet communism.

**The German-Soviet [[Concept Notes/Nonaggression Pact|Nonaggression Pact]]** (August 1939) shocked the world: the ideological enemies agreed to neutrality, secretly dividing Eastern Europe between them. Hitler invaded Poland on September 1, 1939; Britain and France declared war two days later.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Compare WWI and WWII causes — a favorite Long Essay prompt.** Both involved alliance systems, nationalism, and economic factors — but WWII added the specific legacies of Versailles (German resentment, Weimar instability) and the agency of specific leaders (Hitler, Mussolini, Hirohito's military government) pursuing deliberate expansionist programs. WWI was more structurally determined; WWII involved more individual decision-making within a structural context.

### 7.7 — Conducting World War II

WWII was fought on every inhabited continent, killed approximately 70–85 million people, and ended with weapons that could destroy cities in seconds. Topic 7.7 traces the war's major turning points and its transformative consequences for the international order.

**Blitzkrieg** ("lightning war") — coordinated armored and air assault — shattered the French defense in six weeks (May–June 1940). The speed and success of German operations in Poland, France, and the Low Countries reflected the integration of tanks, motorized infantry, and tactical aircraft in ways the Allies had not yet matched. Britain survived the **Battle of Britain** (1940) through RAF fighter defense and Hitler's decision not to attempt a Channel crossing.

**The Eastern Front** was the war's decisive theater. Operation Barbarossa (June 1941) opened the largest military campaign in history: Germany invaded the USSR with 3 million troops along a 1,800-mile front. Initial German advances were enormous; but Soviet resistance stiffened, the Russian winter struck, and at **Stalingrad** (1942–43) the USSR encircled and destroyed an entire German army. After Stalingrad, Germany was permanently on the defensive in the east. The Eastern Front cost the USSR approximately 27 million dead — by far the war's largest national death toll.

**American entry** (December 1941, following Pearl Harbor) shifted the industrial balance decisively. American war production — 300,000 aircraft, 86,000 tanks, 6,500 naval vessels — dwarfed any other power. The "island-hopping" campaign across the Pacific, the North African and Italian campaigns, and the Normandy invasion (June 1944) progressively squeezed the Axis.

**The atomic bomb** ended the war in the Pacific. The bombings of Hiroshima (August 6, 1945) and Nagasaki (August 9, 1945) killed ~200,000 people immediately; Japan surrendered on August 15. The bomb's use remains historically contested: Was it necessary to avoid an invasion that might have killed millions? Was it a demonstration of power aimed partly at the Soviet Union? The AP does not require students to resolve this debate but does expect them to understand the bomb's historical significance as a new category of weapon and a turning point in international relations.

**Postwar consequences** are central to the unit's AP argument. WWII produced the architecture of the modern international order: the United Nations (1945), the International Monetary Fund and World Bank (Bretton Woods, 1944), and the Nuremberg Trials, which established that "following orders" was not a defense for war crimes. American and Soviet power replaced European dominance — setting the stage for the Cold War (Unit 8).

> [!exam-tip]
> **The Allied coalition's breakup is as important as its unity.** The US, Britain, and USSR cooperated to defeat Germany but had fundamentally different war aims and postwar visions. Understanding this tension explains why the Cold War began immediately after Germany's surrender.

### 7.8 — Mass Atrocities

Topic 7.8 is the unit's moral center. It examines genocide and mass killing not as aberrations but as organized state policies implemented using modern administrative and industrial methods — and the international response that these events generated.

**The [[Concept Notes/Holocaust|Holocaust]]** (1941–1945) was Nazi Germany's systematic murder of 6 million Jews and approximately 5–6 million others (Roma, Soviet POWs, people with disabilities, political opponents, homosexuals). It proceeded in stages: legal discrimination (Nuremberg Laws, 1935), forced emigration, concentration and forced labor, and finally industrialized mass killing at death camps (Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibor). The Holocaust was not incidental to WWII — it was a parallel operation that consumed significant German military and administrative resources even when the war was going badly. The term "**[[Concept Notes/Genocide|Genocide]]**" — deliberately destroying a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group — was coined by Raphael Lemkin in 1944 specifically in response to the Holocaust.

**The Armenian Genocide** (1915–1916) was carried out by the Ottoman government during WWI: ~1.5 million Armenians were killed through mass execution, death marches, and starvation. The Ottoman government systematically destroyed Armenian communities across Anatolia, using WWI as cover. The Armenian Genocide is significant as an early 20th-century precedent and as evidence that genocide is not unique to any particular culture, state, or era.

**The Nanjing Massacre** (1937–1938) saw Japanese troops kill ~200,000 Chinese civilians and POWs following the fall of Nanjing. Alongside the broader pattern of Japanese conduct in China — forced labor, medical experimentation, sexual slavery — the Nanjing Massacre illustrates how imperial military culture can produce atrocity on a mass scale.

**The international response** established the framework of modern human rights:
- The **Nuremberg Trials** (1945–46) prosecuted Nazi leaders for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and conspiracy to wage aggressive war. The principle that individual officials could be held criminally responsible for state policy — that "I was following orders" was not a defense — was new in international law.
- The **[[Concept Notes/Genocide|Genocide]] Convention** (1948) made genocide an international crime.
- The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (1948) established a global framework of individual rights that states were obligated to respect.
- The **[[Concept Notes/International Criminal Court|International Criminal Court]]** (established 2002) institutionalized international criminal justice.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The AP links mass atrocities to the human rights framework as a cause-and-effect relationship.** The Holocaust, Armenian Genocide, and Nanjing Massacre are evidence for the argument that 20th-century violence drove the creation of international human rights institutions. Know the institutions and their founding documents, not just the atrocities themselves.

> [!warning]
> **Do not treat the Holocaust as unique to the point of being incomparable.** The AP expects comparative thinking. The Armenian Genocide (1915), Nanjing Massacre (1937–38), and later atrocities (Cambodia, Rwanda, Bosnia — Unit 8) share structural features: state direction, dehumanizing ideology, bureaucratic organization, and the use of wartime conditions to provide cover. The comparison does not diminish any atrocity — it helps explain how such events occur.

### 7.9 — Causation in Global Conflict

Topic 7.9 is the CCOT synthesis chapter: it asks students to step back from the individual events of 7.1–7.8 and analyze the unit's patterns — what changed, what persisted, and what caused what across the arc of 1900–1945.

**The core argument:** Both world wars shared structural causes — nationalism, economic rivalry, military alliance systems, imperial competition — but WWII was also caused by the *specific failure* of WWI's settlement. Versailles created the conditions that fascism exploited; fascism pursued a deliberate program of expansion that appeasement failed to deter. This means WWII was both structurally overdetermined (the conditions were there) and contingently caused (specific decisions by specific leaders mattered).

**What changed between 1900 and 1945:**
- Military technology escalated from machine guns and poison gas to strategic bombing and atomic weapons
- The scale of civilian death rose dramatically (WWI: ~7–10 million civilian deaths; WWII: ~50+ million)
- The United States replaced Britain as the world's leading power
- European empires were fatally weakened — decolonization became inevitable (Unit 8)
- International institutions (League of Nations → United Nations) became a permanent feature of global governance
- The concept of international human rights emerged as a legal framework

**What persisted:**
- Nationalism remained the dominant political ideology worldwide
- Economic inequality among nations continued and deepened
- Great-power rivalry continued — shifting from European imperial competition to US-Soviet Cold War competition
- Colonial relationships persisted (only beginning to unravel after 1945)

**AP skill focus:** Topic 7.9 is primarily about the historical reasoning skill of **causation** — specifically, distinguishing between long-term structural causes and short-term triggers, evaluating the relative significance of multiple causes, and recognizing that causation operates at multiple levels (individual decisions, structural conditions, contingent events).

> [!exam-tip]
> **LEQ causation prompts often ask you to evaluate a claim like "The Treaty of Versailles caused World War II."** A sophisticated response agrees that Versailles created *conditions* for WWII without treating those conditions as *determinative*. Include: (1) how Versailles created economic instability and nationalist resentment in Germany; (2) how the Depression converted that resentment into political power for extremists; (3) how appeasement failed to halt aggression once it began. Then complicate the thesis: Japan's imperial expansion had independent causes; the Soviet-German pact was a specific decision by specific leaders; WWII might have happened even with a better Versailles.

## AP Exam Strategy for Unit 7

**This unit appears on every AP exam.** WWI and WWII are Period 4 anchors — you will see at least one MCQ set, one SAQ, and very likely an LEQ option from this unit on the actual exam.

**Highest-frequency LEQ prompts:**
- Explain the causes of WWI (long-term vs. short-term)
- Compare the causes of WWI and WWII
- Evaluate the extent to which the Treaty of Versailles caused WWII
- Explain the causes and effects of economic instability in the interwar period
- Explain how total war changed the nature of warfare and its effects on society
- Explain the causes and effects of mass atrocities in the 20th century

**Cross-unit connections to master:**
- Unit 6 → 7: Imperialism and nationalist competition are direct structural causes of WWI
- Unit 7 → 8: WWII's outcome (American/Soviet power, weakened European empires) causes decolonization and the Cold War
- Unit 7 → 9: Postwar institutions (UN, IMF, human rights framework) shape the post-1945 world

**The unit's three most important causal chains:**
1. WWI causes → Versailles → economic instability → fascism → WWII
2. Colonial participation in WWI + mandate system betrayal → radicalized anti-colonial movements → decolonization
3. Holocaust + other atrocities → Nuremberg Trials + Genocide Convention + UDHR → international human rights law
