# Shifting Power Overview

**Unit 7 — Global Conflict** | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 453–460
**AP Themes:** GOV · CDI · SIO | **AP Skill:** Causation
**Essential Question:** How did internal and external factors contribute to change in various states after 1900?

## The Big Picture

In the first two decades of the 20th century, revolutions toppled authoritarian governments in Russia, China, and Mexico while the Ottoman Empire collapsed under war and internal decay. Each case follows a recognizable pattern: governments weakened by internal problems — economic stagnation, ethnic tension, land inequality — became vulnerable to external shocks such as military defeat and foreign economic domination. The outcomes differed dramatically (communist state, secular republic, single-party reform state), but all represent a fundamental redistribution of political power away from old empires and hereditary autocracies.

## Revolution in Russia (pp. 453–454)

Russia in 1900 was falling behind the industrializing West. The tsarist government was slow to expand education, infrastructure, or entrepreneurial support, and actively resisted political reform. Military defeats exposed this weakness: the Crimean War (1853–1856) and the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) — the first time a modern East Asian state defeated a European power — revealed just how fragile the regime was.

**Bloody Sunday** (January 22, 1905) — when tsarist troops fired on peaceful petitioners demanding better wages and voting rights, killing around 1,300 — triggered a general strike of 400,000 workers and the Revolution of 1905. The tsar survived by granting a parliament (Duma) but never genuinely reformed. World War I delivered the fatal blow: Russia's poorly trained, ill-equipped army suffered catastrophic losses and civilians faced severe food shortages. In 1917 the tsar abdicated. In October 1917 the **Bolsheviks** under Vladimir Lenin seized power, establishing the world's first communist government. They abolished private trade, redistributed food to urban workers, and nationalized industry. Their success alarmed capitalist powers worldwide and planted the seed of the Cold War's defining ideological conflict.

> [!exam-tip]
> Russia's 1917 revolution had **two distinct phases**: the February Revolution (liberal/moderate forces topple the tsar) and the October Revolution (Bolsheviks seize power from the provisional government). AP questions often focus on *causes* — internal weakness compounded by WWI.

## Upheaval in China (pp. 454–456)

China's Qing Dynasty (1644–1911) faced three compounding internal problems: ethnic tension between Manchu rulers and the Han majority, recurring famine caused by rapid population growth outpacing agricultural expansion, and chronically low government revenues that starved infrastructure. European industrialization sharpened China's vulnerability as Western powers forced unequal trade terms and carved out spheres of influence.

In 1911 a revolutionary movement led by **Sun Yat-sen** overthrew the Qing, ending over 2,000 years of imperial rule. Sun's political vision — the **Three People's Principles** of nationalism (central Chinese authority), democracy (governance by capable experts), and livelihood (reducing extreme inequality) — guided the new republic. His **Kuomintang** (Chinese Nationalist Party) never consolidated control, however. Warlords dominated most of the country, and Sun stepped aside after just two months. The Kuomintang would regain power later but ultimately lose a civil war to the Chinese Communists (see **Establishment of the People's Republic of China** [[Unit-7.5/Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview|Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview]]).

## Ottoman Collapse and the Rise of Turkey (pp. 456–457)

By 1900 the Ottoman Empire was "the sick man of Europe" — export revenues had collapsed and the empire relied mainly on its position as a trade hub. The **Young Turks** emerged as reformers advocating a Western-style constitution and **Turkification**, a policy requiring all ethnic groups to adopt Turkish-Islamic identity. Armenians, the largest Christian minority, were scapegoated for Ottoman economic decline, setting the stage for mass atrocities. Resentment of British and French economic domination led the empire to ally secretly with Germany in WWI — a strategic miscalculation. After Germany's defeat the Allies dismantled the empire and occupied Anatolia.

**Kemal Atatürk** led the Turkish National Movement to expel Allied forces (1921) and founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923, becoming its first president. He modeled Turkey on Western secular democracy — establishing public education for girls, abolishing polygyny, extending women's suffrage, and adopting Western dress — while ruling as an authoritarian dictator until his death in 1938.

## Power Shifts in Mexico (pp. 457–458)

Mexico under dictator **Porfirio Díaz** (1876–1911) achieved surface stability at a severe social cost: the wealthiest 1% controlled 97% of the land, typical peasants were landless, and U.S. investors controlled key national resources. When Díaz jailed opposition candidate **Francisco Madero** in 1910, it ignited the **Mexican Revolution**. Madero organized forces from El Paso, Texas; troops under **Francisco "Pancho" Villa** defeated the government army and sent Díaz into exile. **Emiliano Zapata** led actual land redistribution to impoverished peasants in the south.

The revolution was devastating — roughly 2 million deaths out of a population of 15 million between 1910 and 1920, with political violence continuing into the 1930s. But it produced two stabilizing legacies: the landmark **1917 Constitution** (guaranteeing land redistribution, universal suffrage, and public education) and the **Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI)**, formed in 1929, which dominated Mexican politics until 2000.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate the **1917 Mexican Constitution** with the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution — two very different events in the same year. The Mexican Constitution was a reform document enshrining revolutionary goals; the Bolshevik takeover was a violent seizure of power establishing a communist state.

> [!tip]
> **AP comparison frame:** All four cases share internal causes (autocratic rule, economic inequality, ethnic or class tension) and external triggers (foreign domination or military defeat). Compare outcomes: communist one-party state (Russia), unstable republic → civil war (China), secular nationalist authoritarian republic (Turkey), reform single-party state (Mexico).
