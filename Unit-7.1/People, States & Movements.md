## Russia

**Nicholas II (r. 1894–1917)**
The last Russian tsar, whose refusal to accept representative government made him a symbol of autocratic rigidity. His quote — "I shall never, under any circumstances, agree to a representative form of government because I consider it harmful to the people" — captures the fatal miscalculation of his reign. Military humiliations (Russo-Japanese War, WWI) and his inability to address civilian suffering eroded all legitimacy. He abdicated in 1917 and was executed with his family in 1918.

**Vladimir Lenin (1870–1924)**
Marxist theorist and leader of the Bolshevik Party who adapted Marx's revolutionary theory to Russian conditions. Where Marx predicted revolution would emerge naturally in industrialized economies, Lenin argued for a disciplined "vanguard party" of professional revolutionaries to seize power on behalf of workers. He led the October 1917 coup, immediately implemented communist economic policy (nationalizing industry, abolishing private trade), and created the Soviet Union — the world's first communist state. His model of Marxism-Leninism became the template for 20th-century communist revolutions from China to Cuba.

**The [[Concept Notes/Bolsheviks\|Bolsheviks]]**
Revolutionary party representing Russia's industrial working class. Seized power in October 1917 ("the October Revolution") and established a communist government. Their immediate policies — abolishing private trade, nationalizing factories, redistributing food — were radical breaks from the tsarist and provisional government order. Their survival through the ensuing civil war (1917–1922) cemented Soviet power and made communism a global political force for the next 70 years.

> [!exam-tip]
> The **Bolsheviks** (October 1917) are distinct from the broader revolutionary movement that toppled the tsar (February 1917). The February Revolution was led by liberals and moderate socialists; the Bolsheviks then outmaneuvered those moderates to seize power.

## China

**[[Concept Notes/Qing Dynasty\|Qing Dynasty]] (1644–1911)**
China's last imperial dynasty, ruled by the [[Concept Notes/Manchu\|Manchu]] ethnic minority. Never fully accepted by the Han majority. By the late 19th century the Qing faced: (1) ethnic tension with Han Chinese, (2) persistent famine from overpopulation relative to farmland, (3) insufficient tax revenues to maintain infrastructure, and (4) European economic imperialism that forced unequal trade concessions. The dynasty collapsed in 1911 under Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary movement.

**Sun Yat-sen (1866–1925)**
"Father of the Chinese Republic" and founder of the Kuomintang. A Christian educated in Hawaii and Hong Kong, Sun blended Confucian traditions with modern political thought in his Three People's Principles: *nationalism* (loyalty to a central Chinese state, expulsion of foreign domination), *democracy* (governance by capable experts rather than the masses), and *livelihood* (ending extreme inequality and foreign economic exploitation). He led the 1911 revolution but stepped aside after two months, lacking the military strength to govern. Both the Kuomintang and the Chinese Communists later claimed Sun as their founder.

**Kuomintang (Chinese Nationalist Party)**
Political party founded by Sun Yat-sen that became the dominant force in Republican China. After Sun's death it came under the leadership of Chiang Kai-shek, ruled China for two decades, and ultimately lost the Chinese Civil War to Mao Zedong's Communists in 1949 (see **Establishment of the People's Republic of China** [[Unit-7.5/Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview|Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview]]). The Kuomintang government then retreated to Taiwan, where it governed until democratic elections in 1996.

**Chinese Warlords**
Regional military strongmen who dominated much of China after the Qing collapse. Their fragmentation of power prevented Sun Yat-sen's republic from functioning effectively and made unified national governance impossible for decades. Warlordism is a recurring consequence of revolutionary collapse when no single force has enough military strength to control the whole country.

## Ottoman Empire & Turkey

**The Young Turks**
Reform movement that took control of the Ottoman government in the early 20th century. Advocated a constitution and modernization on Western models, but increasingly turned to Turkification — forced cultural assimilation — as a solution to the empire's ethnic diversity and economic decline. Their decision to ally with Germany in WWI proved catastrophic. Their legacy includes both genuine modernizing impulses and responsibility for mass atrocities against Armenians.

**Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (1881–1938)**
Military officer who led the Turkish National Movement to defeat Allied occupation of Anatolia (1921) and founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923 as its first president. Atatürk pursued a sweeping secular modernization program: replacing Islamic law with secular codes, adopting the Latin alphabet, establishing public education for girls, abolishing polygyny, extending women's suffrage, and discouraging religious clothing in public life. He ruled as an authoritarian dictator — simultaneously modernizer and strongman — until his death in 1938. The national assembly honored him with the surname Atatürk ("Father of the Turks").

> [!tip]
> Atatürk's secular modernization **from above** parallels Peter the Great's Westernization of Russia in the 18th century — both leaders imposed dramatic cultural change through authoritarian power rather than democratic consent. This is a productive AP comparison.

**Republic of Turkey (est. 1923)**
Successor state to the Ottoman Empire, created after Atatürk expelled Allied forces from Anatolia. Significantly smaller than the Ottoman Empire, it was defined by ethnic Turkish nationalism rather than the empire's multiethnic model. Its creation demonstrated how WWI dismantled old empires and produced new nation-states aligned with the principle of national [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]].

## Mexico

**Porfirio Díaz (1830–1915)**
Mexican president-dictator who ruled from 1876 to 1911, a period called the *Porfiriato*. Promoted economic growth and foreign investment but at the cost of extreme inequality: the wealthiest 1% owned 97% of land, and U.S. corporations controlled key sectors (mining, railroads, oil). His heavy-handed suppression of opposition and jailing of Madero in 1910 triggered the revolution that sent him into exile. Díaz is a textbook example of the modernizing dictator whose development benefits elites and foreign investors while impoverishing the majority.

**Francisco Madero (1873–1913)**
Liberal politician and the immediate trigger of the Mexican Revolution. His jailing by Díaz in 1910 galvanized opposition; after escaping to El Paso he coordinated the revolutionary forces that toppled Díaz. Madero became president but was overthrown and murdered by General Victoriano Huerta in 1913, illustrating how the Mexican Revolution quickly became a multi-sided conflict rather than a simple democracy-vs-dictatorship story.

**Francisco "Pancho" Villa (1878–1923)**
Northern Mexico's most famous revolutionary general, whose cavalry forces defeated Díaz's army in 1911. A former bandit turned military commander, Villa became a folk hero celebrated for his charisma and populist image. He operated primarily in Chihuahua, conducting raids (including a 1916 raid into New Mexico that drew a U.S. military response) and challenging both Díaz and later governments. Represents the popular, military, northern dimension of the revolution.

**Emiliano Zapata (1879–1919)**
Agrarian revolutionary from Morelos in southern Mexico who championed land redistribution. His slogan "¡Tierra y Libertad!" (Land and Liberty) articulated the revolution's core promise to landless peasants. Unlike Villa's cavalry tactics, Zapata's movement focused on physically seizing and redistributing hacienda lands to villages. Assassinated in 1919, Zapata became the revolution's most enduring symbol of peasant rights and land justice — his ideology (Zapatismo) still animates Mexican politics.

**Institutional Revolutionary Party — PRI**
Founded in 1929 to consolidate the gains of the Mexican Revolution, the PRI dominated Mexican politics for 71 years. By monopolizing patronage networks, controlling media, and co-opting opposition, the PRI maintained power through corruption as much as legitimacy. All Mexican presidents from 1929 to 2000 were PRI members. Its long reign is a striking example of how revolutionary movements can institutionalize into new forms of single-party authoritarianism — compare to the Soviet Communist Party or China's Kuomintang.

> [!exam-tip]
> For SAQ/LEQ comparisons: All four revolutions in Topic 7.1 produced **new single-party or authoritarian states** (Soviet Union, Chinese warlord republic/Kuomintang, Republic of Turkey under Atatürk, PRI Mexico). The AP often asks students to compare revolutionary *causes* or *outcomes* across regions.
