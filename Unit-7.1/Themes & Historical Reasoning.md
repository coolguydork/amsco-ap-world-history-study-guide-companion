**Unit 7 — Global Conflict** | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 453–460

## AP Theme Analysis

### GOV — Governance

Topic 7.1 is fundamentally about governance failure and transformation. In all four cases — Russia, China, Mexico, and the Ottoman Empire — authoritarian regimes that refused to reform were overthrown (or dismantled from outside) and replaced by new political orders. The AP CED frames this as "shifts in power after 1900," and the mechanisms are remarkably consistent: governments that lost legitimacy through inequality, military defeat, or ethnic conflict became vulnerable to revolutionary coalitions.

The outcomes show governance diversity: Russia became a one-party communist state (the Soviet Union), China fragmented into warlordism before eventual Kuomintang and later Communist rule, Turkey became a secular authoritarian republic, and Mexico developed a one-party reform state under the PRI. None of these were liberal democracies, but all represented breaks from the hereditary empires they replaced.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP often asks: "What were the causes of political change after 1900?" The answer must include **both internal** (inequality, ethnic tension, weak economies, autocratic refusal to reform) **and external** factors (military defeat, foreign economic domination). The essential question for this topic explicitly calls for both.

### CDI — Cultural Developments & Interactions

Three ideological frameworks competed for dominance in this period:

**Communism** (Russia): Marx's theory, adapted by Lenin, proposed collective ownership and worker governance as the path to a just society. The Soviet model's early success terrified capitalist governments and inspired revolutionaries worldwide. The communism-vs-capitalism tension born here structured much of 20th-century world history.

**Secular Nationalism** (Turkey, China): Both Atatürk and Sun Yat-sen combined Western political models with indigenous cultural traditions. Atatürk explicitly Westernized Turkey's institutions while retaining Turkish ethnic identity. Sun Yat-sen blended Confucian values with democratic and nationalist ideas in his Three People's Principles. Both cases show non-Western leaders selectively borrowing Western concepts rather than wholesale adopting them.

**Turkification** (Ottoman Empire): The Young Turks' effort to impose cultural homogeneity on a multiethnic empire was a response to perceived weakness — by erasing ethnic difference, they hoped to create a unified, strong nation. This led directly to the persecution of Armenians, demonstrating how cultural nationalism can generate state-sponsored violence.

### SIO — Social Interactions & Organization

Land inequality was the explosive social issue in Mexico and China. In Mexico, 1% of the population owned 97% of the land; in China, landlordism combined with famine and ethnic hierarchy. Both revolutions promised (and to varying degrees delivered) land redistribution. Zapata's movement in Mexico physically redistributed hacienda lands; the 1917 Constitution enshrined land reform as a constitutional right.

Class conflict drove Russia's revolution. The Bolsheviks explicitly framed their seizure of power as the working class defeating the aristocracy and bourgeoisie. Their policies — nationalizing industry, abolishing private trade — were designed to eliminate class hierarchy at a stroke. In practice, power concentrated in a new communist party elite, but the revolutionary rhetoric of class liberation resonated globally.

Ethnic minority scapegoating characterized the Ottoman collapse. As the empire weakened economically, Armenians were blamed for problems rooted in structural decline and foreign economic domination. Turkification transformed ethnic difference from a political challenge into a national security threat, with catastrophic consequences for minority populations.

## AP Skill: Causation

The core skill for Topic 7.1 is **causation** — distinguishing internal from external causes of revolution, and understanding which were *necessary* vs. *sufficient* conditions.

**Internal causes (present in all four cases):**
- Autocratic governments resisted reform and political participation
- Extreme economic inequality (land, wealth, political access)
- Weak or outdated economic institutions that fell behind industrializing rivals
- Military and administrative inefficiency

**External causes (varying by case):**
- Military defeat (Russia: Russo-Japanese War, WWI; Ottoman Empire: WWI)
- Foreign economic domination (China: European spheres of influence; Mexico: U.S. investment control)
- Competitive pressure from industrializing powers

**Key causation insight:** Internal weaknesses made these regimes fragile; external shocks delivered the breaking point. Neither alone was sufficient. A strong government could have survived external pressure (as Japan did); an externally unchallenged government might have muddled through internal discontent (as many autocracies did).

> [!tip]
> For LEQ or SAQ causation questions: **Argue for the *relative significance* of causes.** Don't just list causes — argue which was most important and why. For example: "While WWI provided the immediate trigger for Russia's 1917 revolution, the underlying cause was decades of autocratic refusal to modernize politically or economically."

## Think as a Historian: Brinton's Anatomy of Revolution (p. 460)

Historian Crane Brinton (1938) identified four stages shared by the English (1640s), American (1776), French (1789), and Russian (1917) revolutions:

1. Citizens grow dissatisfied with government
2. Moderates gain power
3. Radicals seize control in a "terror" phase
4. A period of relative calm and acceptance follows (often under a strong leader)

**Applying Brinton to Topic 7.1 cases:**

| Revolution | Stage 1 | Stage 2 | Stage 3 | Stage 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Russia | Worker/peasant discontent, 1905 | Provisional Government, Feb. 1917 | Bolshevik seizure + Red Terror | Stalin consolidates power |
| Mexico | Peasant/opposition dissatisfaction under Díaz | Madero's liberal presidency | Villa/Zapata radical land seizures, factional violence | PRI stabilization (1929) |
| China | Han resentment of Manchu Qing | Republic under Sun Yat-sen | Warlord fragmentation | Kuomintang consolidation (partial) |
| Turkey | Ottoman decline, Young Turk reform attempts | Allied occupation + nationalist resistance | Turkish National Movement military campaign | Atatürk's authoritarian republic |

**Brinton's own caveat:** The American Revolution lacked a "terror" phase. Top-down reforms imposed by elites often produced more lasting social change than the political revolutions he studied. This is worth remembering when comparing Atatürk's reforms (top-down, lasting) with the Mexican Revolution (bottom-up, more contested).

## Historical Perspectives

The revolutions of the early 20th century have been interpreted through several competing frameworks:

**Modernization theory** sees these revolutions as necessary (if violent) steps toward modern nation-states, democracy, and economic development. From this view, the Young Turks, Sun Yat-sen, and Atatürk were progressive forces bringing backward empires into the modern world.

**Marxist/class conflict analysis** emphasizes economic inequality as the root cause: peasants and workers revolted against landowners, industrialists, and foreign capitalists who extracted wealth from their labor. Mexico's land inequality and Russia's industrial exploitation fit this framework well.

**Dependency/world systems theory** foregrounds foreign economic domination: China, Mexico, and the Ottoman Empire were economically subordinated to industrial powers, which created the resentments and structural weaknesses that made revolution possible. The revolution was as much against foreign exploitation as against domestic autocracy.

**Nationalist interpretation** frames these as struggles for national self-determination — peoples asserting their right to govern themselves against foreign powers or alien dynasties (Manchus in China, Ottoman sultans in Turkey, U.S. investors in Mexico).
