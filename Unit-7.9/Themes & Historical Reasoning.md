# Themes & Historical Reasoning

## AP Themes in This Topic

**GOV — Governance**
Global conflict both destroyed and created states. The Ottoman, Russian, and Qing empires collapsed. New states emerged from decolonization (India, Pakistan, etc.). Totalitarian regimes (Nazi Germany, Soviet Union, Fascist Italy) arose from the political and economic instability that conflict produced. The [[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]] (1945) represented a new attempt to manage interstate relations through international institutions — a major change from the pre-WWI system of bilateral alliances.

**ECN — Economic Systems**
Imperial rivalry over markets and resources was a root economic cause of both world wars. The [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]] demonstrated how global economic interconnection meant collapse in one region (U.S.) cascaded worldwide, creating conditions for radical political movements. Japan's resource scarcity drove Pacific expansion, directly connecting economic motive to military action.

**CDI — Cultural Developments and Interactions**
[[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] — the ideology that a people sharing culture and ethnicity deserve their own state — was the most powerful cultural force driving both WWI and WWII. After WWII, the same force powered decolonization: colonized peoples demanded self-rule on the same nationalist grounds that European states had long claimed for themselves.

**TEC — Technology and Innovation**
Industrial technology transformed warfare's scale and lethality: machine guns, poison gas, long-range artillery, aerial bombing, and ultimately the atomic bomb. Each escalation meant more civilian deaths. Technology also enabled states to mobilize resources and populations at unprecedented scale, making [[Concept Notes/Total War\|Total War]] possible.

## The Causation Skill — AP Framework

**What AP means by "causation":**
Causation is not just listing factors. It requires explaining *how* a cause made an outcome more likely, identifying the *mechanism* by which cause produced effect, and evaluating *relative significance* — which cause mattered most and why.

**Types of causes:**
- **Underlying (long-term) causes** — structural conditions that made conflict possible: imperial rivalry, nationalism, arms race, alliance system. These built up over decades.
- **Immediate (short-term) causes** — specific events that triggered war: assassination of Franz Ferdinand (WWI); German invasion of Poland (WWII in Europe); Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor (WWII in Pacific).
- **Necessary vs. sufficient causes** — underlying causes were *necessary* (war couldn't happen without them) but not *sufficient* alone; immediate causes were the triggers that made structural tensions erupt.

**Chain causation in Unit 7:**
WWI → Treaty of Versailles humiliation → economic instability → rise of fascism → WWII → decolonization → superpower rivalry → Cold War. Each link in this chain is both an effect of what came before and a cause of what follows.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP LEQ and DBQ rubrics award the **complexity point** for demonstrating how a cause became an effect that then became a new cause (chain causation), or for explaining how a single cause produced multiple effects (like how WWI simultaneously produced Bolshevik revolution, Versailles resentment, and the self-determination principle — three effects pointing in different directions).

## Continuity and Change

**Continuity:** Nationalism and imperial rivalry drove conflicts from the 19th century through the 20th. The same forces that produced the Crimean War, the Sino-Japanese War, and the Scramble for Africa continued to operate in both world wars. Competition over resources and markets persisted as a structural feature of the capitalist world economy.

**Change:** The *scale* of conflict changed fundamentally. Pre-20th-century wars killed soldiers; 20th-century total wars killed civilians at mass scale (WWI: ~15M civilian deaths; WWII: ~50M civilian deaths). The atomic bomb made the annihilation of entire cities possible with a single weapon. Post-WWII, nuclear deterrence changed the logic of great-power conflict — direct war between nuclear powers became too costly, producing the Cold War's indirect conflicts instead.

**Change in world order:** Before 1914, Europe dominated the global political order. By 1945, two non-European powers (U.S. and USSR) were the new poles of global power. This represents one of the most profound changes in world political history.

## Historiographical Perspectives

Historians debate which causes were most significant in each war:

For **WWI**: The "Fischer Controversy" (1960s) argued Germany deliberately sought war for imperial aims (economic/political cause dominant). Others emphasize the alliance system's mechanical escalation (structural cause dominant) — illustrated by the Kaiser-Tsar correspondence, where both monarchs tried and failed to stop mobilization. A third view emphasizes Austro-Hungarian aggression against Serbia as the regional catalyst that the great powers failed to contain.

For **WWII**: Traditional view: Hitler's unique ideology and will drove the war (political/personality cause). Structuralist view: systemic failures — Versailles, appeasement, Great Depression — made WWII nearly inevitable regardless of who led Germany (economic and structural causes dominant). The AMSCO text leans toward structural explanation while acknowledging Hitler's role.

> [!tip]
> Using historiographical debate in an LEQ is one of the clearest paths to the **complexity point**. Acknowledge that historians disagree about which cause was most significant, take a defensible position, and use evidence to support it.
