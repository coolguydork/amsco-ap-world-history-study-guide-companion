# People, States & Movements

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 7.9 is a causation synthesis chapter — the narrative names figures primarily as examples of forces (nationalism, economic desperation, self-determination). On the AP, these figures are evidence for arguments about causes and effects, not ends in themselves.

## Key Figures

**Albert Einstein (1879–1955)**
German-American physicist, quoted in AMSCO's epigraph: *"As long as there are sovereign nations possessing great power, war is inevitable."* Cited as evidence of the intellectual climate after WWII — the recognition that the scale of destruction (including the atomic bomb, to which Einstein contributed indirectly) demanded a new approach to international relations.

**Woodrow Wilson (1856–1924)**
U.S. President who championed **self-determination** and the League of Nations after WWI. His Fourteen Points framed the 1919 Paris Peace Conference. Wilson's principle that peoples should choose their own government inspired anti-colonial independence movements worldwide, even though it was rarely applied to non-European peoples at Versailles. A key figure in understanding both the effects of WWI and the causes of WWII (the League's failure).

**Adolf Hitler (1889–1945)**
Leader of [[Concept Notes/Fascism\|Nazi Germany]]. AMSCO identifies him as a **populist leader** who exploited economic desperation during the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]] to rise to power and then pursued expansionist war, exploiting Western appeasement. His policies caused WWII in Europe and produced the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]].

**Kaiser Wilhelm II (1859–1941) and Tsar Nicholas II (1868–1918)**
Central figures in the DBQ documents (pp. 540–543). Their personal correspondence during the July Crisis (July 1914) illustrates how dynastic relationships, miscommunication, and mobilization timetables made a regional conflict (Austria-Hungary vs. Serbia) uncontrollable. Both were swept up by forces larger than their personal friendship. Key evidence for the argument that the alliance system and militarism, not individual decisions, caused WWI to escalate.

## Key States

**United States**
Emerged from both world wars as the dominant Western power, relatively undamaged compared to European belligerents. Its oil embargo against Japan triggered Pearl Harbor; its industrial and military capacity determined the outcome of WWII. By 1945, the U.S. was the world's leading economic and military superpower.

**Soviet Union (U.S.S.R.)**
Emerged as the second superpower by 1945, having absorbed enormous casualties in WWII (20 million civilian deaths). Set up as the ideological and geopolitical rival of the U.S., establishing the bipolar Cold War world (Unit 8).

**Ottoman Empire → Turkish Republic**
The Ottoman Empire collapsed as a result of WWI. Its replacement, the modern Turkish Republic under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, was a Western-style secular democracy — a major political effect of global conflict.

**Russian Empire → Soviet Union**
WWI combined with internal decay to bring down the Romanov dynasty (Russian Revolution, 1917). The Communist Party established a totalitarian state — the polar opposite of the Ottoman Empire's successor in terms of political outcome, illustrating how global conflict produced radically different regime changes.

**British India → India + Pakistan (1947)**
The largest independence movement in human terms: hundreds of millions of people. India achieved independence through passive resistance and civil disobedience (led by the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]] and Mahatma Gandhi). The 1947 partition into Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan produced mass violence and displacement, and tensions between the two states have produced multiple wars since independence.

## Key Movements

**Anti-colonial independence movements**
Accelerated by both world wars. Colonial soldiers who fought for European powers returned home with expectations of self-rule. The economic weakness of post-WWII colonial powers made maintaining empires unsustainable. The pattern: small settler populations → relatively peaceful independence; large settler populations → violent struggle (e.g., Algeria, Kenya, Vietnam).

**[[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] as a cross-cutting force**
Appeared throughout the 20th century as both a *cause* (Serbian nationalism → WWI; German/Italian ultranationalism → WWII) and a *tool of liberation* (Indian independence movement; African anti-colonial movements). A rare historical force that simultaneously drove conflict and drove emancipation.

> [!tip]
> The Kaiser-Tsar correspondence (DBQ documents) is excellent HAPP evidence: **Purpose** — both leaders were trying to avoid war but constrained by alliance commitments. **Point of View** — monarchs who shared family ties but represented states with competing interests. **Historical Situation** — mobilization timetables meant military logic was overtaking diplomatic communication by late July 1914.
