# Chapter 7: Key Terms — The Silk Roads

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently uses Silk Road trade as a stimulus context. Know not just *what* moved along the routes, but *how* and *why* — the mechanisms of diffusion matter as much as the goods themselves.

---

## Vocabulary

**Silk Roads** — The network of overland and maritime trade routes connecting East Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East, East Africa, and Europe from antiquity through the early modern period. (Significance: the primary mechanism for long-distance cultural and commercial exchange in Eurasia; named for the Chinese silk that was its most prized commodity, though it carried far more than silk.)

**Pax Mongolica** — ("Mongol Peace") The period of relative stability across Eurasia under Mongol rule (c. 1250s–1340s) that enabled safe travel and commerce along the Silk Roads. (Significance: allowed merchants like Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta to travel vast distances; facilitated the spread of the [[02-Key-People-and-Groups|Black Death]] alongside goods.)

**Caravanserai** — Roadside inns built at regular intervals along Silk Road routes to provide shelter, food, water, and trading space for merchants and their animals. (Significance: critical infrastructure that made multi-month overland journeys survivable; often built by rulers seeking tax revenue from trade.)

**Bubonic plague** — A bacterial disease (*Yersinia pestis*) transmitted by fleas on rodents that devastated Eurasian populations in the mid-14th century, killing an estimated one-third of Europe and reducing populations across the Islamic world and China. (Significance: traveled the Silk Roads from Central Asia westward; caused the [[06-Connections|Black Death]]; destabilized the Yuan Dynasty, Ilkhanate, and European feudalism.)

**Overland Silk Road** — The land-based trade network running from China through Central Asia (the Tarim Basin, Transoxiana) to Persia and the Mediterranean. (Significance: the route along which silk, paper, gunpowder, and Buddhism moved westward; dependent on political stability — thrived under Mongol rule, declined after Yuan collapse.)

**Maritime Silk Road** — The sea-based complement to the overland routes, running from Chinese ports through Southeast Asia, across the Indian Ocean to East Africa and the Persian Gulf. (Significance: often more efficient for bulk goods; became increasingly dominant as overland routes destabilized after the 14th century.)

**Sogdians** — Central Asian merchants (based in modern Uzbekistan) who dominated Silk Road trade from antiquity through the early Islamic period. (Significance: archetypal Silk Road middlemen; spread Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism, and later Islam along the routes; exemplify the role of merchant diasporas.)

**Diasporic communities** — Permanent settlements of merchants from one culture living in a foreign city to facilitate trade. (Significance: Chinese communities in Southeast Asian ports, Muslim communities in East African cities — these communities enabled long-distance trust and credit across cultural boundaries.)

**Monsoon winds** — Seasonal wind patterns of the Indian Ocean that reverse direction between summer and winter, making predictable ocean voyages possible. (Significance: the natural engine of Indian Ocean trade; sailors could ride winds east in summer, west in winter — created the calendar of maritime Silk Road commerce.)

**Bill of exchange** — A written financial document that allowed merchants to conduct long-distance trade without transporting large amounts of coin. (Significance: a key commercial innovation that reduced the risk and cost of Silk Road trade; an early form of credit.)

**Paper money** — Currency in paper form, first developed in Song Dynasty China and adopted by the Mongols. (Significance: facilitated large-scale trade by replacing heavy metal coins; the Mongols attempted to impose it across their empire — its devaluation contributed to Yuan economic collapse.)

**Tributary goods** — Luxury items exchanged between rulers as part of diplomatic tribute relationships. (Significance: luxury goods like silk, porcelain, and spices were not just commercial items — they carried political meaning and reinforced hierarchies between states.)

**Epidemic disease diffusion** — The spread of infectious diseases through contact along trade routes. (Significance: the Silk Roads were as effective at spreading plague as they were at spreading silk — the Black Death is the defining example of epidemic disease diffusion in this period.)

**Nestorian Christianity** — A branch of Christianity, declared heretical by the western Church in 431 CE, that survived and spread eastward along the Silk Roads into Central Asia, China, and India. (Significance: exemplifies the role of the Silk Roads in religious diffusion beyond Islam and Buddhism; Nestorian communities existed in Mongol courts.)

**Sufism** — The mystical branch of Islam, practiced by wandering holy men (Sufis) who emphasized personal experience of God. (Significance: Sufi missionaries were the primary agents of Islam's spread into Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia along trade routes — their flexible, syncretic approach won more converts than conquest.)

**Syncretism** — The blending of elements from different religious or cultural traditions into a new, hybrid form. (Significance: a near-universal result of Silk Road contact — Buddhism blended with local traditions in Southeast Asia, Islam incorporated Sufi and local practices in West Africa, Christianity mixed with indigenous beliefs in Ethiopia.)
