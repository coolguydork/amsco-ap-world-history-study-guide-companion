# Key Terms & Concepts

*Topic 8.9 is a causation/synthesis chapter. AMSCO provides no KEY TERMS BY THEME sidebar. Vocabulary is derived from the narrative and the AP skill framework for Causation and Argumentation.*

---

**Cold War**
The geopolitical, ideological, and economic rivalry between the United States (and its allies) and the Soviet Union (and its allies) from approximately 1947 to 1991. Characterized by proxy wars, arms races, and ideological competition rather than direct military conflict between the superpowers. The defining framework of global politics for the second half of the 20th century.

**containment**
The U.S. foreign policy doctrine established after WWII that sought to prevent the spread of communism beyond the areas where it already existed. Articulated by diplomat George Kennan and operationalized through military interventions (Korea, Vietnam), economic aid (Marshall Plan), and military alliances ([[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]]). Containment drove nearly every major U.S. foreign policy decision during the Cold War.

**First World / Second World / Third World**
The Cold War's tripartite division of global political alignments. The First World was the U.S. and its democratic, free-market allies. The Second World was the Soviet Union, Eastern Europe, and other communist states. The Third World (also "non-aligned") consisted of mostly postcolonial states in Asia, Africa, Latin America, and Oceania that tried to avoid alignment with either superpower. The term "Third World" today often connotes economic underdevelopment, but its original meaning was political non-alignment.

**Iron Curtain**
The metaphorical (and partly physical) boundary dividing democratic Western Europe from communist Eastern Europe during the Cold War. Coined by Winston Churchill in 1946. Represented ideologically by contrasting systems of government and economically by the divide between free-market and command economies. The most literal expression was the Berlin Wall (1961–1989).

**proxy war**
A conflict in which two opposing powers support rival sides rather than fighting each other directly. The Cold War produced dozens of proxy wars across the Third World, where the U.S. and Soviet Union backed opposing factions to advance their ideological and strategic interests without risking direct nuclear confrontation. Key examples: Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan, Nicaragua.

**satellite state**
A country that is formally independent but in practice heavily controlled by a more powerful state. During the Cold War, the Eastern European nations — Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Bulgaria, East Germany — were Soviet satellite states. The USSR maintained control through military presence, economic integration (COMECON), and ideological enforcement.

**welfare state**
A system of government in which the state provides comprehensive social services and protections — including public healthcare, housing, unemployment insurance, and pensions — as a right of citizenship. Western European governments built welfare states after WWII, partly to promote social stability and partly to reduce the appeal of communism. Represents a middle path between laissez-faire capitalism and Soviet-style command economy.

**Non-Aligned Movement**
A coalition of Third World states that refused alignment with either the U.S. or Soviet blocs. Formally launched at the Bandung Conference (1955), led by figures such as Jawaharlal Nehru (India), Gamal Abdel Nasser (Egypt), Sukarno (Indonesia), Kwame Nkrumah (Ghana), and Josip Broz Tito (Yugoslavia). The movement sought to preserve newly won sovereignty, avoid superpower domination, and address economic underdevelopment on their own terms.

**Bandung Conference (1955)**
The first large-scale gathering of Asian and African nations, held in Bandung, Indonesia, hosted by President Sukarno. 29 countries participated. The conference articulated opposition to colonialism and racial discrimination and laid the groundwork for the Non-Aligned Movement. Key documents include Nehru's speech criticizing superpower militarism.

**nuclear arms race**
The competition between the United States and the Soviet Union to develop and stockpile nuclear weapons after WWII. The U.S. used atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945; the Soviet Union tested its first nuclear weapon in 1949. The race escalated into hydrogen bombs, intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), and submarine-launched weapons. Created the doctrine of mutually assured destruction (MAD), which paradoxically prevented direct superpower conflict.

**brinkmanship**
The Cold War strategy of pushing dangerous situations to the edge ("brink") of conflict to force an adversary to back down. Associated with Secretary of State John Foster Dulles and the Eisenhower administration. The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) is the most dramatic example of brinkmanship — the U.S. and USSR came closest to nuclear war before the Soviets agreed to remove missiles from Cuba.

**decolonization**
The process by which European colonial empires dissolved and their subject peoples gained political independence, primarily between 1945 and the 1970s. The number of independent states more than doubled between 1945 and 2000 (from ~50 to ~100+). Decolonization intersected with the Cold War as both superpowers competed to influence newly independent states. Not always peaceful: violent independence struggles occurred in Vietnam, Algeria, Kenya, Angola, Mozambique, and elsewhere.

**neo-colonialism**
The continuation of economic dependency and exploitation of developing nations by former colonial powers (or new superpowers) after formal political independence. Former colonies often remained dependent on exporting raw materials and importing manufactured goods, maintaining unequal trade relationships. The term was coined by Kwame Nkrumah. Key AP argument: formal decolonization did not end economic subordination.

**military-industrial complex**
The interconnected relationship between a nation's military establishment and its defense industry. In the Soviet Union, the military-industrial complex employed approximately 20% of workers, making the post-Cold War economic transition especially painful. In the United States, President Eisenhower warned against the undue influence of this complex in his 1961 farewell address.

**Causation (AP skill)**
The AP historical reasoning skill of explaining why events occurred (causes) and what resulted from them (effects). For Topic 8.9, causation analysis means: (1) identifying the causes of the Cold War (WWII's outcome, ideological rivalry, Yalta/Potsdam disagreements); (2) analyzing the effects across political, economic, social, and cultural dimensions; (3) distinguishing between short-term and long-term effects; (4) comparing effects across regions (Eastern vs. Western Hemisphere).

**Argumentation (AP skill)**
The AP skill of constructing a historically defensible thesis with a line of reasoning, supporting it with evidence, and qualifying or modifying the argument using nuanced evidence. For 8.9, strong argumentation means: a thesis that addresses both similarity and difference; body paragraphs that use specific regional examples as evidence; and a conclusion that qualifies the overall pattern (e.g., "similar in kind but different in intensity").

> [!exam-tip]
> **The vocabulary of causation matters on the AP exam.** Use: "caused," "accelerated," "facilitated," "resulted in," "contributed to," "was a consequence of," "deepened," "intensified." Avoid vague verbs like "affected," "influenced," or "led to" without specificity.
