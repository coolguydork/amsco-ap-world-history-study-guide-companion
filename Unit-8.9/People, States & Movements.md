## Leaders of the Non-Aligned Movement

**Jawaharlal Nehru** (India, 1889–1964)
India's first Prime Minister and a founding voice of the Non-Aligned Movement. At the 1955 Bandung Conference, Nehru argued that superpower militarism — not communist or capitalist ideology — posed the greatest danger to humanity. His speech warned that "greatness sometimes brings false values" and that if the world divided between two blocs, "the inevitable result would be war." Nehru's insistence that India maintain strategic autonomy shaped postcolonial foreign policy across Asia and Africa.

**Sukarno** (Indonesia, 1901–1970)
President of Indonesia and host of the 1955 Bandung Conference. Sukarno led Indonesia's independence struggle against the Dutch and articulated the Bandung spirit: collective opposition to colonialism and racial discrimination, and the subordination of all policy to human well-being. His opening address defined the Non-Aligned Movement's moral framework. Indonesia later experienced a U.S.-backed anti-communist crackdown (1965) that overthrew Sukarno.

**Kwame Nkrumah** (Ghana, 1909–1972)
Ghana's first president and the African architect of pan-Africanism and non-alignment. Nkrumah coined the term "neo-colonialism" to describe how formal independence could mask continued economic subordination. He was present at the 1960 UN meeting of non-aligned leaders (photographed alongside Nehru, Nasser, Tito, and Sukarno). A key figure for AP essays on African decolonization and Cold War economic dependency.

**Gamal Abdel Nasser** (Egypt, 1918–1970)
President of Egypt, Arab nationalist, and non-aligned leader. Nasser's nationalization of the Suez Canal (1956) and refusal to align with either superpower made him a symbol of Third World sovereignty. His presence at the 1960 non-aligned meeting illustrated the political breadth of the movement — spanning Asia, Africa, and the Middle East.

**Josip Broz Tito** (Yugoslavia, 1892–1980)
President of Yugoslavia and the only Eastern European communist leader to successfully resist Soviet domination. Tito's independent path made Yugoslavia a bridge between the Cold War blocs. His death in 1980 eventually allowed the suppressed ethnic tensions in Yugoslavia to re-emerge — leading to the wars of Yugoslav succession in the 1990s under Slobodan Milošević's ultra-nationalism.

**Slobodan Milošević** (Serbia, 1941–2006)
Serbian politician and president who exploited ethnic nationalism after the Cold War's end to consolidate power. His pitting of ethnic groups against one another in the post-Tito power vacuum led to wars that killed tens of thousands and created hundreds of thousands of refugees in Bosnia, Croatia, and Kosovo. A key example on the AP exam of **Cold War suppression of ethnic conflict** and the consequences of superpower withdrawal.

## Key States & Their Cold War Roles

**Soviet Union (USSR)**
The communist superpower anchoring the Second World. The USSR controlled Eastern Europe through satellite states, competed globally through proxy wars and economic aid, and developed nuclear weapons to match the U.S. arsenal. The Soviet military-industrial complex consumed ~20% of its workforce, making post-Cold War economic transition especially painful. Collapsed in 1991.

**United States**
The democratic capitalist superpower anchoring the First World. U.S. containment policy drove military interventions in Korea, Vietnam, and support for anti-communist governments across Latin America, Asia, and Africa — often regardless of those governments' democratic credentials. The Marshall Plan rebuilt Western Europe. U.S. backing of repressive anti-communist regimes fueled anti-Americanism across the Third World.

**China**
Became a communist state in 1949, joining the Second World. Later had a falling-out with the Soviet Union (Sino-Soviet Split, 1960s) and began seeking better relations with the United States (Nixon's visit, 1972). China's gradual transition to a free-market economy after Mao's death made it a global economic powerhouse by the early 21st century — a model distinct from both Soviet and Western paths.

**Angola**
A key Cold War proxy war battleground in Africa. Angola's independence war from Portugal (1975) immediately became a superpower contest: Soviet-backed forces (with Cuban troops) vs. U.S.-backed UNITA rebels. The Soviet-supported MPLA government won control and built a government-controlled oil sector that accounts for ~90% of government revenue — a model of state capitalism in a postcolonial state.

## Key Movements

**Non-Aligned Movement**
The coalition of Third World states that refused alignment with either Cold War bloc. Emerging from the 1955 Bandung Conference, it eventually grew to include most newly independent nations. Its central demands: respect for sovereignty, opposition to colonialism and racism, peaceful coexistence, and economic development on terms not dictated by the superpowers.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Non-Aligned Movement is strong evidence for DBQ prompts about decolonization's impact on Cold War politics. Use it to argue that newly independent states were not passive recipients of Cold War pressures — they actively sought to reshape the geopolitical order.

**Warsaw Pact**
Soviet military alliance formed in 1955 in response to NATO. Included the USSR and its Eastern European satellite states. Ensured Soviet military dominance over Eastern Europe and provided the legal framework for Soviet intervention (e.g., Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968). Dissolved in 1991.

**[[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]] (North Atlantic Treaty Organization)**
U.S.-led military alliance formed in 1949 binding Western democracies to mutual defense. NATO's existence guaranteed U.S. commitment to Western European security and deterred Soviet expansion westward. Still in existence today.
