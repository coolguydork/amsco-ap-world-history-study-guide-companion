# People, States & Movements

## Key Individuals

**Bartolomé de las Casas (1484–1566)**
Dominican friar and Spanish colonist turned critic. His 1552 work *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* documented Spanish atrocities against indigenous peoples in graphic detail — the foundational text of the Black Legend. Las Casas appears in Topic 4.8's Historical Perspectives section as the origin point of the debate over how to judge Spanish colonialism. His writings are DBQ gold — a passionate insider critique that invites questions about authorship, purpose, and audience. (AMSCO p. 265)

**Alfred Crosby (1931–2018)**
Modern historian who identified and named the Columbian Exchange. His work reframed the significance of 1492 from political (Columbus finding America) to biological (the exchange of species between hemispheres). The epigraph for Topic 4.8 ("The seams of [the world] were closing, drawn together by the sailmaker's needle") is from Crosby's 1986 work *Ecological Imperialism*. (AMSCO p. 261)

**Julián Juderías (1877–1918)**
Spanish historian who coined the term "Black Legend" in 1914. He argued that anti-Spanish sentiment among Protestant Europeans distorted the historical record, and that Spanish colonial rule was better than critics claimed. His work represents nationalist historical revisionism. (AMSCO p. 265)

**Lewis Hanke (1905–1993)**
American historian who tried to find a middle ground between the Black and White Legends. Hanke emphasized Las Casas as one of many reformers, arguing the Spanish empire made a genuine attempt to apply Christian ethics. His position is useful on DBQs for showing historical complexity — he neither defends nor condemns Spanish colonialism wholesale. (AMSCO p. 265)

**Henry Morgan (c. 1635–1688)**
English privateer (a pirate operating with government sanction) who raided Spanish ports in the Caribbean in the 1660s–1670s. The letter from Don Alonzo del Campo y Espinosa to Morgan (1669) in Topic 4.8's DBQ documents illustrates the Spanish-English imperial rivalry and the use of piracy as a tool of commercial warfare. (AMSCO p. 269)

**Seydi-Ali (c. 1498–1563)**
Ottoman admiral who commanded the fleet defeated by the Portuguese in the Gulf of Oman (1554–1558). This naval conflict illustrates the competition between the established Ottoman maritime power and the emerging Portuguese maritime empire for control of Indian Ocean trade routes. (AMSCO p. 271)

**Suleiman the Magnificent (r. 1520–1566) and Shah Tahmasp I (r. 1524–1576)**
Featured in the Busbecq letters (Topic 4.8's DBQ Document 7). The Ottoman-Safavid rivalry — with Suleiman pressing Shah Tahmasp for compliance with their treaty — illustrates intra-Muslim geopolitical competition in this period. (AMSCO p. 272)

## Key States & Empires

**Dutch Republic / Netherlands**
The Netherlands was the dominant global commercial power of the 17th century. The Dutch East India Company (VOC, founded 1602) was the world's first multinational [[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|joint-stock company]], with the power to wage war, negotiate treaties, and govern territory. Dutch commercial innovation — from the VOC to the Amsterdam stock exchange — represents the emergence of capitalist financial structures. The Netherlands was also notably tolerant of religious minorities including Jews.

**Spanish Empire (Americas)**
Created the largest European colonial empire in this period. Spain's extraction of American silver (Potosí, Zacatecas) drove global trade and the Price Revolution. Spain's [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilist]] policies, encomienda system, and casta hierarchy are the defining example of colonial economic and social structures. The letter from Don Alonzo to Morgan and the debate over the Black Legend both reflect the contested legacy of Spanish imperial power.

**Ottoman Empire**
The Ottoman Empire's naval rivalry with Portugal in the Indian Ocean (illustrated by the Seydi-Ali document) shows that European maritime expansion was contested — not a simple takeover. The Ottoman-Safavid rivalry (Busbecq letters) shows intra-Muslim great-power competition operating simultaneously.

**Portuguese Empire**
The first major European maritime empire, establishing footholds in Africa, Asia, and Brazil. Portuguese dominance of the Indian Ocean trade (c. 1500–1600) was the first chapter of European commercial dominance. Eventually displaced by the Dutch and English. (AMSCO p. 262)

## Key Movements

**German Peasants' War / Great Peasants' Revolt (1524–1525)**
A mass uprising of German peasants protesting serfdom and feudal obligations, articulated in the Twelve Articles of the Swabian Peasants. The Third Article demanded freedom from serfdom on biblical grounds; others sought relief from unjust taxation and arbitrary law. The revolt was crushed. Its relevance to Topic 4.8: economic disruptions from the early capitalist transition and the [[Concept Notes/Price Revolution|Price Revolution]] increased pressure on peasants, contributing to social unrest. (AMSCO p. 270)

**Salt Riot in Moscow (1648)**
Russian artisans and serfs rioted against Czar Alexei I's universal salt tax. Like the German Peasants' War, this illustrates how economic policy — especially taxation of necessities — could generate social upheaval among lower-class groups who bore the heaviest burdens of emerging commercial economies. (AMSCO p. 272)

**Thirty Years War (1618–1648)**
The last major European religious war, fought between Protestant and Catholic states. Friedrich Schiller's analysis (Topic 4.8 DBQ Document 5) argues that religion was the *pretext* but private advantage and state interests were the real motives — princes used religious enthusiasm to recruit armies while pursuing power and territory. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) established the modern state system of sovereign, territorially defined nation-states. (AMSCO p. 271)

> [!tip]
> Topic 4.8's DBQ documents span an impressive geographic range: Caribbean piracy (Morgan/Don Alonzo), Indian Ocean naval conflict (Seydi-Ali), German peasant revolt, the Thirty Years War, a Moscow salt riot, and Ottoman-Safavid diplomacy. This is deliberate — the AP is testing your ability to synthesize diverse evidence into a single argument about change and continuity across the period.

> [!exam-tip]
> For DBQ thesis practice: the strongest argument about 1450–1750 economic/social change identifies the *mechanism* of change (Atlantic System and the silver economy) and the *nature* of continuity (coercive labor intensifies in form but not logic). Avoid listing changes — argue for their *relative significance*.
