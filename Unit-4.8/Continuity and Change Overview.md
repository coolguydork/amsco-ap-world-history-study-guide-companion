# Topic 4.8 – Continuity and Change from c. 1450 to c. 1750

**Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections | Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)**

## Learning Objectives

- Synthesize the major developments of Unit 4 into a coherent understanding of change and continuity.
- Explain how economic developments from 1450 to 1750 affected social structures over time.
- Connect specific topics from Unit 4 to the broader arc of AP World History.
- Practice the skills of CCOT analysis and synthesis for LEQ and DBQ writing.

## The Big Picture: The World of 1750

By 1750, the world looked fundamentally different from the world of 1450. As historian Alfred Crosby described, "The seams of the world were closing, drawn together by the sailmaker's needle." What did that connected world look like?

- Most of the world was integrated within a system of **economic, political, and cultural connections**
- The Western Hemisphere had been fully incorporated into the global trading network
- Maritime empires based in Western Europe dominated trade across the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific Oceans
- Religions continued to spread and transform through syncretic contact
- Coercive labor systems had evolved — some persisted from earlier periods, others were newly created
- New social structures based on race and ethnicity had emerged in the colonial Americas

This is the synthesis task of Topic 4.8: understanding how all the individual developments of the unit fit together into a coherent picture of transformation.

## What Changed

### Transoceanic Travel and Trade

The most significant change of the period was the **integration of the Western Hemisphere into the global trading network**. Before 1450, Afro-Eurasia and the Americas had been essentially isolated from each other. By 1750, they were deeply and permanently connected.

This happened because Western European states — wanting direct sea routes to Asia — borrowed and synthesized navigational technologies from Chinese, Islamic, and classical traditions: astronomical charts, astrolabe, compass, lateen sail, caravel, carrack, fluyt.

The result was the [[Unit-4.3/Columbian Exchange Overview|Columbian Exchange]]: a biological exchange of crops, animals, people, and diseases. The Columbian Exchange had wide-ranging, often unintended effects on all regions it touched.

### The Atlantic System

The Columbian Exchange produced a **transoceanic trading network called the Atlantic System**, connecting Western Europe, West Africa, and the Americas in a circuit of goods and forced labor. The Atlantic System:
- Moved millions of Africans to the Americas through the transatlantic slave trade
- Produced cash crops (sugar, tobacco, cotton) for European markets
- Circulated American silver into the global economy
- Enabled European merchants to profit enormously as middlemen between regions

### Empires and Their Methods

**Maritime trading empires** emerged, led by Portugal (trading post empire) and followed by the Dutch and English (joint-stock company empires). Europeans established:
- Trading ports along the coasts of Africa and the Indian Ocean
- Colonial territorial empires in the Americas
- Commercial relationships with (and eventually political control over) existing Asian states

This brought Europeans into contact — and often conflict — with existing merchant networks. In many cases, Europeans **dominated global trade at the expense of Arab, Indian, and Chinese merchants** who had previously controlled key routes. But this domination was never complete: Asian merchants continued operating, and the Indian Ocean trade network largely absorbed European presence while continuing in its familiar patterns.

### Economic Systems: Silver and Capitalism

Spain's discovery of silver deposits in the Americas was transformative:
- American silver tripled the global money supply in the 16th century (some estimates)
- Silver flowed to China (via Manila galleons) where it became the primary currency
- Silver also caused severe inflation in Spain (the Price Revolution), ultimately weakening the Spanish empire

**Mercantilism eventually gave way to capitalism** as the dominant European economic system. Joint-stock companies (the key institutional innovation of the period) allowed investors to share risks and rewards, pooling capital for ventures that no individual or state could finance alone. This capitalist model of shared investment would become the foundation of the Industrial Revolution.

**Colonial wealth's downstream effects**: The flow of wealth into Europe from colonies:
- Expanded the middle class
- Provided capital for the Industrial Revolution
- Funded artistic patronage that produced the works of the Dutch Golden Age (Rembrandt's era)

### New Social Structures

The encounter of European, African, and indigenous American populations created **new social systems based on racial or ethnic identity** — the casta system and other race-based hierarchies that were genuinely new to human history. These rigid, hierarchical societies concentrated wealth and political power among white Europeans and their descendants. A new multiracial subculture appeared; societal conflicts would eventually fuel revolutions.

## What Continued

### Traditional Forced Labor Systems

Serfdom continued in areas of Afro-Eurasia — notably in Russia, where it was actually strengthened during this period (Law of 1649). Other traditional coerced labor systems continued in parts of Asia and Africa.

New coercive labor systems in the Americas complemented existing ones rather than replacing the general pattern of forced labor. Chattel slavery, encomienda, mit'a, hacienda, and indentured servitude all served the same fundamental purpose as serfdom: extracting labor from the powerless for the benefit of the powerful.

### Regional Markets

Traditional regional markets in Europe, Africa, and Asia continued to function alongside the new global trade. Peasant and artisan production — wool from Western Europe, cotton from India, silk from China — continued to circulate through established port city networks. The global trade did not replace regional trade; it layered on top of it, creating a more complex, multi-tiered world economy.

### Religions

Religions continued to spread through trade networks, missionary activity, and conquest — as they had in earlier periods. But the *scale* of religious spread increased dramatically with maritime empires. Christianity spread to the Americas, sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Asia. Islam continued to spread through the Indian Ocean trade network and into sub-Saharan Africa.

What was genuinely new was **syncretism** — the blending of religious traditions that occurred wherever different belief systems came into sustained contact through trade, conquest, and forced migration.

### Cultural Funding

As merchants and governments grew wealthy from trade, funding for the arts increased. Some rulers used art and architecture to legitimize their power. Other art (like Dutch Golden Age paintings) symbolized the growing importance of global trade. This pattern — elites funding culture to display power and wealth — continued from earlier periods, though the sources of wealth (colonial trade rather than agrarian tribute) were new.

## Practice Thesis Statements

**Prompt**: "Evaluate the extent to which the period 1450–1750 represented a turning point in global economic integration."

**Strong thesis**: "The period 1450–1750 represented a decisive turning point in global economic integration, as the incorporation of the Western Hemisphere into the global trading network created the first truly worldwide economic system — though traditional regional markets and labor systems continued alongside the new global connections, demonstrating that the transformation was profound but not total."

**Another strong thesis**: "While the period 1450–1750 saw the dramatic expansion of European maritime empires that integrated the Americas into global trade, the most transformative economic change was the acceleration of the transatlantic slave trade, which permanently altered the demographics of three continents and created new coercive labor systems that sustained colonial wealth at the cost of millions of lives."
