## DBQ Practice Documents

*Source: AMSCO AP World History, printed pp. 269–272*

### Document 2 — Letter from Don Alonzo del Campo y Espinosa to Henry Morgan, 1669

> "Having… received news that you have had the audacity [boldness] to commit hostilities in the territories and cities owing obedience to His Catholic Majesty, the king of Spain my master, I have come to this place… and have built up again that fortress which you took from a set of faint-hearts… to prevent your escape from this lake and do you all the injury my duty requires."
>
> "Nevertheless, if you will surrender with humility all which you have taken, including all the slaves and other prisoners, I will have the clemency to let you pass, that you may return to your own country."

**Point of View:** Don Alonzo is a Spanish military commander defending Spanish territorial claims. His tone is imperious and treats Morgan's actions as mere banditry against legitimate Spanish sovereignty. The letter reveals the Spanish perception that the Caribbean was rightfully theirs.

**Possible argument uses:**
- Evidence that European imperial competition involved privateers as commercial/military proxies
- Evidence of Spanish control mechanisms in the Americas
- Evidence of the Atlantic world as a contested space, not a Spanish monopoly

> [!tip] DBQ Connection
> Henry Morgan (an English privateer) raiding Spanish ports illustrates that by the mid-17th century, England actively challenged Spanish Atlantic dominance through state-sanctioned piracy. This connects to the broader shift from Spanish/Portuguese dominance → Dutch/English commercial challenge.

### Document 3 — Twelve Articles of the Swabian Peasants, German Peasants' War, 1525

> "The Third Article: …It has been the custom… for men to hold us as their own property… it is consistent with the Scripture that we should be free and should wish to be so… We therefore take it for granted that you will release us from serfdom as true Christians…"
>
> "The Seventh Article: We will not hereafter allow ourselves to be further oppressed by our lords, but will let them demand only what is just and proper according to the word of the agreement between the lord and the peasant."

**Point of View:** German peasants, likely literate leaders (Sebastian Lotzer and Christoph Schappeler drafted this document). They frame their demands in explicitly *religious* language (freedom as a Christian right), which is strategic — appealing to the Reformation's emphasis on scripture over tradition.

**Possible argument uses:**
- Evidence that coercive labor (serfdom) generated resistance even in Europe
- Evidence that Reformation ideas had political/social consequences beyond theology
- Evidence of continuity in coercive labor — peasants were still fighting serfdom in 1525

> [!tip] DBQ Connection
> The Twelve Articles illustrate the social disruption that accompanied early capitalism and the Price Revolution in Europe. As landlords extracted more from peasants to meet rising costs, resistance intensified. The revolt was crushed — demonstrating that coercive systems were capable of self-defense.

### Document 4 — The Ambush of Seydi-Ali, 1554–1558 (illustration)

From the *Livro de Lisuarte de Abreau* (1558–1565). Shows the defeat of an Ottoman naval fleet by a Portuguese fleet in the Gulf of Oman.

**Point of View:** This is a Portuguese source celebrating a Portuguese naval victory — it frames the encounter as Portuguese triumph over Ottoman resistance.

**Possible argument uses:**
- Evidence that European maritime expansion was contested — the Ottoman Empire actively resisted Portuguese incursion into the Indian Ocean
- Evidence of Portuguese naval capability in the Indian Ocean by mid-16th century
- Evidence that the shift in Indian Ocean dominance from Ottoman/Arab to European was a military process, not just a commercial one

> [!tip] DBQ Connection
> The Seydi-Ali document directly illustrates the competition for Indian Ocean trade routes that [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview|Topic 4.4]] covers. European dominance of the Indian Ocean was not given — it was won in engagements like this one.

### Document 5 — Friedrich Schiller on the Thirty Years War, late 18th century

> "Religion alone could have rendered possible all that was accomplished, but it was far from being the sole motive of the war. Had not private advantages and state interests been closely connected with it… princes fought in self-defense or [to increase their own power], while religious enthusiasm recruited their armies… Of the multitude who flocked to their standards [agreed to fight for them], such as were not lured by the hope of plunder [stolen riches], imagined they were fighting for the truth, while in fact they were shedding their blood for the personal objects of their princes."

**Point of View:** Friedrich Schiller was a late 18th-century German historian writing after the Enlightenment. He is skeptical of religious motivations and emphasizes rational self-interest as the real driver of political behavior — a very Enlightenment perspective.

**Possible argument uses:**
- Evidence that state power (GOV) and economic interest drove European conflict, even when framed as religious
- Evidence of how historians' context shapes their interpretations (Enlightenment skepticism of religion)
- Evidence that the Thirty Years War marks the transition from religious to secular political logic in Europe

> [!tip] DBQ Connection
> Schiller's analysis supports the argument that by the early 17th century, European rulers used religion as a *tool* to mobilize subjects for wars that were fundamentally about territory and trade. This connects to the mercantilism → absolutism → state power thread of Unit 4.

### Document 6 — Salt Riot in Moscow, 1648 (painting by Ernest Lissner, 1938)

Depicts violent riots by artisans and serfs against Czar Alexei I's government over a universal salt tax.

**Point of View:** The painting was made in 1938 — nearly 300 years after the event. As a later artistic representation, it reflects the artist's interpretation as much as the historical reality. Soviet-era Russian art often emphasized popular resistance to aristocratic/tsarist oppression.

**Possible argument uses:**
- Evidence that coercive economic policies (universal taxation of necessities) generated popular resistance in Russia
- Evidence that Russian social hierarchies were contested from below
- Point of view: a 1938 Soviet painting may idealize or dramatize peasant resistance

> [!tip] DBQ Connection
> The Salt Riot illustrates [[Unit-4.6/Internal & External Challenges to State Power Overview|Topic 4.6]]'s theme of internal challenges to state power — economic policy as a trigger for rebellion. It also shows that coercive economic structures (taxing the poor) generated resistance that mirrors the German Peasants' War, though separated by over a century and a continent.

### Document 7 — Letters of Ogier Ghiseline de Busbecq, 1555–1562

The Flemish ambassador to the Ottoman court describes Suleiman the Magnificent's diplomatic and military pressure on Shah Tahmasp I of the Safavid Empire:

> "Solyman [i.e. Suleiman] at one time tried persuasion on the Shah, reminding him of the treaty… at another, endeavored to frighten him with menacing language… He had placed strong garrisons in all his towns on the Persian [i.e. Safavid] frontier, and filled Mesopotamia and the bank of the Euphrates with soldiers…"

**Point of View:** Busbecq was a Flemish (Habsburg) diplomat who spent years at the Ottoman court. His observations are detailed and generally accurate, though he writes from an outsider's perspective. He is interested in Ottoman power partly because the Habsburgs were themselves in conflict with the Ottomans.

**Possible argument uses:**
- Evidence of intra-Muslim imperial competition (Ottoman vs. Safavid) running parallel to the European-Ottoman competition
- Evidence of the Ottoman Empire's continued military and diplomatic reach in the mid-16th century
- Evidence that the period's conflicts were multi-polar, not simply "Europeans vs. everyone else"

## LEQ Practice Prompts

**Prompt 1 (CCOT):**
"Evaluate the extent to which economic developments changed social structures in the period from c. 1450 to c. 1750."

> [!tip] Brainstorm: Strong Evidence
> **Changes:** Atlantic slave trade + plantation economy → racial social hierarchies; Price Revolution → expanded middle class + peasant unrest; joint-stock companies → merchant capitalist class gains power relative to landed aristocracy
>
> **Continuities:** Serfdom persists in Russia and parts of Europe; coercive labor logic unchanged even as specific forms evolve; patriarchal structures unchanged across all regions
>
> **Thesis approach:** "Economic changes in this period significantly reshaped social structures by creating new racial hierarchies and expanding the commercial middle class; however, coercive labor systems that had defined social structures for centuries persisted — and in many regions intensified — demonstrating that economic change reinforced as much as it transformed the fundamental inequality of pre-industrial societies."

**Prompt 2 (Comparison):**
"Compare the economic systems of ONE European maritime empire and ONE gunpowder empire in the period 1450–1750."

> [!tip] Brainstorm: Spain vs. Ottoman Empire
> **Spain:** mercantilism; silver extraction; encomienda; Atlantic slave trade; casta system ties race to economic status
>
> **Ottoman Empire:** timar system (land grants for military service); jizya revenue from religious minorities; millet system allows protected trade communities; Mediterranean and Indian Ocean commerce; Janissary system provides military-economic elite
>
> **Key comparison:** Both empires used land grants and special status to reward military loyalty. Spain extracted wealth through racial coercion; the Ottomans used religious-status differentiation. Both benefited from silver (via trade), though Spain controlled the source.

## CCOT Reflection Questions

*From AMSCO p. 264:*

1. Identify four historical developments in the period from c. 1450–c. 1750. Rank them according to how significant they were in either (1) maintaining continuity or (2) bringing about change. Explain your rankings.

> [!tip] Model Rankings
> **Greatest change:** Integration of the Western Hemisphere into the global economy (Americas had been entirely separate; now linked by silver, slaves, and Columbian Exchange)
>
> **Second greatest change:** Rise of joint-stock companies and early capitalism (transforms how wealth is organized and who benefits from trade)
>
> **Greatest continuity:** Coercive labor systems (serfdom, slavery, tribute labor all persist; only the form shifts)
>
> **Second greatest continuity:** Patriarchal social structures (no fundamental change in gender hierarchy across any major region)

2. Explain how economic developments from c. 1450 to c. 1750 affected social structures over time.

> [!tip] Model Response
> The most significant social consequence of economic development in this period was the intensification and globalization of coercive labor. The Atlantic System required labor to grow cash crops (sugar, tobacco, cotton) and extract silver. When indigenous American populations collapsed from epidemic disease, the slave trade filled the gap — forcibly migrating millions of Africans to the Americas. This produced a racial social hierarchy (casta system) that tied economic status to ancestry in an entirely new way. At the same time, expanding merchant wealth in Europe created a growing commercial middle class whose economic interests would eventually challenge the landowning aristocracy. The Price Revolution disrupted traditional economic relationships and fueled peasant unrest (German Peasants' War, Salt Riot). By 1750, the economic transformations of the period had both reinforced existing hierarchies (coercive labor persisted) and created new ones (race-based colonial societies, commercial bourgeoisie).
