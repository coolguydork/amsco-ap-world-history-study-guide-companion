# Continuity & Change: c. 1450 to c. 1750

## Essential Question
How did economic developments from 1450 to 1750 affect social structures over time?

## Overview

Topic 4.8 is Unit 4's synthesis and CCOT topic. Rather than introducing new content, it asks students to step back and assess the full arc of change across the period, evaluating what was genuinely new versus what represented adaptation of earlier patterns.

The historian Alfred Crosby captured the era's defining feature: *"The seams of [the world] were closing, drawn together by the sailmaker's needle."* By 1750, virtually every region on earth had been drawn into an interconnected global economy — a transformation that was incomplete in 1450 and irreversible by 1750. (AMSCO p. 261)

## Key Continuities

**Coercive Labor Systems**
Forced labor did not begin in 1450 and did not end in 1750. Serfdom continued across Afro-Eurasia; the Russian serf system intensified. What changed was the form: the Atlantic world added chattel slavery, indentured servitude, [[Concept Notes/Encomienda|encomienda]], hacienda, and the adapted Inca mit'a to the menu of coerced labor. The total scale of coercive labor expanded dramatically, but the logic — elites extracting surplus from unfree laborers — was ancient. (AMSCO p. 264)

**[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|Mercantilism]] as State Economic Policy**
Throughout the period, European rulers used mercantilist policies to ensure that trade generated state revenue. Mercantilism — controlling exports and imports to accumulate gold and silver — was the dominant economic framework from 1450 through much of 1750. It represented continuity in *state intervention in the economy*, even as the specific mechanisms evolved. (AMSCO p. 263)

**Regional Trade Networks**
The Indian Ocean trade network, [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads\|Silk Roads]], and trans-Saharan trade all continued operating. European maritime expansion supplemented rather than replaced these networks in the short run, though Europeans increasingly dominated and redirected existing trade flows over time. (AMSCO p. 262)

**Patriarchal Social Structures**
In every region, men dominated political, religious, and economic life. Women could exercise influence through exceptional pathways (harem politics, religious life, elite marriage) but the fundamental patriarchal structure of society was not challenged in this period.

## Key Changes

**Integration of the Western Hemisphere**
Before 1492, the Americas were entirely outside the Afro-Eurasian world system. After 1492, they were integrated — producing the Columbian Exchange, the Atlantic System, and massive demographic, economic, and ecological transformations. This is the most fundamental change of the period. (AMSCO pp. 261–262)

**The Atlantic System**
A new transoceanic trading network emerged linking Western Europe, West Africa, and the Americas. Its defining features were the slave trade, cash-crop plantation agriculture (sugar, tobacco, cotton), and the circulation of silver. The Mediterranean-centered economy of 1450 gave way to an Atlantic-centered economy by 1750. (AMSCO p. 262)

**Mercantilism → [[Concept Notes/Capitalism\|Capitalism]]**
By the late 17th and early 18th centuries, [[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|joint-stock companies]] (Dutch [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] / VOC, British East India Company / EIC) transformed commerce. Investors could pool risk and profit across multiple ventures. This represents the early emergence of capitalism as a system — distinct from mercantilism in that profit-seeking drove economic decisions rather than state revenue targets. The middle class expanded. Capital for the coming Industrial Revolution accumulated. (AMSCO p. 263)

**New Social Structures Based on Race**
The colonial encounter created genuinely new social hierarchies based on racial ancestry — the casta system, chattel slavery, and the racially stratified plantation economy. These were not adaptations of earlier class or religious hierarchies; they were new. (AMSCO p. 264)

**European Dominance of Global Trade**
By 1750, European maritime empires had displaced Arab, Indian, and Chinese merchants from dominant positions in global trade. Portuguese traders established footholds in the Indian Ocean; the Dutch, then English, followed. This shift in who controlled trade routes and profits represented a structural change in the global economy. (AMSCO p. 262)

## Scope of Change: The Silver Economy

One of the clearest measures of global integration is silver. American silver (primarily from Potosí, Bolivia) flowed across the Pacific to China, where demand was insatiable. It flowed east to Europe, where it financed wars and commercial expansion. The amount of silver in the global economy roughly tripled in the 16th century. This flood of silver caused the **[[Concept Notes/Price Revolution|Price Revolution]]** — sustained inflation across Europe and parts of Asia — demonstrating that regional economies were now truly interdependent. (AMSCO p. 263)

## Demand for Labor Intensifies

As plantation agriculture and silver mining expanded, demand for labor intensified. This is a key causal link: the *economics* of the Atlantic System drove the *social structure* of the Americas. Plantation owners needed labor; indigenous populations were devastated by disease; enslaved Africans were imported. By 1750, millions of Africans had been forcibly transported to the Americas, African communities experienced gender imbalances (men were captured disproportionately), and the African population declined before recovering as new Columbian crops (manioc) spread. (AMSCO p. 264)

## Summary: What the AP Exam Expects

> [!exam-tip]
> For CCOT essays, the strongest arguments for Topic 4.8 are: (1) The integration of the Americas into the global economy is the period's defining change; (2) Coercive labor is the defining continuity — the *form* changed (chattel slavery replaces serfdom in the Atlantic world) but the *logic* did not; (3) Mercantilism represents continuity in state economic control even as capitalism began to emerge by the period's end.

> [!tip]
> The "sailmaker's needle" metaphor is worth remembering: global integration was a process, not an event. In 1450, the seams were loose. By 1750, they were pulled tight — though not without tearing some communities apart (African depopulation, indigenous demographic collapse, peasant revolts).
