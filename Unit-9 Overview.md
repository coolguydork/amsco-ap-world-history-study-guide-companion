**AP Period:** Period 4 — c. 1900 to the present
**Unit Theme:** *Globalization* — after 1900, accelerating technology collapsed distance, integrated economies, and homogenized culture at unprecedented speed — while simultaneously creating new inequalities, environmental crises, and resistance movements that defined the modern world.

---

## The Big Picture

Unit 9 is the culmination of AP World History — not a collection of miscellaneous modern topics but a single, interconnected argument about what happens when the world becomes fully integrated for the first time in human history. The organizing concept is **globalization**: the accelerating flows of goods, information, disease, culture, money, and people that bound the world together after 1900.

Technology drove everything. Three overlapping revolutions — in communication and transportation (radio → television → internet → social media), agriculture (the [[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]), and medicine (antibiotics, vaccines, birth control) — transformed human life within a single century. Global population grew from 1.6 billion in 1900 to 8 billion today. Average life expectancy doubled from ~35 to ~70 years. Physical distance, which had constrained human interaction for all of prior history, effectively collapsed.

But globalization created winners and losers — and understanding who won and who lost is the analytical engine of the unit. Economic globalization concentrated gains in developed nations and East Asia while perpetuating a core-periphery structure that colonial extraction had established. The same containerized shipping that made cheap goods available to American consumers enabled factories in Bangladesh to pay workers near-starvation wages. The same [[Concept Notes/Internet\|Internet]] that enabled pro-democracy movements in the Arab Spring enabled authoritarian surveillance in China. Technology was never neutral — it amplified the agency of those who already had power.

The global response to these inequalities took two forms. On one hand, international institutions — the [[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]], WTO, IMF, World Bank, and thousands of NGOs — built a framework for collective management of global problems. On the other, mass resistance movements — labor unions, environmental activists, nationalists, feminists, and anti-globalization protesters — challenged who set the rules and for whose benefit. The tension between these two responses — integration versus resistance — runs through every topic in Unit 9 and drives the highest-frequency AP exam questions.

For the AP exam, the central skill demand is **causation paired with comparison**: being able to explain what caused globalization's benefits and costs, and comparing how those effects played out differently across regions and social groups. Developed nations and East Asia versus the developing world is the master comparison frame. Within that, know the comparison between globalization's technological acceleration (change) and its perpetuation of inequality and exploitation (continuity).

## Topic-by-Topic Narrative

### 9.1 — Advances in Technology and Exchange After 1900

[[Unit-9.1/Advances in Technology & Exchange Overview|Topic 9.1]] is the unit's causal anchor. Every subsequent topic — disease spread, environmental damage, economic integration, cultural exchange, resistance — traces its roots to the technological revolutions introduced here.

Three overlapping transformations defined the era. In **communication and transportation**, radio and television created mass culture; the Internet (developed from ARPANET in the 1990s) rewired global commerce and politics; standardized shipping containers slashed freight costs after the 1950s, making intercontinental supply chains economically viable; jet aviation moved 2 million passengers daily. The political consequences were immediate: social media enabled the Arab Spring; mobile cameras created accountability movements against police brutality.

The **[[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]** — developed by Norman Borlaug and others — used crossbreeding and genetic engineering to produce high-yield grain varieties, dramatically expanding food supplies in Asia and Latin America. Its costs were real: small farmers lost land to large agribusinesses; chemical fertilizers degraded soil and water. Borlaug himself warned in 1970 that population growth could overwhelm the gains. This is the AP's model of technology with double-edged effects — a pattern you should apply to every other technology in Unit 9.

**Medical innovations** transformed demographics. Antibiotics (penicillin, 1928) reduced bacterial-disease mortality; vaccines eradicated smallpox (1980) and nearly eliminated polio; oral contraceptives (approved 1960) reduced fertility rates globally and gave women reproductive agency. The result was the **epidemiological transition**: as infectious disease declined in wealthy nations, chronic conditions (heart disease, Alzheimer's) became leading killers — a byproduct of longer lives that itself illustrates the paradox of medical progress.

> [!exam-tip]
> 9.1 is the causation foundation for all of Unit 9. When an AP prompt asks you to explain the causes of globalization, lead with technology — specifically, how communication, transportation, agricultural, and medical innovations together compressed distance and accelerated exchange in ways no prior era could match.

> [!tip]
> The oral contraceptive pill → declining fertility rates → demographic transition → aging wealthy-nation populations is a CCOT chain the AP loves. Connect birth control to women's rights movements (9.5) and to the contrast between aging developed-nation populations and still-growing developing-nation populations.

### 9.2 — Technological Advancements—Diseases

The same global connectivity that enabled trade and cultural exchange also created rapid pathways for epidemic disease. Unit 9.2 presents the paradox at the heart of globalization: progress and vulnerability are inseparable.

Three diseases illustrate **persistent global inequality** — they kill predominantly in poor countries despite having known causes and partial treatments. **Malaria** killed over 600,000 people annually in the early 21st century, mostly young children in sub-Saharan Africa. **Tuberculosis** — curable since 1946 — resurged in drug-resistant forms in prisons and impoverished communities. **Cholera** — preventable through clean water and hand-washing — still kills ~95,000 people annually. The pattern: when poverty makes prevention and treatment inaccessible, even solved medical problems remain unsolved social problems.

Global eradication campaigns demonstrated what coordinated international effort could achieve. The [[Concept Notes/Smallpox\|smallpox]] campaign declared success in 1979 — the only human disease ever eradicated. The polio campaign reduced cases by 99.9% within 30 years, limited only by war and religious mistrust in Pakistan and Afghanistan. These campaigns are the AP's model of **international cooperation working** — contrast them with the failures of HIV/AIDS response to show the limits of cooperation when stigma, cost, and political will are lacking.

**Emerging epidemics** tracked the pathways of globalization itself. The **1918 influenza pandemic** killed 20+ million people (more than WWI) by spreading along trade routes and troop movements. **HIV/AIDS** identified in 1981 killed 25+ million between 1981 and 2014, with sub-Saharan Africa bearing the heaviest toll. Antiretroviral drugs existed by the mid-1990s but cost what developing-world patients could not pay — until Brazil's 1996 policy of providing free treatment became a global model. **SARS** (2003) and **Ebola** (2014 West Africa outbreak) demonstrated how modern air travel could disseminate a novel pathogen globally within days.

> [!exam-tip]
> Connect 9.2 to 4.3 (Columbian Exchange): the causal structure is identical — increased global interaction spreads disease; populations without prior exposure suffer disproportionately. The differences are scale, speed, and intentionality. That comparison earns complexity points on any LEQ.

> [!warning]
> HIV and AIDS are not synonyms. HIV is the virus; AIDS is the advanced disease stage. A person can be HIV-positive for years without developing AIDS, especially with antiretroviral treatment. The AP expects precision.

→ [[Unit-9.2/Technological Advancements & Limitations — Disease Overview\|Technological Advancements & Limitations — Disease Overview]]

### 9.3 — Technology and the Environment

The industrial technologies that drove unprecedented economic growth in the 20th century also imposed unprecedented costs on the planet. [[Unit-9.3/Technology & the Environment Overview|Topic 9.3]] is the AP's environmental causation chain: industrialization → population growth → deforestation, emissions, water scarcity → **global warming** → contested international responses.

Four interlocking crises: **deforestation** (forests store carbon; their removal both releases CO₂ and eliminates a carbon sink); **desertification** (agricultural overextension degrading land in sub-Saharan Africa and Central Asia); **declining air quality** (industrial and vehicle emissions); and **water scarcity** (31 countries facing shortage by the early 21st century, disproportionately burdening women and girls who bear water collection responsibilities in developing nations). Water scarcity is an environmental problem that becomes a gender equity problem — a favorite AP cross-theme link.

The global response produced two landmark agreements. The **Kyoto Protocol** (1997) was binding but excluded developing nations and was never ratified by the U.S. — severely limiting its impact. The **Paris Agreement** (2015) was broader (195 nations, including China, India, and the U.S.) but less binding; the U.S. withdrew in 2017 and re-entered in 2021. The gap between these two agreements captures the core political tension: developed nations want fast global action; developing nations insist they deserve the same right to industrialize that wealthy nations already exercised. This tension appears on AP exams as a comparison or causation prompt, not a simple morality question.

Environmental activism emerged as a political force. **Earth Day** (1970, now 174 countries), **Greenpeace** (1971), and the **[[Concept Notes/Green Belt Movement\|Green Belt Movement]]** (Kenya's Wangari Maathai, 1977) demonstrated that ordinary people — especially women — could organize against environmental destruction. The concept of the **Anthropocene** — a proposed geological epoch defined by human impact on Earth's systems — represents both scientific classification and moral reckoning.

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the causal chain **and** its political complications: industrialization → greenhouse gases → global warming → Kyoto (failed: no U.S., no developing nations) → Paris (broader but weaker). The AP reward is demonstrating why the international response was limited — not just that it was. The developed vs. developing divide is the answer.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate cause and effect. Population growth is a **cause** of deforestation and water scarcity; global warming is an **effect** of greenhouse gas accumulation. Precision in this chain is what AP causation prompts reward.

→ [[Unit-9.3/Technology & the Environment Overview\|Technology & the Environment Overview]]

### 9.4 — Economics in the Global Age

The late 20th century produced the most dramatic acceleration of economic globalization in human history, driven by three converging forces: the collapse of the Soviet bloc (opening formerly closed economies), the spread of **neoliberal** free-market ideology championed by Reagan and Thatcher, and the digital revolution enabling near-instantaneous global transactions.

**Economic liberalization** spread globally from the 1970s: privatization, deregulation, and reduced tariffs became the dominant policy framework. Chile under Pinochet tested radical free-market economics in the 1970s, with catastrophic social costs that required later governments to re-add social programs before poverty actually declined. China, after Deng Xiaoping's Four Modernizations (1978–1981), opened Special Economic Zones and leased peasant plots — producing explosive growth while brutally suppressing the pro-democracy Tiananmen Square protests in 1989. The lesson: economic openness and political liberalization are separable, and in China were explicitly kept separate.

The **Asian Tiger** model (Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan — following Japan's postwar template) used state-guided export-oriented industrialization and intensive education investment to achieve rapid development. East Asia's rise is the 20th century's defining economic success story — and the AP's model counterargument to claims that globalization benefited only the West.

**NAFTA** (1994) is the AP's go-to example of regional free trade's costs and benefits: growth of Mexican maquiladoras employing cheap labor, U.S. unions protesting job loss, the same day's Zapatista uprising showing who bore the costs. The **WTO** (1995) governed 90%+ of global trade but became a lightning rod for anti-globalization movements because its closed governance served corporate interests over workers and environment (→ [[Unit-9.7/Resistance to Globalization Overview|Topic 9.7]]).

**Multinational corporations** are a deep continuity: the [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] was the original MNC. The AP exam tests students' ability to compare colonial-era joint-stock company extraction with modern MNC behavior — same profit logic, different technology and scale.

> [!exam-tip]
> MNC continuity argument: British East India Company (16th–17th c.) → modern multinationals. Both operate globally for profit, exploit cheap labor, avoid regulation, and concentrate gains in home countries. Use this for CCOT complexity on any Unit 9 economics LEQ.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate free-market ideology with economic success. Chile only reduced poverty after combining free trade WITH social programs. Pure neoliberalism consistently failed the poor — the AP exam rewards that nuance.

### 9.5 — Calls for Reform and Responses After 1900

The age of globalization was also an age of rights. For the first time in human history, a global framework — the **[[Concept Notes/Universal Declaration of Human Rights\|Universal Declaration of Human Rights]]** (1948) — asserted that certain freedoms belonged to all people regardless of race, sex, language, or religion. [[Unit-9.5/Calls for Reform & Responses Overview|Topic 9.5]] traces how individuals and movements demanded that states and institutions honor those rights, and how often they had to fight to make them real.

**Global feminism** advanced dramatically but unevenly. Women won voting rights across the 20th century (New Zealand led in 1893; Kuwait not until 2005). The Fourth World Conference on Women in Beijing (1995), where Hillary Clinton declared "women's rights are human rights," marked a high point of international feminist consensus. But legal rights rarely translated immediately to lived equality — in Pakistan, women had the right to vote in 1947 but cast only 10% of ballots in 2018.

**South Africa's apartheid** struggle is the unit's central political narrative. The **apartheid** system (1948–1994) enforced racial segregation through pass laws, residential restrictions, and educational Afrikanerization to preserve white minority (15%) control. The **[[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|African National Congress]]** led resistance, with Nelson Mandela as its defining symbol. His 27-year imprisonment — and eventual release, election as president in 1994, and establishment of the **Truth and Reconciliation Commission** — illustrates both the power of sustained resistance and the complexity of post-atrocity justice. The TRC, chaired by Desmond Tutu, offered amnesty in exchange for truthful testimony — prioritizing national healing over punishment. Contrast this with the Nuremberg approach (criminal prosecution): both are AP comparison prompts.

**[[Concept Notes/Liberation Theology\|Liberation Theology]]** combined Catholicism with socialist economics in Latin America, interpreting Jesus's teachings as a mandate to end oppression. When Pope Francis — the first Latin American pope — endorsed it in 2013, a movement that military dictators had persecuted gained institutional legitimacy. The Negritude movement (Léopold Senghor, W. E. B. Du Bois) made a parallel cultural argument: colonial assimilation was not progress but cultural erasure.

**Wangari Maathai** is the AP's most productive single figure: she connects Africa + environment + women's rights + colonial land dispossession in a single biography, winning the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004 as the first African woman laureate.

> [!exam-tip]
> Apartheid and anti-apartheid is the most exam-tested topic in 9.5. Know: (1) ANC as the key organization; (2) Mandela as the key figure; (3) **international pressure** (sanctions, divestment, UN expulsion) as the key causal factor ending apartheid; (4) TRC as a distinctive model of post-conflict justice — and how it differs from Nuremberg.

> [!tip]
> **Wangari Maathai** connects four AP themes in one person: ENV (tree-planting), SIO (women's empowerment), GOV (challenging postcolonial government), CDI (indigenous land knowledge). Memorize her for cross-theme essay arguments.

→ [[Unit-9.5/Calls for Reform & Responses Overview\|Calls for Reform & Responses Overview]]

### 9.6 — Globalized Culture After 1900

Globalization after 1900 transformed culture in ways earlier empires never could. Three forces drove it: technological change (radio → film → television → internet → social media), Cold War soft-power competition that weaponized American pop culture, and the economic integration that followed the Cold War's end.

The AP's key distinction for this topic is **bidirectionality**: globalization is not simply Americanization. American consumer culture — Hollywood, Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Nike, Apple, English as a global second language — achieved genuinely global reach. Critics called it **throwaway culture** and cultural imperialism. But culture also flowed the other direction. **Bollywood** is the world's most prolific film industry by output. **Anime** accounts for 60% of the world's animated TV content. **Reggae** (Jamaica) carried Pan-African politics globally through Bob Marley. **K-pop** (South Korea), actively supported by government cultural diplomacy, became a global phenomenon through YouTube and streaming platforms.

**Social media** transformed both cultural diffusion and political resistance. Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube enabled the Arab Spring and amplified voices like **Malala Yousafzai**. Simultaneously, China banned foreign platforms and built state-monitored domestic alternatives (WeChat, Weibo) that enabled both citizen expression and government surveillance. The same technology that empowered dissidents worldwide became a tool of authoritarian control — a double-edged quality the AP tests consistently.

**Modernism** — the early 20th century's deliberate rejection of artistic tradition — reflected the same disorientation that produced World War I and the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]]. The **Harlem Renaissance** of the 1920s was modernism's most vital American expression, and jazz became an early example of Global South culture flowing northward — decades before Bollywood or K-pop.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate globalization with Americanization. The AP explicitly rewards recognizing that cultural flows are multidirectional. Bollywood, K-pop, anime, and reggae are all exam-level examples of non-Western culture achieving global reach.

> [!exam-tip]
> Social media's dual nature — tool of liberation (Arab Spring, Malala) AND tool of control (China's Weibo, surveillance) — is a high-frequency AP prompt. Always argue both sides. That's how you earn the complexity point.

→ [[Unit-9.6/Globalized Culture Overview\|Globalized Culture Overview]]

### 9.7 — Resistance to Globalization

Globalization's benefits were real — and unequally distributed. By the late 20th century, a broad coalition of opponents had organized to resist its effects: labor unions, environmentalists, nationalists, NGOs, and governments. Resistance was not monolithic — some opposed worker exploitation; others rejected cultural homogenization; others defended national sovereignty from supranational institutions. The AP exam tests why resistance emerged (causation) and how different forms of resistance compared across regions and time.

**Labor and consumer rights** resistance focused on the human cost of global supply chains. The **Rana Plaza** factory collapse (Dhaka, 2013 — 1,100 garment workers killed while making clothes for Western brands) became the defining symbol of what globalization meant for workers at the bottom of the supply chain. Child labor in West African cocoa farms supplied the world's chocolate companies even as those companies repeatedly missed self-imposed reform deadlines. At Amazon warehouses in the developed world, workers described productivity quotas enforced at the cost of basic dignity — demonstrating that exploitative conditions were not limited to the Global South.

**Brexit** (2016) is the AP's go-to example of nationalist resistance to supranational integration. 52% of British voters chose to leave the EU over immigration control and sovereignty concerns — a vote that produced years of parliamentary deadlock, Theresa May's resignation, and formal EU exit in 2020. Compare it to 1930s economic nationalism or the post-WWI rejection of the League of Nations: the structural argument (global integration threatens national sovereignty) recurs across centuries.

**Anti-WTO and anti-IMF movements** peaked at the 1999 "Battle of Seattle," where 40,000+ protesters shut down the WTO's millennium summit — one of the first mass social movements coordinated via the internet. Anti-globalists demanded **human rights**, **fair trade**, **sustainable development**, and **debt relief** for heavily indebted nations. The same internet that enabled their organizing enabled China to build surveillance infrastructure to suppress the Uighur minority after 2009 riots — again illustrating technology's double edge.

> [!exam-tip]
> 9.7 pairs with 8.7 (Global Resistance to Established Power Structures) and 6.3 (Indigenous Responses to State Expansion) as AP comparison prompts. The through-line: people resist power arrangements that threaten their autonomy, livelihood, or identity. The forms differ; the logic is consistent across centuries.

### 9.8 — Institutions Developing in the Age of Globalization

The catastrophic destruction of two world wars forced nations to reckon with the limits of unilateral power. The **[[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]]** (1945) was built to replace the failed League of Nations — this time including all major powers, backed by enforcement authority through the Security Council.

The UN's structure reflects the tension between democratic ideals and great-power realism. The **General Assembly** gives every nation an equal vote; the **Security Council** gives five victorious Allied powers (US, UK, France, Russia, China) permanent veto authority. The veto mechanism repeatedly deadlocked the UN during the Cold War, but the organization still expanded from 51 founding members to 193 by 2019. Its specialized agencies — UNESCO, UNICEF, WHO, World Food Program, **[[Concept Notes/International Court of Justice\|International Court of Justice]]** — became major forces in humanitarian governance.

UN **peacekeeping** has a genuinely mixed record. Successes: Namibia's independence transition, ending civil wars in Mozambique and El Salvador, Cambodia's stabilization. Failures: Somalia (civil war raged as troops withdrew), Bosnia (years of mixed results), and most catastrophically Rwanda — where 800,000 people were killed in 100 days while UN peacekeepers watched without authorization to intervene. Rwanda is the AP's strongest evidence for arguments about the limitations of international institutions. The structural problem: Security Council veto deadlock prevents consensus on intervention; and the principle of national sovereignty creates a legal ceiling on what the UN can do inside a member state.

Alongside the UN, a parallel network of **NGOs** — Amnesty International, [[Concept Notes/Doctors Without Borders\|Doctors Without Borders]], Greenpeace, [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|Human Rights]] Watch — applied pressure on governments and filled humanitarian gaps. NGOs demonstrate that international cooperation extends beyond state-level institutions and that civil society shapes global norms — an argument connecting 9.8 to 9.5 (reform movements) and 9.7 (resistance).

> [!exam-tip]
> The most common 9.8 AP essay prompt: "Evaluate the extent to which international institutions successfully promoted global cooperation." Structure your argument: agree partially (UN expanded rights norms, coordinated vaccine campaigns, extended sovereignty to newly independent nations) — but qualify (Security Council veto, Rwanda failure, IMF austerity harmed developing nations). A nuanced evaluation earns the complexity point.

> [!warning]
> The UN did NOT end the Cold War or prevent all conflicts. Avoid overstating its effectiveness. The Rwanda genocide (1994) — where UN peacekeepers were present and did not act effectively — is the strongest counter-evidence. Always pair any success with a failure when evaluating institutional effectiveness.

### 9.9 — Continuity and Change in a Globalized World

[[Unit-9.9/Continuity & Change Overview|Topic 9.9]] is the unit — and the entire course — in synthesis. The AP uses it to ask: across the full sweep of 1900 to the present, what changed, what stayed the same, and what does that tell us about the age of globalization?

The changes are staggering. World population grew from 1.6 billion to 8 billion. Average life expectancy doubled. The center of global manufacturing shifted from Western Europe to East Asia. Women gained legal rights in nearly every country. The Internet collapsed physical distance for information exchange. Environmental change reached planetary scale.

The continuities are equally striking. Economic inequality between developed and developing nations persisted despite new faces of power. Patriarchal structures endured even as women gained legal rights — in Pakistan, women gained the vote in 1947 but cast only 10% of ballots in 2018. States continued to suppress dissent and manipulate economies. Diseases still tracked the fault lines of poverty. The fundamental core-periphery economic structure — more-developed nations extracting resources from less-developed ones — intensified rather than reversed under globalization.

The **economic policy comparison table** (AMSCO p. 710) is a prime AP evidence source: know the goals and results of Lenin's NEP, Stalin's Five-Year Plans, Mao's Great Leap Forward, Deng's Four Modernizations, Roosevelt's New Deal, Reagan's supply-side cuts, and Thatcher's privatization. These contrasting experiments in political economy drive LEQ prompts in both Units 7 and 9.

The **CCOT argument framework**: changes were real but uneven — accelerating in developed nations while leaving structural inequality, patriarchy, and environmental exploitation largely intact in the developing world. That unevenness is not a qualification on the argument — it IS the argument.

> [!exam-tip]
> Thesis template for any Unit 9 CCOT LEQ: "While [X changed significantly] between 1900 and the present, [Y remained a persistent continuity], demonstrating that globalization transformed [domain] unevenly — accelerating change in developed nations while leaving [inequality/patriarchy/environmental exploitation] largely intact in the developing world."

> [!warning]
> The most common AP mistake on synthesis prompts: treating the era as purely progressive. The AP rewards nuance. New vaccines saved millions AND the 1996 UN report showed they only reached developing-world children in the mid-1980s, 15 years after they existed. Medical progress was real AND unequally distributed. Hold both.

## AP Exam Strategy

### Highest-Frequency LEQ Prompts for Unit 9

- Explain the causes and/or effects of technological change after 1900
- Explain the causes and effects of globalization on economic development
- Evaluate the extent to which economic globalization benefited or harmed developing nations
- Explain the causes of resistance to globalization
- Compare resistance movements in the age of globalization to resistance movements in earlier periods
- Evaluate the extent to which international institutions effectively promoted global cooperation after 1945
- Explain continuities and changes in the global economy, culture, or environment from 1900 to the present

### Top Causal Chains

1. **Technology → Globalization:** Communication/transportation innovations + Green Revolution + medical breakthroughs → collapsed distance + expanded food supply + longer lifespans → accelerated economic and cultural integration
2. **Globalization → Inequality:** Free trade + multinational corporations + structural adjustment programs → manufacturing relocated to low-wage regions → core-periphery exploitation intensified in new form
3. **Globalization → Resistance:** Unequal gains + labor exploitation + cultural homogenization + loss of sovereignty → labor movements + Brexit + anti-WTO protests + digital resistance
4. **[[Concept Notes/Industrialization\|Industrialization]] → Environmental Crisis:** Fossil fuels + population growth + deforestation → greenhouse gas accumulation → global warming → Kyoto (failed) → Paris (fragile) → ongoing political deadlock
5. **WWII legacy → International Institutions:** UN created to manage collective security + human rights + development → Security Council veto limits effectiveness → Rwanda failure exposes structural weakness

- **Unit 6 → Unit 9:** Colonial inequality creates the core-periphery structure globalization inherits; anti-colonial resistance ([[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]], Indian National Congress) seeds Unit 9 reform movements
- **Unit 7 → Unit 9:** WWII produces the UN system that manages globalization's collective problems; [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] drives the human rights framework the UDHR codifies
- **Unit 8 → Unit 9:** Cold War end opens Eastern Bloc economies to globalization; Cold War proxy wars create the failed states that become 9.8 peacekeeping crises; civil rights and anti-colonial movements (8.5, 8.7) feed directly into 9.5 reform movements
