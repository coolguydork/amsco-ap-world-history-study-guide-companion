## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the passage below.*

> "Throughout the sixteenth century, the Safavid empire remained a profoundly disturbing force in the Muslim world, dedicated to the defense and propagation of Shi'a doctrines at home and abroad. This policy implied a normal state of hostility with the Ottoman empire, punctuated only briefly by periods of peace. By the seventeenth century, however, when the Safavid empire reached its apogee [peak] under Shah Abbas the Great [1588–1629], the fanaticism of the Shi'a revolution had faded, at least in court circles and a lasting peace with the Ottomans was concluded in 1639."
>
> — William H. McNeill, *The Rise of the West*, 1963

**Question 1.** Which would be the most useful source of evidence to support McNeill's contention that "the Safavid empire remained a profoundly disturbing force in the Muslim world"?

A. A private diary written by an Ottoman government official
B. A biography written in 2018 by an anti-Shi'a leader
C. A Hollywood movie script about Shah Abbas I
D. A campaign speech by Iran's current leader on his country's history

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> A private diary by an Ottoman official would be a **primary source** written by someone directly experiencing the Safavid-Ottoman conflict. It provides firsthand evidence of the Safavid "disturbing force" from the perspective of those affected. B is from 2018 and has ideological bias. C is fiction. D is political speech, not historical evidence.

---

**Question 2.** Today, Iran and Turkey are often political rivals. This passage suggests that this rivalry is based on historical conflicts over:

A. Democracy and political extremism
B. Control of the cities of Medina and Mecca
C. The role of leaders such as the shah and emperor
D. How to practice Islam correctly

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The passage explicitly describes the rivalry as rooted in "Shi'a doctrines" versus the Sunni Ottoman Empire — a dispute over *how to practice Islam correctly*. Iran (formerly Safavid Persia) remains Shia-majority; Turkey (formerly Ottoman) is Sunni-majority. The other options don't match the passage's focus.

---

**Question 3.** What brought an end to the "normal state of hostility" between the Safavids and Ottomans mentioned in the passage?

A. Safavid fervor for its brand of Islam slowly declined until the two empires stopped fighting.
B. The constant fighting increased the respect of the empires for each other, which eventually led to peace.
C. Both sides united to fight against European Christian forces that threatened them.
D. The Ottomans slowly weakened until the Safavids no longer saw them as a threat.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage states: "the fanaticism of the Shi'a revolution had faded, at least in court circles and a lasting peace with the Ottomans was concluded in 1639." This matches A — religious intensity *declined* at the Safavid court, reducing the ideological driver of conflict. B, C, and D are not supported by the passage. Note that D is also historically wrong — the Safavids declined first, not the Ottomans.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Religious Tolerance (Akbar/Mughal)

*Read the passage below, then answer all parts.*

> "[H]aving on one occasion asked my father [Akbar] the reason why he had forbidden any one to prevent or interfere with the building of these haunts of idolatry [Hindu temples], his reply was…: 'I find myself a powerful monarch, the shadow of God upon earth. I have seen that he bestows the blessings of his gracious providence upon all his creatures without distinction. Ill [badly] should I discharge the duties of my exalted station, were I to withhold my compassion and indulgence from any of those entrusted to my charge. With all of the human race, with all of God's creatures, I am at peace: why then should I permit myself, under any consideration, to be the cause of molestation or aggression to any one? Besides, are not five parts in six of mankind either Hindus or aliens to the faith and were I to be governed by motives of the kind suggested in your inquiry, what alternative do I have but to put them all to death? I have thought it therefore my wisest plan to let these men alone.'"
>
> — Jahangir, Mughal emperor (r. 1605–1627), *Memoirs* (quoting his father Akbar)

**A.** Identify ONE example of Akbar's display of religious tolerance.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Akbar permitted the construction of Hindu temples in the Mughal Empire, forbidding officials from interfering with their building. His justification was that he governed all of God's creatures without distinction, and that since most of his subjects were non-Muslim, persecution would require putting them all to death.

**B.** Explain ONE way in which Akbar's response to religious diversity compared to the practices of the Mongols during the 13th century.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Both Akbar and the Mongols practiced a form of religious tolerance toward the diverse populations they governed. The Mongols under rulers like Kublai Khan allowed subject peoples to maintain their own religious practices — Buddhism, Islam, Christianity, and others — as long as they paid taxes and remained politically loyal. Akbar's tolerance was similar in its pragmatism: he explicitly reasoned that persecuting his majority-Hindu subjects would be politically impossible. In both cases, tolerance was as much a governance strategy as a moral commitment.

**C.** Explain ONE way in which Akbar's practice of tolerance was different from the religious tolerance of the Safavids.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contrast
> Unlike Akbar, the Safavids did not practice meaningful religious tolerance. The Safavid state used Shia Islam as an *exclusionary* tool: Shah Ismail I declared Iran officially Shia and used Shia identity to deny legitimacy to Sunni rivals — both domestically and against the Ottoman Empire. Safavid women had limited rights under Islamic law, and the state enforced Islamic orthodoxy. By contrast, Akbar ruled over a largely Hindu population and explicitly allowed Hindu temples, using tolerance to *include* a non-Muslim majority rather than exclude them.

### SAQ 2 — Gunpowder Empires and Technological Expansion

**A.** Identify ONE way in which technological advances affected the expansion of land-based empires.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Gunpowder technology — specifically artillery and cannons — fundamentally transformed imperial expansion by allowing conquering armies to breach stone fortifications that had previously been nearly impregnable. Mehmed II's use of massive bronze cannons in the 1453 siege of Constantinople is the clearest example: a city that had withstood sieges for centuries fell in weeks once Ottoman artillery could destroy its walls.

**B.** Explain ONE reason the Gunpowder Empires rose during the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The Gunpowder Empires rose in part because they exploited the power vacuums left by the collapse of Mongol rule in Central Asia and the Middle East. Tamerlane's invasions in the late 14th century had destabilized existing states across Persia, Central Asia, and northern India, leaving territories weakly defended and politically fragmented. The Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals all moved into regions that had been disrupted by Mongol and post-Mongol instability, using gunpowder weapons to consolidate control where prior regimes had lost legitimacy.

**C.** Explain ONE way in which the cultures of the Gunpowder Empires differed from one another.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contrast
> The Mughal and Safavid empires differed sharply in their approach to religious diversity. The Safavids enforced Shia Islam as the exclusive state religion, using it to unify Persian identity and distinguish themselves from the Sunni Ottomans. The Mughals, by contrast, governed a predominantly Hindu population and under Akbar pursued genuine religious tolerance — allowing Hindu temples, employing Hindu officials, and avoiding religious persecution. While both empires were Islamic states, the Mughal approach was fundamentally pluralistic while the Safavid approach was doctrinally exclusive.

## Essential Question Review

**Reflect:** In one to three paragraphs, explain how certain land-based empires developed and expanded in the period from 1450–1750.

*Use this as a timed writing exercise. A strong response should include: (1) a claim about the factors driving expansion, (2) at least two specific empires as evidence, (3) both military/technological and political/administrative causes.*

> [!tip] Key Points to Hit
> **Argument:** Land-based empires expanded through the combination of gunpowder military technology, exploitation of power vacuums, and effective centralization of governance.
>
> **Evidence:** Ottoman use of artillery at Constantinople (1453); Mughal conquest of India using Timurid military tradition (1526); Russian Cossack expansion to the Pacific (completed 1640); Manchu conquest of Ming China (1644).
>
> **Complexity:** Note that expansion alone wasn't sufficient — the empires that *sustained* their growth (Akbar's Mughal Empire, Kangxi's Qing) were those that combined military expansion with effective administrative systems (mansabdar, devshirme, Confucian bureaucracy). Those that neglected administration eventually collapsed.
