# Unit 3.1: Key People and Groups

#apwh #unit-3 #key-people

---

## Ottoman Empire

### Mehmed II ("The Conqueror") — r. 1451–1481 | Anatolia/Balkans
- Captured Constantinople in 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire and renaming the city Istanbul; used massive bronze cannons to breach the ancient walls — a landmark moment in gunpowder warfare
- Styled himself "Caesar of Rome" (Kayser-i Rum), claiming legitimacy from both Islamic and Roman imperial traditions
- Transformed the Hagia Sophia from a Christian church into a mosque, signaling the new Ottoman order

### Suleiman I ("The Magnificent") — r. 1520–1566 | Ottoman Empire
- Brought the Ottoman Empire to its greatest territorial extent, pushing into Hungary, besieging Vienna (1529), and expanding into North Africa and the Arabian Peninsula
- Known in the Ottoman world as "The Lawgiver" (Kanuni) for his extensive legal reforms codifying Ottoman law
- His reign represents the apex of Ottoman cultural and military power; a benchmark for AP comparisons with contemporaries like the Mughal Akbar

> [!exam-tip]
> Suleiman is a top AP figure — know both his military campaigns (Vienna, Hungary) and his domestic role as a lawgiver/administrator.

## Safavid Empire

### Shah Ismail I — r. 1501–1524 | Persia
- Founded the Safavid dynasty in 1501 and forcibly converted Persia from Sunni to Shia Islam — one of history's most significant forced religious conversions
- Claimed descent from the Sufi Safaviyya order and from Ali (the Prophet Muhammad's son-in-law), giving him both religious and dynastic legitimacy
- His Shia declaration created the enduring Ottoman-Safavid rivalry that shaped Middle Eastern geopolitics

### Shah Abbas I ("The Great") — r. 1588–1629 | Persia
- Reorganized the Safavid military by creating a slave army loyal directly to the shah (modeled on the Ottoman devshirme), reducing dependence on powerful Turkic tribal leaders
- Moved the capital to Isfahan, which he transformed into one of the world's most beautiful cities (the saying "Isfahan is half the world")
- Opened diplomatic and trade relations with European powers to counter Ottoman influence; promoted Persian arts, architecture, and silk production

## Mughal Empire

### Babur — r. 1526–1530 | Indian subcontinent
- Descendant of both Timur (Tamerlane) and Genghis Khan; invaded the Indian subcontinent from Central Asia and defeated the Delhi Sultanate at the First Battle of Panipat (1526) using gunpowder artillery
- Founded the Mughal dynasty but ruled only briefly; his memoirs (Baburnama) are a remarkable first-person historical source
- His conquest demonstrates the role of gunpowder technology in displacing older military orders

### Akbar ("The Great") — r. 1556–1605 | Indian subcontinent
- Expanded Mughal territory across most of the Indian subcontinent through military conquest and strategic marriages with Rajput Hindu nobility
- Famous for religious tolerance: abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims, invited Hindu, Jain, Christian, and Zoroastrian scholars to court debates, and created the syncretic Din-i-Ilahi ("Divine Faith")
- Established the mansabdar administrative system, creating a merit-based imperial bureaucracy

> [!exam-tip]
> Akbar is the single most tested Mughal figure on the AP exam. His religious tolerance policies are compared with rulers who used religion to *enforce* unity rather than *accommodate* diversity.

### Aurangzeb — r. 1658–1707 | Indian subcontinent
- Expanded Mughal territory to its greatest extent but overextended the empire; reversed Akbar's tolerant policies by reinstating the jizya and destroying Hindu temples
- His strict Sunni orthodoxy alienated Hindus, Sikhs, and regional nobles, fueling revolts (especially the Marathas) that fragmented the empire after his death
- A key "continuity vs. change" figure — he represents a sharp break from Akbar's approach

## Qing Dynasty

### Nurhaci — r. 1616–1626 | Manchuria/China
- Unified the Manchu tribes and created the Eight Banners military system, which organized Manchu society and military power
- Launched the Qing conquest of China, though he died before its completion; laid the organizational and military groundwork for the dynasty

### Kangxi Emperor — r. 1661–1722 | China
- One of the longest-reigning emperors in Chinese history; consolidated Qing rule by defeating Ming loyalists, incorporated Taiwan (1683), fought the Zunghars on the northern steppe, and asserted Qing influence in Tibet — note: greatest *territorial* expansion (esp. Xinjiang) came under his grandson Qianlong (r. 1735–1796)
- Managed the tension between Manchu identity and Confucian governance; tolerated Jesuit missionaries at court for their scientific knowledge, though he restricted Christianity's spread among the general population; broader suppression came under Yongzheng, following the Chinese Rites Controversy
- Compiled the Kangxi Dictionary and promoted the arts — a "sage-king" figure in the Confucian tradition

## Russian Empire

### Ivan III ("The Great") — r. 1462–1505 | Russia
- Threw off Mongol (Golden Horde) domination and began unifying Russian principalities under Muscovite rule
- His reign created the conditions for the "Third Rome" ideology — marrying Byzantine princess Sophia Palaiologina and adopting the double-headed eagle — but the idea was articulated explicitly by the monk Filofei of Pskov in letters to his son Vasili III, not formally proclaimed by Ivan III himself
- Began the process of Russian state-building that would accelerate under Ivan IV and Peter the Great

### Ivan IV ("The Terrible") — r. 1547–1584 | Russia
- First Russian ruler to formally adopt the title "Tsar of All Russia," centralizing power in the monarchy
- Created the streltsy (musketeer units) and used Cossack warriors to push Russia's eastern frontier into Siberia
- The "Oprichnina" — his secret police and campaign of terror against noble (boyar) families — represents a brutal tool of political centralization

## Songhai Empire

### Sunni Ali — r. 1464–1492 | West Africa
- Military genius who transformed Songhai from a regional power into the largest empire in West African history by capturing Timbuktu and Djenné
- Relied on cavalry and river fleet to project power along the Niger River
- His relationship with Islamic scholars was tense — he was seen as insufficiently orthodox — a contrast to his successor

### Askia Muhammad (Muhammad Toure) — r. 1493–1528 | West Africa
- Devout Muslim who overthrew Sunni Ali's son and established a more thoroughly Islamized administration
- Organized the empire into provinces with governors, a professional army, and standardized weights and measures — sophisticated state-building for the era
- Made a famous pilgrimage to Mecca in 1496–97 that enhanced Songhai's prestige; forged ties with scholars across the Islamic world
