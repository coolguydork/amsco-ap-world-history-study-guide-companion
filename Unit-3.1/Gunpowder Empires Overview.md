# Topic 3.1 — European, East Asian, and Gunpowder Empires Expand

> [!summary] Essential Question
> How did certain land-based empires develop and **expand** in the period from 1450–1750?

## Big Picture: Why 1450 Matters

The year 1450 marks the opening of the **early modern period** — a world of consolidating states, gunpowder weapons, and transoceanic expansion. The mid-1400s saw: the end of the plague waves, the end of the Hundred Years' War between France and England, and the invention of the **Gutenberg printing press**. Europe's "new monarchies" began launching overseas empires. Meanwhile, across Eurasia, powerful states were rising on the foundation of a single technology: **gunpowder**.

The pattern is clear: empires that mastered gunpowder — cannons, muskets, artillery — could conquer territory that was previously defensible. This period is characterized by **armed trade**: empires exchanged goods with one another, but always with troops ready if trade terms were challenged.

---

## The Gunpowder Empires: Who Were They?

The **[[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|Gunpowder Empires]]** were large, multiethnic states in Southwest, Central, and South Asia that relied on firearms to conquer and hold territory. The three Islamic empires — **Ottoman**, **Safavid**, and **Mughal** — shared critical characteristics:

- Descended from Turkic nomads of Central Asia
- Spoke a Turkic language
- Exploited power vacuums left by the collapse of Mongol khanates (see [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview]])
- Relied on artillery, cannons, and gunpowder weapons
- Rose because of their own military strength *and* the weakness of the regimes they replaced

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests the **shared origins** of the three Islamic Gunpowder Empires. They all trace back to Turkic nomads from Central Asia who took advantage of post-Mongol disorder. Tamerlane is the critical link.

## Tamerlane: The Stage-Setter

[[Concept Notes/Tamerlane|Tamerlane]] (Timur the Lame, c. 1370s–1405) was a Mongol-Turkic ruler who moved out of **Samarkand** (in modern Uzbekistan) to make ruthless conquests in Persia and India. Some historians credit him with the massacre of 100,000 Hindus before the gates of Delhi.

The **ghazi ideal** — a model for warrior life blending nomadic cooperative values with holy war for Islam — shaped the armies of the Gunpowder Empires. Tamerlane's rule illustrates the tension between violence and cultural patronage that characterized these empires. He championed literature, architecture, and the arts in Samarkand even while devastating the regions he conquered.

Tamerlane's weakness: he could conquer but not govern. Without effective political structure in conquered areas, his empire fragmented. His descendant **Babur** would eventually channel this legacy into founding the Mughal Empire in India.

## The Ottoman Empire

**Founded**: 1300s by the Osman dynasty  
**Peak**: Under Suleiman I (ruled 1520–1566)  
**Duration**: Over 600 years — until 1918 defeat in WWI

### Mehmed II "the Conqueror" (ruled 1451–1481)

The pivotal moment: the **fall of Constantinople in 1453**. Using a 26-foot bronze cannon and several additional cannons, Ottoman forces breached the city's legendary triple fortifications. Constantinople became **Istanbul** — capital of an empire at the crossroads of the Bosporus Strait, controlling the only waterway between the Aegean and Black Seas.

Mehmed next seized lands around the Black Sea, pushed into the Balkans, and challenged Venice's maritime power. The early 16th century added Syria, Israel, Egypt, and Algeria. Istanbul became a new center of Islam as the Mamluk Dynasty declined (see [[Unit-1.2/Dar al-Islam]]).

### Suleiman I "the Magnificent" (ruled 1520–1566)

The Ottoman Empire at its absolute height. Suleiman's armies:
- Overran Hungary in 1526
- Threatened Vienna twice (closest points to Western Europe)
- Captured Rhodes from Christian knights (1522)
- Dominated North Africa

The near-siege of Vienna sent shockwaves through Christian Europe. The Ottomans were not a distant threat — they were at Austria's doorstep.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse the **Siege of Vienna (1529)** under Suleiman with the **later Siege of Vienna (1683)**, which marked the definitive Ottoman retreat from Central Europe.

## The Safavid Empire

**Founded**: 1501, when Ismail conquered Persia and declared himself **shah**  
**Peak**: Under Shah Abbas I "the Great" (ruled 1588–1629)  
**Defining feature**: Shi'a Islam as state religion

The Safavids had two structural weaknesses: no significant navy (despite access to the Arabian Sea) and no natural geographic defenses. Yet they rose to power through land-based military strength.

Shah Abbas I imported European weapons technology and used Christian boys from Georgia as soldiers — mirroring the Ottoman **devshirme** system. Slowly, the shahs merged political and religious authority.

### The Sunni-Shi'a Fault Line

The Safavids' insistence on Shi'a Islam as the only legitimate faith put them on a permanent war footing with the **Sunni Ottoman Empire**. In 1541, Ottoman forces stopped Safavid expansion at Tabriz. The Safavid-Ottoman border became the historical Sunni-Shi'a dividing line — visible today in the Iraq-Iran border.

The conflict was not purely religious: the Ottomans used **trade embargoes** against Safavid silk traders to assert economic dominance. Commerce and faith were intertwined.

> [!tip]
> The Sunni-Shi'a split originated in a 7th-century dispute over Muhammad's successor. The Safavids institutionalized Shi'a Islam in Persia, giving the ancient theological conflict a geopolitical form that persists today.

## Mughal India

**Founded**: 1520s by Babur (descendant of Tamerlane)  
**Peak**: Under Akbar (ruled 1556–1605)  
**Setting**: India in disarray after collapse of earlier sultanates

Babur established a central government in northern India. His grandson **Akbar** made the Mughal Empire one of the richest and best-governed states in the world. Trade flourished — Arab merchants dominated commerce in textiles, tropical foods, spices, and precious stones, exchanged for gold and silver.

The Indian **caste system** (jatis) structured Mughal society: Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras, and outside the system, the Dalits (untouchables). Merchant castes participated in banking and handicraft production.

Mughal India produced magnificent architecture — the **Taj Mahal** (built by Shah Jahan) being the most famous — as testaments to imperial wealth and Mughal patronage of the arts.

## Europe and Russia

### New Monarchies in Europe

Post-plague Europe saw centralized "new monarchies": the **Tudors** in England, **Valois** in France, **Isabella and Ferdinand** in Spain. These monarchs:
- Controlled taxes and the army
- Expanded bureaucracies
- Curbed the power of feudal nobles

The middle class grew at the expense of lords and the Church. Literacy increased with the Gutenberg press. These states would soon fund transoceanic exploration — connecting empires to a global network (see [[Unit-4.1/European Maritime Exploration]]).

### Russia Under Ivan IV

**Ivan IV** ("Ivan the Terrible," ruled 1547–1584) was crowned tsar and immediately expanded eastward — defeating the khanates of Kazan, Astrakhan, and Siberia, all descendants of the [[Concept Notes/Mongol Empire|Golden Horde]].

Key expansion mechanism: the **Cossacks**, fierce peasant warriors hired by the Stroganov landowners to fight tribes and Siberian khans. By controlling the Volga River, Moscow gained direct access to the Caspian Sea — and trade routes to Persia and the Ottomans without dealing with Crimean Tartar intermediaries.

By **1639**, Russia had expanded all the way to the Pacific Ocean. Fur traders and missionaries followed, converting many indigenous peoples to Eastern Orthodoxy.

### China: Ming to Qing

The **Ming Dynasty** (1368–1644) stabilized East Asia for nearly 300 years after overthrowing the Mongol Yuan Dynasty. But in the 1440s, Mongol armies defeated Ming forces and captured the emperor — prompting massive expansion and restoration of the **Great Wall**.

In 1644, the **Manchu** from Manchuria seized power and founded the **Qing Dynasty** (ruled until 1911).

Key Qing rulers:
- **Kangxi** (ruled 1661–1722): expanded into Taiwan, Mongolia, Central Asia; imposed protectorate over Tibet
- **Qianlong** (ruled 1736–1796): poet and scholar; annexed Xinjiang; repelled Nepal; drained treasury in Burma and Vietnam campaigns

When Britain asked for expanded trading rights in 1793, Qianlong famously refused, saying China had no need of British manufactured goods. This exchange foreshadows the conflicts of the 19th century (see [[Unit-6.2/Opium Wars and China]]).

## Decline of the Gunpowder Empires

All three Islamic Gunpowder Empires eventually fell as **Western Europe grew in economic and naval power**. Unlike these empires, Russia modernized its army along Western European lines (England, France, the Netherlands).

| Empire | Key Cause of Decline |
|--------|---------------------|
| Ottoman | Weak sultans after Suleiman; "harem politics"; Battle of Lepanto (1571); failed Siege of Vienna (1683) |
| Safavid | Ineffectual leaders after Abbas; Sunni Pashtun rebellion (1722); sack of Isfahan |
| Mughal | Aurangzeb's intolerant policies; treasury drained by expansion; British and French economic penetration |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves the concept of **"harem politics"** in the Ottoman Empire — concubines and wives competing to place their sons on the throne. This weakened the sultanate by producing rival factions at court rather than competent successors.

## Continuity and Change

**Continuity**: Trade along established routes (Silk Roads, Indian Ocean) continued. The use of Islam as a legitimizing force for rulers carried forward from earlier periods. The hierarchical social structures of Central Asian nomadic culture persisted inside these states.

**Change**: Gunpowder fundamentally changed **who could hold power**. States without access to firearms could not defend territory. This period produced the most powerful centralized empires Eurasia had ever seen — until European naval power began to surpass them.
