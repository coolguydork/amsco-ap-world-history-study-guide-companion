# European, East Asian, and [[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|Gunpowder Empires]] Expand

**Period:** 1450–1750 | **AP Themes:** GOV · TEC · MIG
**Essential Question:** How did certain land-based empires develop and expand in the period from 1450–1750?

## Context: The Age of Land-Based Empires

Between 1450 and 1750, several of history's greatest land-based empires reached their peak of wealth and influence. These were multiethnic states that exercised direct political control over large regions and overland trade routes. Their power rested on three pillars: **military expansion** (they measured power in land, so they warred constantly for territory), **centralization** (consolidating authority through bureaucratic elites and professional armies, funded by tribute and taxation), and **religious legitimacy** (empires were often tightly tied to particular faiths, intertwining political and religious conflict).

The key empires of this era — Songhai, Safavid, Mughal, Ottoman, and Qing — all began declining after 1750, as ocean trade routes, rising ethnic nationalism, and European economic power eroded the unity and influence these states had built.

> [!tip]
> Think of 1450–1750 as the *last great age of land empires* before oceanic trade and European industrialization shift the balance of power. Three AP themes run throughout: GOV (how rulers built and controlled states), TEC (how gunpowder changed warfare), and MIG (how populations moved with and against expanding empires).

## Europe: New Monarchies

By the early 1500s, several European states were becoming powerful, wealthy nations after the slow development of the Middle Ages. The key driver was **centralization**: new monarchs moved to control taxes, armies, and religion — curbing the private power of nobles and churches. Key examples include the Tudors in England, the Valois in France, and Ferdinand and Isabella in Spain. Bureaucracies expanded, and a growing middle class gained influence at the expense of old feudal lords.

The invention of the **Gutenberg printing press** (mid-1400s) accelerated literacy across Europe. This same period saw new monarchies launching overseas exploration — the beginning of Europe's expansion beyond its own borders (explored in Unit 4).

## Russia: Expansion East

Russia occupied a pivotal position for trade between Europe and Asia. When **Ivan IV** ("Ivan the Terrible") was crowned tsar in 1547, he immediately pushed Russia's borders eastward. He took control of the khanates of Kazan, Astrakhan, and Siberia from the descendants of the Golden Horde — expansion made possible by gunpowder weapons.

To push further east, Ivan IV used the **Stroganov** merchant family, who hired fierce peasant-warriors called **Cossacks** to fight local tribes and the Siberian khan. Russian control of the Volga River gave Moscow direct access to the Caspian Sea and trade routes to Persia and the Ottomans — bypassing the Crimean Tatars.

By 1640, Russia had reached the Pacific Ocean. Missionaries followed traders, converting indigenous Siberians to Eastern Orthodox Christianity, though local shamans retained influence. Russia's expansion continued across the Pacific to Alaska (1741) and down the North American coast.

> [!exam-tip]
> Russia is the **non-Islamic gunpowder empire** the AP exam loves to compare. Unlike the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires, Russia later *modernized its military* after Western European models — a key reason it survived while the others declined.

## East Asia: Ming and Qing China

**The Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)** stabilized East Asia for nearly 300 years after overthrowing the Mongol Yuan Dynasty. The Ming expanded into Mongolia and Central Asia but could not hold those gains. After renewed Mongol pressure in the 1400s, the Ming rebuilt and expanded the **Great Wall of China** as protection from northern invaders.

In 1644, the **Manchu** from neighboring Manchuria overthrew the Ming and established the **Qing Dynasty** (1644–1912). Although foreign rulers, the Manchu adopted Chinese bureaucratic governance while maintaining ethnic distinctions.

**Emperor Kangxi** (r. 1661–1722) presided over a golden age: stability, territorial expansion into Taiwan, Mongolia, and Central Asia, and a protectorate over Tibet. **Emperor Qianlong** (r. 1736–1796) extended Chinese territory further west into Xinjiang (at great human cost to the local Uighur population) and maintained tributary relationships with surrounding states. However, the later Qing period saw growing bureaucratic corruption and popular rebellion — notably the **White Lotus Rebellion (1796–1804)**, suppressed brutally by the Qing government.

Japan experienced parallel developments: in 1603, **Tokugawa Ieyasu** became shogun, unifying Japan under centralized military rule and beginning a long era of stability and controlled isolation.

## The Islamic Gunpowder Empires

Three large, multiethnic Islamic empires — the **Ottoman**, **Safavid**, and **Mughal** — share a common origin story. All descended from Turkic nomads of Central Asia, spoke a Turkic language, took advantage of power vacuums left by the collapse of Mongol khanates, and relied on **gunpowder weapons** (artillery and cannons) for their conquests. Collectively they are called the **Gunpowder Empires**.

### Tamerlane (Timur): The Precursor

**Tamerlane** (Timur the Lame, late 14th century) set the stage for all three empires. Operating from Samarkand (modern Uzbekistan), he used a nomadic-Islamic warrior ideal called the **ghazi ideal** to justify brutal conquests across Persia and India — including the massacre of approximately 100,000 Hindus outside Delhi. Though his empire fragmented after his death, he modeled the gunpowder-dependent military state that the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals would each refine. He also showed that rulers could pair ruthless conquest with patronage of the arts and learning.

### The Ottoman Empire

Founded by the Osman Dynasty in the 1300s, the Ottoman Empire was the largest and most enduring Islamic empire, lasting over 600 years.

**Mehmed II** (r. 1451–1481), called "the Conqueror," captured **Constantinople in 1453** using massive bronze cannons to breach the Byzantine city's triple walls. Renamed Istanbul, the city controlled the Bosporus Strait (the only waterway between the Aegean and Black Seas) and became a nexus of trade and the center of the Islamic world. Mehmed then expanded into the Balkans and countered Venetian sea power by building a strong Ottoman navy.

**Suleiman I** (r. 1520–1566), "the Magnificent," brought the empire to its peak. His armies overran Hungary in 1526 and reached the gates of **Vienna** in 1529. His navy captured Rhodes (1522) and controlled Tripoli in North Africa. Called "the Lawgiver" by his own people, Suleiman codified Ottoman law. His forces fought constant wars against the Safavids — both over religion (Sunni vs. Shia) and over control of overland trade routes.

### The Safavid Empire

Founded in 1501 by **Shah Ismail I** at just 14–15 years old, the Safavid Empire declared Iran officially **Shia Muslim** — creating a permanent Sunni-Shia fault line with the Ottomans. Despite lacking a navy and natural defenses, the Safavids thrived through land-based military power and strong leadership.

**Shah Abbas I** ("the Great," r. 1588–1629) brought the Safavid Empire to its height. He modernized the military using European mercenaries and imported European weaponry and advisors. He moved the capital to Isfahan and recovered territories lost to the Ottomans. Safavid women, though veiled and restricted in movement, retained Islamic legal rights to inheritance and, in extreme cases, divorce.

The Safavid Empire collapsed in 1722 when Afghan Sunni Pashtuns sacked Isfahan — demonstrating how internal religious repression and weak leadership could rapidly unravel even a powerful empire.

### The Mughal Empire

**Babur** (a descendant of Tamerlane) founded the Mughal Empire in 1526 after conquering northern India during a period of political disarray. His grandson **Akbar** (r. 1556–1605) made the Mughal Empire one of the richest and best-governed states in the world. Akbar promoted **religious tolerance**, developed the **mansabdar** administrative system, and oversaw flourishing overseas and internal trade (textiles, spices, precious stones exchanged for gold and silver). India's **caste system** (*jati*) structured internal commerce, with merchant castes controlling banking and handicraft production.

Mughal decline accelerated under **Aurangzeb** (r. 1658–1707), who reversed Akbar's tolerance — imposing the *jizya* tax on non-Muslims, persecuting Sikhs and Hindus, and overextending the empire in southern India. His policies sparked constant rebellions, drained the treasury, and left the empire vulnerable to British and French economic penetration.

## Decline of the Gunpowder Empires

All three Islamic gunpowder empires declined for similar structural reasons: weak successors, expensive wars, falling revenues, and failure to modernize militaries in the face of growing European sea power. The Battle of Lepanto (1571) — where a Spanish-Venetian fleet defeated the Ottomans — signaled that European naval power was beginning to outmatch land-based empires. Russia survived this era because it *did* modernize its military along European lines; the Gunpowder Empires did not.

| Empire | Peak Ruler | Key Decline Moment |
|---|---|---|
| Ottoman | Suleiman I (r. 1520–1566) | Failed Siege of Vienna (1683); "Sick Man of Europe" |
| Safavid | Shah Abbas I (r. 1588–1629) | Afghan sack of Isfahan (1722) |
| Mughal | Akbar (r. 1556–1605) | Aurangzeb's overextension and intolerance (post-1707) |

> [!warning]
> A common mistake: labeling Russia or China as "Gunpowder Empires." The term specifically refers to the three **Islamic** empires (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal). Russia and China also used gunpowder and expanded in this era, but they are analyzed separately on the AP exam.
