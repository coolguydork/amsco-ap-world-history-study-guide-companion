# Key Terms & Concepts

*Source: AMSCO pp. 143–151*

---

## Core Concepts

**[[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|Gunpowder Empires]]**
A term historians use to describe the large, multiethnic states in Southwest, Central, and South Asia (specifically Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal) that relied on firearms — especially artillery and cannons — to conquer and control vast territories. Their shared Turkic-nomadic origins, use of gunpowder, and Islamic identity distinguish them from other land-based empires of the period. (p. 143)

**Land-based empires**
States that measured power in territory controlled rather than in maritime trade. These empires expanded by conquering neighboring lands, controlling overland trade routes, and taxing subject peoples. Between 1450 and 1750 they reached peak power; after 1750 they began declining as ocean trade routes and European sea power grew. (p. 141)

**Ghazi ideal**
A warrior model that blended the cooperative values of nomadic steppe culture with the willingness to fight as a holy warrior for Islam. Originating on the Eurasian steppes, the ghazi ideal served as the motivational framework for the Mongol-Turkic warriors who built the Gunpowder Empires — including Tamerlane. (p. 146)

**Centralization**
The political process by which rulers concentrated power in a central government — controlling taxes, armies, bureaucracies, and often religion. All land-based empires of this period (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Qing, Russian) pursued centralization to maintain control over multiethnic, geographically vast territories. (p. 141)

**Armed trade**
A type of exchange common among expanding empires in this period. Empires traded with one another but kept troops and armaments ready in case trading rights were questioned. This differed from the free markets that emerged in later eras. (p. 143)

## Government & Administration

**Shah**
Title equivalent to king or emperor, used by rulers of the Safavid Empire (and later Persia generally). Shah Ismail I (founded 1501) and Shah Abbas I (r. 1588–1629) were the two most significant Safavid shahs. (p. 148)

**Devshirme**
The Ottoman system of conscripting Christian boys from conquered territories, converting them to Islam, and training them for elite government and military service. Discussed in detail in Topic 3.2; relevant here because it reflects Ottoman administrative innovation built on military expansion.

**Janissaries**
Elite Ottoman infantry units composed of soldiers trained through the devshirme system. Their military discipline was a key factor in Ottoman expansion. Discussed in depth in Topic 3.2.

**Mansabdar system**
The Mughal administrative system developed under Akbar in which officials held ranks (*mansab*) entitling them to revenue from assigned lands in exchange for military and administrative service. A key tool of Mughal centralization. Detailed coverage in Topic 3.2.

**Tsar**
Title meaning "Caesar," used by Russian rulers. Ivan IV became the first Russian ruler to formally adopt the title of tsar in 1547, symbolizing the centralization of Russian power and the claim to imperial authority. (p. 144)

**New monarchies**
The centralized states that emerged in Western Europe in the late 1400s–1500s, characterized by rulers who consolidated control over taxes, armies, and religion. Examples: Tudors (England), Valois (France), Ferdinand and Isabella (Spain). Bureaucracies expanded; nobility's private power shrank. (p. 144)

## Technology & Military

**Gunpowder**
Invented in China, gunpowder spread via trade routes and became the transformative military technology of the 1450–1750 period. Artillery and cannons allowed smaller forces to breach fortifications and conquer territories that would have been impregnable in earlier eras. Mehmed II's 26-foot bronze cannon destroyed Constantinople's triple walls in 1453. (p. 143, 147)

**Gutenberg printing press**
Invented in the mid-1400s by Johannes Gutenberg, this technology dramatically increased literacy rates in Europe by making books affordable. It accelerated the spread of ideas (including the [[Concept Notes/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]]) and contributed to the emergence of powerful new European states. (p. 144)

**Artillery / Cannons**
Heavy firearms mounted on wheeled carriages, capable of destroying stone fortifications. The Ottoman use of massive cannons at the siege of Constantinople (1453) demonstrated their game-changing role in land-based imperial expansion. Both the Safavids and Mughals similarly relied on artillery. (p. 147)

**Cossacks**
Fierce peasant-warriors from the Russian steppe, hired by the Stroganov merchant family under Ivan IV's authorization to push Russia's frontier eastward through Siberia. The Cossacks defeated local indigenous tribes, enabling Russian control of the fur trade and the Volga River route. (p. 144–145)

## China & East Asia

**Ming Dynasty**
Chinese dynasty (1368–1644) that overthrew the Mongol Yuan Dynasty. The Ming stabilized East Asia for ~300 years, expanded the Great Wall, and oversaw the arrival of European traders. Overthrown by the Manchu in 1644. (p. 145)

**Manchu**
The ethnic group from Manchuria (northeastern China) who overthrew the Ming Dynasty and established the Qing Dynasty in 1644. Though foreign rulers, the Manchu largely adopted Chinese governance structures. (p. 145)

**Qing Dynasty**
Chinese dynasty (1644–1912) established by the Manchu. Under Kangxi and Qianlong, the Qing expanded China's territory significantly and presided over long periods of stability. Became increasingly vulnerable to internal rebellion and European pressure in the 19th century. (p. 145)

**Emperor Kangxi**
Qing emperor (r. 1661–1722); one of China's longest-reigning emperors. Expanded into Taiwan, Mongolia, Central Asia; imposed protectorate over Tibet. Presided over the Qing golden age. (p. 145)

**Emperor Qianlong**
Qing emperor (r. 1736–1796); poet and patron of arts. Extended Chinese territory west into Xinjiang (with mass killings of Uighurs); imposed Chinese authority on Nepal. Later reign marked by bureaucratic corruption and the White Lotus Rebellion. (p. 145–146)

**White Lotus Rebellion (1796–1804)**
A peasant uprising in late Qing China, sparked by high taxes and a desire to restore the Ming Dynasty. The Qing government suppressed it brutally, killing around 100,000 people. An early sign of Qing decline. (p. 146)

**Great Wall of China**
A series of fortifications along China's northern border. Originally built in earlier dynasties, the Wall fell into disrepair under Mongol rule. The Ming Dynasty restored and expanded it to protect against renewed Mongol invasions. Symbolizes the Ming strategy of territorial defense over expansion. (p. 145)

## Islamic Empires

**Ottoman Empire**
The largest and most enduring of the Islamic Gunpowder Empires (c. 1299–1922). Founded by the Osman Dynasty; reached peak under Suleiman I (r. 1520–1566). Controlled the Bosporus Strait, key trade routes, and vast territory across the Middle East, North Africa, and southeastern Europe. Sunni Muslim. (p. 147)

**Safavid Empire**
Islamic Gunpowder Empire based in Persia (1501–1736). Founded by Shah Ismail I; peaked under Shah Abbas I. Distinguished by official **Shia** Islam, creating a permanent religious fault line with the Sunni Ottomans. Collapsed in 1722 after Afghan forces sacked Isfahan. (p. 148)

**Mughal Empire**
Islamic Gunpowder Empire based in India (1526–1857). Founded by Babur; peaked under Akbar (r. 1556–1605). Known for religious tolerance under Akbar and magnificent architecture (Taj Mahal under Shah Jahan). Declined under Aurangzeb's intolerance and military overextension. (p. 149)

**Tamerlane (Timur the Lame)**
Mongol-Turkic ruler of the late 14th century who conquered Central Asia, Persia, and northern India from his base in Samarkand. His invasions created the power vacuums and established the gunpowder-military model that the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals later inherited. Despite brutal tactics, he patronized learning and the arts. (p. 146–147)

**Sunni-Shia rivalry**
The fundamental theological split within Islam, between Sunni (majority worldwide, dominant in the Ottoman Empire) and Shia (minority, established as state religion by the Safavids). This rivalry fueled nearly constant Ottoman-Safavid conflict, making it one of the defining geopolitical tensions of the 1450–1750 period. (p. 148)

**Trade embargoes**
Official bans on trade, used by the Ottomans as economic weapons against the Safavids. The Ottomans consistently imposed embargoes on Safavid silk traders to assert dominance over their eastern rival. (p. 148)

**Castes (jati)**
Strict social groupings in Hindu society, determined at birth. The four main categories (Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, Shudras) plus the Dalits (untouchables) governed educational and vocational opportunities. Merchant castes (*jati*) organized internal trade and banking within the Mughal Empire. (p. 149)

> [!exam-tip]
> For the AP exam, the most important comparison is **Akbar vs. Aurangzeb**: Akbar's religious tolerance and administrative innovation vs. Aurangzeb's intolerance and overextension. This contrast illustrates both why empires succeed and why they decline — a classic continuity and change question.

> [!tip]
> All three Gunpowder Empires share this pattern: brilliant founding/peak ruler → weak successors → economic strain from military spending → inability to modernize → decline. Memorize one example per empire (Suleiman/Aurangzeb/Shah Abbas), and you can answer most comparison questions.
