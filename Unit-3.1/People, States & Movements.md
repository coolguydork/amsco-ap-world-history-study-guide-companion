# People, States & Movements

*Source: AMSCO pp. 143–151*

---

## Key Leaders

### Tamerlane (Timur the Lame) — Central Asia, late 14th century
Mongol-Turkic conqueror who operated from Samarkand (modern Uzbekistan). Used the **ghazi ideal** to justify holy war conquests across Persia and India; massacred approximately 100,000 Hindus outside Delhi. His empire largely fragmented after his death, but his invasions cleared the path for the three Islamic [[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|Gunpowder Empires]]. Despite brutal tactics, he was a patron of arts, architecture, and literature — his buildings in Samarkand still stand. His descendant Babur would use the remnant of his empire to found the Mughal Dynasty. (pp. 146–147)

> [!tip]
> Tamerlane is the **bridge figure** between Mongol disruption [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Mongol Empire Overview]] and Gunpowder Empire rise [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview]]. He demonstrates that destruction and cultural patronage could coexist — a pattern the Mughals later perfected.

### Mehmed II — Ottoman Sultan (r. 1451–1481)
Called "the Conqueror." His defining act: the **siege of Constantinople (1453)**, where massive bronze cannons — including a 26-foot bronze cannon — breached the Byzantine city's triple walls. Renamed the city Istanbul and made it the Ottoman capital and a hub of Islamic civilization. Then pushed into the Balkans, strengthened the navy to counter Venice, and forced Venice to pay a yearly tax. Transformed the Ottoman state from a regional power into a major empire. (pp. 147–148)

### Suleiman I (Suleiman the Magnificent) — Ottoman Sultan (r. 1520–1566)
Peak of Ottoman power. Militarily: overran Hungary (1526), threatened Vienna (1529), captured Rhodes (1522), and took Tripoli in North Africa. Legally: codified Ottoman law — his own people called him "the Lawgiver." His reign represents the high-water mark of Ottoman expansion into both Europe and the Mediterranean. After his death, the empire entered a long decline, eventually known as the "Sick Man of Europe." (p. 148)

### Shah Ismail I — Safavid Founder (r. 1501–1524)
Founded the Safavid dynasty at approximately 14–15 years old by conquering all of Iran and declaring himself Shah. His pivotal act: declaring Iran an officially **Shia Muslim** state, creating a permanent religious divide with the Sunni Ottoman Empire. He built a power base using Shia identity as a unifying force, denying legitimacy to any Sunni ruler. (p. 148)

### Shah Abbas I (Abbas the Great) — Safavid Shah (r. 1588–1629)
Brought the Safavid Empire to its height. Key reforms: imported European military advisors and weaponry; recruited Christian soldiers (often Georgian boys pressed into service) from as far as Russia; moved the capital to Isfahan; reconquered territory lost to the Ottomans. His military modernization — using European mercenaries and technology — was a rare example of a Gunpowder Empire adapting to European military advances. (p. 148)

### Babur — Mughal Founder (r. 1526–1530)
A descendant of Tamerlane from Central Asia. Conquered northern India in 1526 when India was in political disarray, establishing the 300-year Mughal dynasty. Created a central government modeled on the style of Suleiman in Turkey. The Mughal name itself is a variant of "Mongol," reflecting Babur's Timurid/Mongol heritage. (p. 149)

### Akbar — Mughal Emperor (r. 1556–1605)
Babur's grandson; widely regarded as the greatest Mughal ruler. Under Akbar, the Mughal Empire became one of the richest and best-governed states in the world. Key achievements: promoted **religious tolerance** toward Hindus and other minorities; developed the **mansabdar** administrative system; oversaw flourishing overseas trade in textiles, spices, and precious stones. His reign is the counterexample to every other intolerant ruler in this period. (p. 149)

### Aurangzeb — Mughal Emperor (r. 1658–1707)
Shah Jahan's son. Inherited an empire weakened by corruption, then made it worse. Reversed Akbar's religious tolerance by imposing the *jizya* (poll tax on non-Muslims), persecuting Sikhs and Hindus, and expanding south — overextending the empire, draining the treasury, and triggering constant rebellions. After his death in 1707, the empire rapidly fragmented, allowing British and French economic penetration to accelerate. (pp. 150–151)

### Ivan IV (Ivan the Terrible) — Russian Tsar (r. 1547–1584)
First Russian ruler to formally adopt the title of **tsar** (1547). Immediately set about expanding Russia's borders eastward: took control of the Kazan, Astrakhan, and Siberian khanates from Golden Horde descendants. Authorized the Stroganov merchant family to hire Cossack warriors to push further east. His reign began Russia's transformation from a principality into a transcontinental empire stretching to the Pacific. (p. 144)

### Emperor Kangxi — Qing China (r. 1661–1722)
One of China's longest-reigning emperors. Presided over the Qing golden age: expanded into Taiwan, Mongolia, and Central Asia; imposed a protectorate over Tibet. His reign represents the high point of Qing power and stability. (p. 145)

### Emperor Qianlong — Qing China (r. 1736–1796)
Poet, calligrapher, and military expansionist. Extended Chinese territory into Xinjiang (with mass killings of Uighur people) and exerted tributary authority over Nepal. Famously rebuffed British trade requests by telling King George III that China had no need for British manufactured goods. His later reign saw bureaucratic corruption and the White Lotus Rebellion. (pp. 145–146)

### Tokugawa Ieyasu — Japanese Shogun (r. 1603–1616)
In 1603, declared shogun and established the Tokugawa Shogunate (Edo period), ending decades of Japanese civil war. Centralized military rule in Japan and initiated a long era of stability and controlled isolation from foreign influence. (p. 145, *Key Dates*)

## Key States & Empires

### Ottoman Empire (c. 1299–1922)
**Location:** Anatolia (modern Turkey), Balkans, Middle East, North Africa
**Religion:** Sunni Islam
**Key feature:** Controlled the Bosporus Strait — the only waterway between the Aegean and Black Seas — making Istanbul a critical hub for East-West trade. The most enduring of the Gunpowder Empires; the only one to last into the 20th century. Defeated in WWI and formally dissolved in 1922.

### Safavid Empire (1501–1736)
**Location:** Persia (modern Iran), parts of Iraq and the Caucasus
**Religion:** Shia Islam (state religion — unique among Islamic empires)
**Key feature:** The Sunni-Shia divide defined Safavid foreign policy. Constant wars with the Sunni Ottomans over both religion and overland trade routes made the Safavids perpetually vulnerable on their western flank. Collapsed rapidly after 1722.

### Mughal Empire (1526–1857)
**Location:** Indian subcontinent (present-day India, Pakistan, Bangladesh)
**Religion:** Sunni Islam, governing a predominantly Hindu population
**Key feature:** The only Gunpowder Empire ruling a non-Muslim majority. This made religious policy — tolerance under Akbar, persecution under Aurangzeb — the single most important variable in the empire's success or failure.

### Qing Dynasty — China (1644–1912)
**Location:** East Asia, expanding into Manchuria, Mongolia, Central Asia, Tibet
**Religion:** Varied; rulers Buddhist/shamanist, population mostly Confucian/Buddhist
**Key feature:** A foreign ([[Concept Notes/Manchu\|Manchu]]) dynasty that adopted Chinese governance traditions while maintaining ethnic distinction. Expanded Chinese territory more than any previous dynasty; eventually fell to internal rebellion and European imperialism in the 19th century.

### Russia (Muscovite/Tsarist)
**Location:** Eastern Europe, expanding across Siberia to the Pacific
**Religion:** Eastern Orthodox Christianity
**Key feature:** Unlike the Islamic Gunpowder Empires, Russia's expansion was eastward (into Siberia) rather than toward Europe's core. Russia's eventual military modernization along European lines allowed it to survive into the modern era as the others collapsed.

## Key Movements

### Rise of New Monarchies (Europe, c. 1450–1600)
A political movement across Western Europe in which rulers centralized state power — controlling taxes, armies, and religion — at the expense of feudal nobility and the church. The Tudors (England), Valois (France), and Ferdinand/Isabella (Spain) are prime examples. This centralization drive laid the groundwork for European overseas expansion and colonization (Unit 4).

### Manchu Conquest of China (1644)
The Manchu people of northeastern China swept into the [[Concept Notes/Ming Dynasty\|Ming Dynasty]], which had been weakened by internal corruption and peasant rebellions. The Manchu established the [[Concept Notes/Qing Dynasty\|Qing Dynasty]] and governed China for over 250 years. Their success required both military strength and political flexibility — adopting Chinese Confucian governance while maintaining Manchu ethnic identity and traditions.

### Sunni-Shia Conflict (ongoing from 1501)
When Shah Ismail I declared Iran Shia, he created a fault line that defined the geopolitics of Southwest Asia for centuries. The Ottoman-Safavid wars were fought simultaneously as religious conflicts and as competition for overland trade routes. The hostility persists in the present-day tensions between Iran (Shia majority) and surrounding Sunni-majority states, notably in Iraq.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to compare **two** of these empires. The most common pairings are: Ottoman vs. Mughal (both Islamic, different religious tolerance policies), Qing vs. Ottoman (different approaches to governing multiethnic populations), and Mughal Akbar vs. Aurangzeb (internal continuity/change). Always anchor your comparison with a specific ruler, policy, and outcome.
