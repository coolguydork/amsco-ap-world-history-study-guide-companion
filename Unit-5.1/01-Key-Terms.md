# Key Terms — Unit 5.1: The Enlightenment

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Enlightenment** — An 18th-century intellectual movement that applied reason and scientific thinking to questions of politics, society, and religion. (It provided the ideological foundation for the [[Atlantic Revolutions]] of the late 18th–early 19th centuries.)

2. **Natural rights** — Rights that, according to Enlightenment thinkers, all human beings possess simply by virtue of being human — including life, liberty, and property (Locke) or liberty, equality, and fraternity (French Revolution). (These ideas directly challenged hereditary aristocracy and absolute monarchy.)

3. **Social contract** — The idea, developed by [[Locke]] and [[Rousseau]], that legitimate government is based on the consent of the governed and that citizens surrender some freedoms in exchange for government protection of their rights. (Provided the philosophical basis for popular sovereignty and constitutional government.)

4. **Popular sovereignty** — The principle that political authority derives from the people, not from God or hereditary right. (Directly challenged divine right monarchy; embedded in the U.S. Constitution, French Declaration of the Rights of Man, and Latin American independence documents.)

5. **Separation of powers** — [[Montesquieu]]'s principle that government power should be divided among executive, legislative, and judicial branches to prevent tyranny. (The United States Constitution explicitly adopted this framework in 1787.)

6. **John Locke (1632–1704)** — English philosopher who argued that humans possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed; if government violates these rights, citizens have the right to revolt. (His ideas directly inspired the American Declaration of Independence.)

7. **Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778)** — French-Swiss philosopher who argued for the "general will" — the collective well-being of the citizenry — as the basis for political authority; more radical than Locke in his critique of private property and social inequality. (His ideas influenced both the French Revolution and later nationalist movements.)

8. **Voltaire (1694–1778)** — French writer and philosopher best known for his sharp criticism of religious intolerance, superstition, and arbitrary government; advocated freedom of speech and religion. (His wit and satire made Enlightenment ideas widely accessible; he represents the *philosophe* tradition of public intellectual engagement.)

9. **Baron de Montesquieu (1689–1755)** — French political philosopher who analyzed the separation of powers in *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) and studied how environment and culture shape political systems. (His comparative method was innovative; his work influenced both the U.S. Constitution and early comparative politics.)

10. **Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797)** — British writer who argued in *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) that Enlightenment principles of reason and natural rights must logically extend to women. (She is the founding figure of Western feminism and a key example of Enlightenment contradictions: most male thinkers excluded women from "natural rights.")

11. **Adam Smith (1723–1790)** — Scottish economist whose *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued that free markets, guided by the "invisible hand" of self-interest, produce greater prosperity than mercantilist regulation. (Provided the intellectual foundation for laissez-faire capitalism and free trade ideology.)

12. **Philosophes** — French Enlightenment intellectuals who wrote for the *Encyclopédie* and debated ideas in salons and coffeehouses; they popularized Enlightenment ideas for a broad reading public. (The *Encyclopédie*, edited by Denis Diderot, was the great collective project of the French Enlightenment.)

13. **Deism** — The belief that God created the universe and its natural laws but does not intervene in human affairs; a common Enlightenment religious position that valued reason over revelation. (Many American Founding Fathers — Jefferson, Franklin, Washington — were deists.)

14. **Laissez-faire** — French for "let it be"; the economic doctrine that government should not interfere in the market, allowing supply and demand to regulate prices and production naturally. (Adam Smith's idea; later taken up by industrial capitalists to resist labor regulations.)

15. **Scientific Revolution** — The 17th-century intellectual transformation, associated with Copernicus, Galileo, Newton, and Descartes, that established systematic observation and reason as the basis of knowledge. (The Enlightenment applied the Scientific Revolution's methods to human society and politics.)

16. **Public sphere** — The realm of coffeehouses, salons, newspapers, and print culture in which Enlightenment ideas were debated by educated citizens outside the court or church. (Habermas's concept; explains *how* Enlightenment ideas spread, not just what they were.)

17. **Salons** — Gatherings hosted primarily by elite women (*salonnières*) in Paris where philosophers, writers, and aristocrats debated new ideas. (Women like Madame du Deffand and Marie Thérèse Rodet Geoffrin played crucial roles as intellectual organizers, even though women were excluded from most Enlightenment political rights.)

18. ***Encyclopédie*** — A massive, 28-volume reference work edited by Denis Diderot (1751–1772) that compiled Enlightenment knowledge and implicitly challenged Church and state authority. (It was censored by the French government; its subversive impact illustrates how knowledge itself became politically dangerous.)

> [!exam-tip]
> On AP stimulus questions, you are likely to see passages from **Locke**, **Rousseau**, or the **Declaration of Independence**. For each, be ready to identify: (1) the core Enlightenment concept illustrated, (2) which revolution or political change it most directly inspired, and (3) who was *excluded* from its promises.
