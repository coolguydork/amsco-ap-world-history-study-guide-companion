# Unit 5.1: The Enlightenment

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

## AP Exam Period
**Period 3: c. 1750–1900**

## Summary

The Enlightenment was an 18th-century intellectual movement centered in Western Europe — especially France, Britain, and Scotland — that applied the methods of the Scientific Revolution to questions of politics, society, economics, and religion. Enlightenment thinkers argued that reason, rather than tradition, scripture, or royal authority, should be the foundation of government and social organization. Their core ideas — natural rights, popular sovereignty, the social contract, separation of powers, religious toleration, and free-market economics — provided the intellectual framework for the Atlantic Revolutions that began in 1776. The Enlightenment did not emerge from a vacuum: it built on Renaissance humanism, the Scientific Revolution of the 17th century (Newton, Descartes), and the commercial world of coffeehouses, salons, and print culture that made new ideas portable and debatable. Its contradictions are as historically significant as its ideals: most Enlightenment thinkers excluded women, enslaved people, and colonized peoples from the natural rights they championed, contradictions that future revolutionaries — especially in Haiti — would expose and exploit.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain the intellectual origins and core principles of the Enlightenment.
- Identify key Enlightenment thinkers and their contributions to political and economic thought.
- Analyze how Enlightenment ideas challenged existing political and social structures.
- Evaluate the limits and contradictions of Enlightenment universalism.

## AP Key Concepts

- **KC-5.3.I** — Enlightenment ideas about reason, natural rights, and the social contract inspired resistance to existing political authorities and established the ideological foundation for new political systems.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests Enlightenment ideas through **stimulus-based MCQs** — a passage from Locke, Rousseau, or Wollstonecraft followed by questions about what political change it supports. Always connect the idea directly to a specific revolution or political document (Declaration of Independence, Declaration of the Rights of Man, Haitian Constitution).
