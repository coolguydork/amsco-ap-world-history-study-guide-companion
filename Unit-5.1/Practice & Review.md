## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO p. 282 | PDF p. 346*

**Questions 1–3** refer to the following passage:

> "Who made man the exclusive judge, if women partake with him the gift of reason? In this style, argue tyrants of every denomination, from the weak king to the weak father of a family; they are all eager to crush reason; yet always assert that they usurp its throne only to be useful. Do you not act a similar part, when you force all women, by denying them civil and political rights, to remain immured [confined against their will] in their families groping in the dark? For surely, sir, you will not assert that a duty can be binding which is not founded on reason. . . Let there be, then, no coercion established in society, and the common law of gravity prevailing, the sexes will fall into their proper places. And now, that more equitable laws are forming your citizens, marriage may become more sacred, your young may choose wives from motives of affection, and your maidens allow love to root out vanity."
>
> — Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Women*, Dedicatory letter to Talleyrand of France, 1792

**1.** Wollstonecraft's main goal in this passage is to

A) secure inheritance rights for surviving wives

B) secure female equality with males

C) allow men and women to marry based on love

D) encourage Britain to support the bourgeoisie in the French Revolution

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Wollstonecraft's entire argument rests on the claim that women possess reason equally with men, and therefore deserve equal civil and political rights — not just legal protections or affective freedoms. Choice A is too narrow (inheritance is not mentioned). Choice C mistakes a secondary point (equitable marriage) for the central argument. Choice D is irrelevant to the passage's content.

---

**2.** Which of the following writers would LEAST likely support the goals of Mary Wollstonecraft?

A) A conservative

B) A utopian socialist

C) A classical liberal

D) A Marxist

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Conservatives championed traditional institutions, including the traditional subordination of women to family and domestic roles. They would view Wollstonecraft's demands as a dangerous attack on a natural social order. Utopian socialists (B) generally favored gender equality in their ideal communities. Classical liberals (C) believed in natural rights, which Wollstonecraft explicitly invoked — many supported women's rights in principle. Marxists (D) critiqued capitalist society's gender hierarchies as class-based and envisioned greater equality. The conservative would be the most resistant.

---

**3.** What part of the excerpt is connected most closely to the ideals of the Enlightenment?

A) its reference to equality in marriage

B) its mention of equitable laws

C) its appeal to reason

D) its rejection of tyrants

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The defining feature of Enlightenment thought is the primacy of **reason** as the basis for all legitimate authority and social arrangements. Wollstonecraft's central argument — "who made man the exclusive judge, if women partake with him the gift of reason?" — directly applies the Enlightenment method: reason is the universal standard; therefore its application cannot be limited by sex. The other elements (marriage equality, equitable laws, rejection of tyranny) are important, but they flow from the appeal to reason, which is the Enlightenment's core contribution.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 283–284 | PDF p. 347*

**SAQ 1** — Primary Source Analysis (Abigail Adams, 1775)

> "I wish I knew what mighty things were fabricating. If a form of government is to be established here, what one will be assumed? . . . I am more and more convinced that man is a dangerous creature; and that power, whether vested in many or a few, is ever grasping. . . . How shall we be governed so as to retain our liberties? . . . When I consider these things, and the prejudices of people in favor of ancient customs and regulations, I feel anxious for the fate of our monarchy or democracy, or whatever is to take place."
>
> — Abigail Adams, letter to John Adams, November 1775

**A.** Identify ONE way in which the passage reflects a philosophical debate in the late 18th or early 19th centuries.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity/Change
> The passage reflects the Enlightenment debate over the nature of government and human nature. Adams's anxiety about power being "ever grasping" echoes both Hobbes (humans are naturally dangerous, requiring strong restraint) and Montesquieu (power must be checked by institutional structures). Her uncertainty about "monarchy or democracy" shows awareness of the core political debate of the era: whether government should rest on tradition and authority or on popular consent and constitutional limits.

**B.** Explain ONE way in which Adams's ideas support or reject the ideas of Thomas Hobbes.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Adams *supports* Hobbes's pessimistic view of human nature. She states that "man is a dangerous creature" and that power "whether vested in many or a few, is ever grasping" — directly echoing Hobbes's argument that people naturally seek power and require strong institutional constraints to prevent chaos. However, she *differs* from Hobbes in that she does not conclude that a powerful sovereign is the solution; instead, she worries about how to "retain our liberties" — implying that government itself must also be checked.

**C.** Explain ONE way in which Adams's ideas support or reject the ideas of John Locke.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Adams *supports* Locke's argument that government must be constrained to protect individual liberties. Her question — "How shall we be governed so as to retain our liberties?" — reflects Locke's core claim that the purpose of government is to protect natural rights, not to accumulate power. Her concern that "ancient customs and regulations" may override rational constitutional design also aligns with Locke's argument that tradition is not a sufficient basis for political authority — reason and natural rights are the proper standard.

**SAQ 2** — Broader Enlightenment Comparison

**A.** Identify ONE way in which the ideas of the Renaissance impacted the ideas of the Enlightenment.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity/Change
> Renaissance humanism contributed the idea that human beings were capable of rational self-improvement and that secular inquiry — not just theological study — was a valid form of knowledge. This humanist confidence in human capacity became a foundation for Enlightenment thinkers who argued that reason, not divine authority, should govern political and social life.

**B.** Explain ONE way in which Enlightenment thinkers in Britain and America were similar in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> British and American Enlightenment thinkers shared a commitment to constitutional government based on individual rights. John Locke (British) provided the theory of natural rights and the right to revolt; American founders like Jefferson and Madison applied those theories directly in the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution. Both traditions emphasized the protection of individual liberty against arbitrary government power, distinguishing British-American liberalism from the more radical popular sovereignty of French Rousseau-influenced thought.

**C.** Explain ONE way in which Enlightenment thinkers in Britain and America differed in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> British Enlightenment thought, particularly among figures like Edmund Burke and the Fabian Society, tended toward gradualism — reforming existing institutions through legal and parliamentary means rather than revolution. American Enlightenment-influenced thought was more willing to embrace revolutionary rupture (the Declaration of Independence explicitly justified overthrowing tyrannical government). Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) represents the British conservative Enlightenment tradition that valued order and tradition alongside rights — a perspective less influential in early American political culture.

## Think As a Historian: Describe an Argument

*Source: AMSCO pp. 283–284*

**Document Pair:** Talleyrand vs. Wollstonecraft on Women's Education

> **Talleyrand (1791):** "Let us bring up women, not to aspire to advantages which the Constitution denies them, but to know and appreciate those which it guarantees them . . . Men are destined to live on the stage of the world. A public education suits them. . . . The paternal home is better for the education of women."

> **Wollstonecraft (1792):** "Contending for the rights of women, my main argument is built on this simple principle, that if she be not prepared by education to become the companion of man, she will stop the progress of knowledge . . . If children are to be educated to understand the true principle of patriotism, their mother must be a patriot . . . [I] earnestly wish to see woman placed in a station in which she would advance, instead of retarding, the progress of those glorious principles that give a substance to morality."

**Argument Analysis:**

Talleyrand uses **deductive reasoning**: his premise is that the sexes have different natural roles (men in the public world, women in the home), and therefore different educational needs. His conclusion — private domestic education for women — follows from this premise. The argument accepts the constitutional exclusion of women as given and argues backward to justify a different educational track.

Wollstonecraft uses **inductive reasoning and analogy**: she argues from observable outcomes (uneducated mothers cannot raise educated, patriotic children; society is therefore harmed by women's exclusion) to the conclusion that women must receive equal education. She also uses an Enlightenment-style appeal to universal principles: *reason* is the standard, and women possess reason equally with men, therefore their exclusion is irrational. Her argument directly inverts Talleyrand's — rather than accepting exclusion as a premise, she treats it as the problem to be solved.

> [!exam-tip]
> The SAQ "describe an argument" skill requires you to identify: (1) the author's claim, (2) the type of reasoning used (deductive, inductive, analogy), and (3) the evidence or premises supporting the claim. Practice applying these labels to primary sources — the AP exam will ask you to "describe" an argument in one sentence and "explain" it in 3–5 sentences.

## Quick Review: Essential Question

**How did the Enlightenment shape the intellectual and ideological thinking that affected reform and revolution after 1750?**

The Enlightenment replaced divine authority with **reason** as the standard for legitimate government. Its core ideas — natural rights (Locke), popular sovereignty (Rousseau), separation of powers (Montesquieu), and free markets (Smith) — gave revolutionary leaders the vocabulary and justification for challenging monarchies. These ideas also powered reform movements in feminism (Wollstonecraft, Seneca Falls), abolitionism (natural rights applied to enslaved people and serfs), and nationalism (people sharing culture should govern themselves). The Enlightenment was not just European — its ideas traveled through print culture, educated elites, and revolutionary struggle to reshape politics across the Atlantic world and beyond.
