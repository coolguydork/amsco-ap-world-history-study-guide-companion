# Connections

**Topic 5.1 — The Enlightenment | AMSCO pp. 275–284**

---

## Builds On

**Topic 4.6 — Internal & External Challenges to State Power**
The [[Concept Notes/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]] (covered in 4.5–4.6) had already fractured the Catholic Church's monopoly on truth and morality, demonstrating that established authority could be challenged on principled grounds. The political instability of the 17th century (English Civil War, Thirty Years' War) created the climate in which Hobbes and Locke wrote their social contract theories. Enlightenment thought inherited the Reformation's impulse to question authority — but applied reason, not scripture, as the alternative standard. See [[Unit-4.6/Internal & External Challenges to State Power Overview|Internal & External Challenges to State Power Overview]].

**Topic 1.6 — Medieval Europe and Scholasticism**
Medieval scholasticism (Thomas Aquinas, the university tradition) preserved and systematized rational inquiry within a theological framework. The Enlightenment secularized this tradition — keeping the commitment to reason and argument while removing the subordination to church authority. European intellectual life thus shows a long arc: Scholasticism → Renaissance Humanism → Scientific Revolution → Enlightenment. See [[Unit-1.6/Medieval Europe Overview|Medieval Europe Overview]].

**Topic 4.5 — Maritime Empires Develop**
Colonial wealth from Atlantic trade funded European courts, academies, and publishing houses — the institutional infrastructure of Enlightenment intellectual life. At the same time, colonial encounters with diverse cultures and peoples prompted Europeans to question whether their own cultural and political arrangements were natural or merely traditional. See [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]].

**Topic 1.2 — Dar al-Islam (Islamic Golden Age)**
The Islamic Golden Age (8th–13th c.) preserved, translated, and extended Greek philosophy, mathematics, and science — especially Aristotle, which reached medieval European scholars via Arabic translations. Without this preservation, the Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment would have lacked key intellectual raw material. See [[Unit-1.2/Dar al-Islam Overview|Dar al-Islam Overview]].

## Leads To

**Topic 5.2 — Nationalism and Revolutions**
Enlightenment ideas are the direct intellectual cause of the American Revolution (Locke → natural rights, Montesquieu → separation of powers), the French Revolution (Rousseau → popular sovereignty, General Will), and the Haitian Revolution (universal natural rights applied to enslaved Africans). Nationalism — the idea that a shared culture should mean shared self-governance — is also an Enlightenment product. Topic 5.2 is the political consequence of Topic 5.1.

**Topic 5.7 — Economic Developments and Innovations**
Adam Smith's laissez-faire capitalism (Topic 5.1) becomes the dominant economic framework for industrializing nations in Topic 5.7. The ideological contest between capitalism, utopian socialism, and later Marxist socialism (which begins here) runs through Topics 5.7–5.8.

**Topic 6.1 — Imperialism**
Enlightenment ideas were used in contradictory ways in the age of high imperialism: European powers cited their "civilizing mission" (reason, progress, science) as justification for colonizing non-European peoples; at the same time, colonized peoples used Enlightenment language of natural rights and self-determination to argue against colonialism. This double-edged legacy is essential for DBQ and LEQ analysis.

## Compare With

**Topic 1.1 — East Asia and Confucianism**
Both Confucianism and Enlightenment thought prioritize ethical governance and educated leadership. But they diverge fundamentally: Confucianism is hierarchical (ruler–subject, parent–child relationships as given), emphasizes social harmony over individual rights, and grounds authority in moral virtue rather than popular consent. The Enlightenment's emphasis on individual natural rights and the right to revolt would have been deeply alien to Confucian political thought. See [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|East Asia Overview]].

**Topic 1.2 — Islamic Golden Age**
Both were periods of intense intellectual synthesis and production. But the Islamic Golden Age occurred within a theocratic framework (the caliphate), while the Enlightenment explicitly moved toward secular political authority. The Islamic Golden Age's contributions were scientific and philosophical; the Enlightenment applied those tools to challenge religious authority in governance.

## AP Threads Running Through This Topic

**Natural rights → Revolution:** Locke's natural rights become the vocabulary of every Atlantic revolution in Unit 5. Track this thread forward.

**Social contract → Constitutional governance:** The social contract idea (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) produces constitutionalism — the idea that government's powers are defined and limited by written law. This thread runs through Topics 5.2, 6.1, and the 20th-century decolonization movements.

**Reason vs. tradition:** The core Enlightenment tension between rational reform and traditional authority recurs across AP World History. It appears in the Protestant Reformation (Unit 4), the Enlightenment (Unit 5), imperialism debates (Unit 6), and 20th-century ideological conflicts (Unit 9).

**Limits of universalism:** Every Enlightenment proclamation of universal rights ran up against specific exclusions — by gender, race, and class. Track how these exclusions were challenged over time: feminism (Topic 5.1), abolition (Topic 5.1–5.2), decolonization (Topics 6–9).
