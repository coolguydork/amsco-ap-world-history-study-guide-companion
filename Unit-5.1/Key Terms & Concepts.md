# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 5.1 — The Enlightenment | AMSCO pp. 275–284**

---

**Enlightenment**
The 18th-century intellectual movement that emphasized reason, individualism, and natural law over tradition and religious authority. Growing from the Scientific Revolution and Renaissance humanism, it produced ideas about natural rights, popular sovereignty, and constitutional government that fueled Atlantic-world revolutions. Its influence extended well beyond Europe.

**Empiricism**
The belief that knowledge derives from *observed* experience, including through experiments — not from tradition or religious authority. Associated with Francis Bacon (17th c.). Empiricism was the scientific method applied to all fields of inquiry, underpinning the Enlightenment's faith in reason.

**Social contract**
The idea that government's legitimacy rests on an agreement between rulers and the governed. Hobbes used it to justify strong authority; Locke used it to justify the right to revolt when government violates natural rights; Rousseau used it to articulate the General Will and popular sovereignty.

**Natural rights**
Rights that people possess by nature, prior to any government — most famously articulated by Locke as life, liberty, and property. These rights cannot be legitimately taken away by government; if they are, citizens may revolt. Became foundational to American and French revolutionary documents.

**Tabula rasa (blank slate)**
Locke's argument (*An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, c. 1690) that the mind begins as a blank slate, shaped entirely by experience and education rather than by birth or ancestry. Radically egalitarian: it implied that circumstances, not heredity, determined ability and worth.

**Philosophes**
18th-century French-language writers who popularized Enlightenment ideas for a broad reading public, including Montesquieu, Voltaire, Rousseau, and (by extension) figures like Jefferson, Franklin, and Adam Smith. They wrote for mass audiences — encyclopedias, novels, pamphlets — not just for academic specialists.

**Separation of powers**
Montesquieu's idea (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748) that government should be divided into executive, legislative, and judicial branches that check each other. Directly shaped the U.S. Constitution and French revolutionary constitutions.

**General Will**
Rousseau's concept (*The Social Contract*, 1762) of the collective expression of a free citizenry. A sovereign government's obligation was to carry out the General Will rather than the will of a monarch or aristocracy. Provided ideological fuel for the French Revolution's claim to govern in the name of "the people."

**Laissez-faire**
French for "leave alone." Adam Smith's argument that governments should minimize interference in economic decisions. If individuals pursue their own interests, the "invisible hand" of the market guides outcomes beneficial to all. Anti-[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilist]]; foundation of classical economic liberalism.

**Capitalism**
An economic system in which the means of production (factories, land, natural resources) are privately owned and operated for profit. Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) provided its intellectual foundation. Contrasts with socialism (worker/public ownership) and mercantilism (state-directed trade for national benefit).

**Deism**
The belief that a divinity created and set natural laws in motion but does not intervene in the day-to-day world. Deists compared God to a watchmaker. They emphasized scientific inquiry over scripture. Associated with Voltaire, Jefferson, Franklin, and Thomas Paine.

**Liberalism (classical liberalism)**
A political ideology favoring natural rights, constitutional government, laissez-faire economics, religious toleration, and reduced state spending on armies and established churches. In 19th-century Britain, classical liberals backed parliamentary Reform Bills (1832, 1867, 1884) that expanded male suffrage.

**Conservatism**
A political ideology favoring traditional institutions and gradual, practical change over ideological blueprints. Popular among European ruling classes after the upheavals of the French Revolution. Conservatives viewed rapid social engineering with deep suspicion.

**Nationalism**
The belief that people who share a language, culture, and history should live in an independent, self-governing nation-state. A major destabilizing force for multiethnic empires (Habsburg, Ottoman, Russian). Became a primary driver of revolution and warfare in the 19th century (see Topic 5.2).

**Socialism**
A political and economic theory advocating public or worker ownership of the means of production, in contrast to private capitalist ownership. Responded to the poverty and inequality produced by industrialization. Branches include utopian socialism (harmonious ideal communities) and Marxist (scientific) socialism.

**Utopian socialism**
19th-century socialist thought that envisioned ideal model communities rather than class struggle. Key figures: Henri de Saint-Simon (rational technocratic management), Charles Fourier (encouraging human passions to make labor enjoyable), and Robert Owen (intentional communities like New Lanark and New Harmony). Later eclipsed by Marxist socialism.

**Fabian Society**
A late-19th-century British socialist organization that advocated gradual reform through parliamentary means rather than revolution. Notable members included H. G. Wells, Virginia Woolf, and George Bernard Shaw. Influenced the development of the British Labour Party.

**Feminism**
The movement for women's rights and equality, grounded in Enlightenment logic: if natural rights apply to all humans, they must apply to women as well. Key milestones: de Gouges's *Declaration of the Rights of Woman* (1791), Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792), and the Seneca Falls Convention (1848).

**Abolitionism**
The movement to end the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] and slavery. Grew from Enlightenment arguments about natural rights applied to enslaved people. Denmark (1807), Britain (1807), and the U.S. (1808) banned the slave trade first; slavery was abolished across most of the Americas within decades. Brazil was last (1888).

**Anti-Semitism**
Hostility toward Jewish people. A persistent feature of European history that intensified in the late 19th century despite (or alongside) Enlightenment ideals. The Dreyfus Affair (1894) exposed anti-Semitism even in "enlightened" France, galvanizing Zionism.

**Zionism**
The nationalist movement for the re-establishment of a Jewish homeland in Palestine (the historic homeland of the Jewish people). Led by Theodor Herzl in the late 19th century. A response to centuries of anti-Semitism and pogroms. The modern state of Israel was founded in 1948.

**Dreyfus Affair**
A scandal in France (1894–1906) in which Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish military officer, was wrongly convicted of treason based on forged evidence. The case exposed virulent anti-Semitism in France and strengthened the Zionist movement; Theodor Herzl covered the trial as a journalist.

**Pogrom**
Organized, often state-tolerated, violent attacks on Jewish communities in Europe, especially in the Russian Empire. Pogroms drove many Jews to emigrate and fueled support for Zionism.

**Declaration of Sentiments**
Document issued at the Seneca Falls Convention (1848) by Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton. Modeled on the Declaration of Independence, it declared "All men and women are created equal" and demanded women's suffrage, property rights, and legal equality.
