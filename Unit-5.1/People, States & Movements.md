# People, States & Movements

**Topic 5.1 — The Enlightenment | AMSCO pp. 275–284**

## Intellectual Founders

**Francis Bacon (1561–1626)**
English philosopher who championed **empiricism** — the idea that knowledge derives from sensed experience and experiment rather than from tradition or scripture. Bacon insisted that natural inquiry must begin with observable data. His method became the epistemological foundation of the Enlightenment: if you can observe and test, you can know.

**Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679)**
English political philosopher, author of *Leviathan* (1651). Argued that human beings in a "state of nature" lived in perpetual war — life was "nasty, brutish, and short." The **social contract** gave people safety: they surrendered freedom to a powerful sovereign in exchange for law and order. Hobbes's pessimism about human nature led him to justify strong, centralized (even authoritarian) government. His ideas contrast sharply with Locke's more optimistic view.

**John Locke (1632–1704)**
English philosopher; author of *Two Treatises of Government* (c. 1690) and *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (c. 1690). The single most influential Enlightenment thinker for Atlantic-world revolutionaries. Key ideas:
- **Natural rights** — life, liberty, and property, which no government can legitimately take away
- **Right to revolt** — citizens may overthrow a government that violates natural rights
- **Tabula rasa** — the mind begins as a blank slate shaped by experience and education, not by birth

Locke's natural rights became the foundation for the American Declaration of Independence (Jefferson paraphrased Locke directly) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man.

**Baron Montesquieu (1689–1755)**
French political philosopher; author of *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748). Admired Britain's constitutional monarchy and argued for **separation of powers** into executive, legislative, and judicial branches — each checking the others. His model was adopted almost verbatim in the U.S. Constitution. He also influenced constitutional framers in France, Spain, and Latin America.

**Voltaire (1694–1778)**
Pen name of François-Marie Arouet; French writer and satirist. Best known for *Candide* (1762) and his articles in Diderot's *Encyclopédie*. Famous for advocating **religious tolerance** and civil liberties. Exiled to England for three years, he returned to France with deep admiration for constitutional monarchy. He corresponded with Frederick the Great (Prussia) and Catherine the Great (Russia), exemplifying the "enlightened despot" phenomenon. His ideas on religious liberty influenced the U.S. Constitution's First Amendment.

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778)**
French-Swiss philosopher; author of *Emile, or On Education* (1762) and *The Social Contract* (1762). Extended the social contract idea by arguing that the sovereign's obligation was to carry out the **General Will** of the people — popular sovereignty. Optimistic about human nature (people are good; corrupt institutions make them bad). His ideas inspired the French Revolution's radical phase, including the Jacobins. More egalitarian and democratic than Voltaire or Montesquieu.

**Adam Smith (1723–1790)**
Scottish economist and philosopher; author of *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). The founding theorist of **capitalism** and **laissez-faire** economics. Argued that free markets, guided by the "invisible hand," allocate resources more efficiently than mercantilist state control. His ideas shaped 19th-century economic policy across the industrializing world and remain foundational to market economics today.

**Thomas Paine (1737–1809)**
Anglo-American political activist and writer. *Common Sense* (1776) argued for American independence from Britain in plain language accessible to ordinary people — a masterpiece of political persuasion. Later, *The Age of Reason* (1794) made him a militant defender of **deism** and a sharp critic of organized religion, costing him much of his earlier popularity. Paine exemplified how Enlightenment ideas crossed the Atlantic.

## Feminist Reformers

**Olympe de Gouges (1748–1793)**
French feminist and political activist. In 1791, as France adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man, she issued a *Declaration of the Rights of Woman*, demanding that women be included in Enlightenment ideals of rights and citizenship. Executed during the Reign of Terror — the very revolution she championed turned against her. A powerful symbol of the limits of Enlightenment universalism in practice.

**Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797)**
British feminist author of *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792). Argued that denying women equal education was irrational — if women were to raise patriotic citizens, they needed to be educated themselves. She directly addressed Talleyrand, who had argued that women's education should be confined to the home. The mother of novelist Mary Shelley (*Frankenstein*). Her work became foundational to all subsequent feminist movements.

**Lucretia Mott (1793–1880) and Elizabeth Cady Stanton (1815–1902)**
American feminist activists who organized the **Seneca Falls Convention** (1848) in New York. Their "Declaration of Sentiments" — modeled explicitly on Jefferson's Declaration of Independence — declared "All men and women are created equal" and demanded women's suffrage, property rights, and legal equality. The Seneca Falls Convention is the landmark event in American women's rights history.

## Abolitionist Movements

The abolition of the slave trade and serfdom proceeded unevenly across the Atlantic world. Key milestones driven by Enlightenment natural-rights arguments:
- **Denmark** banned the slave trade in 1807 (effective from then)
- **Britain** banned the slave trade in 1807
- **United States** banned the slave trade in 1808
- **Brazil** was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery — 1888

For European serfdom: **Queen Elizabeth I** abolished serfdom in England (1574); the French Revolution abolished feudal obligations (1789); **Tsar Alexander II** emancipated Russian serfs in **1861** — the largest single emancipation in history, freeing 23 million people.

## Zionist Movement

**Theodor Herzl (1860–1904)**
Austro-Hungarian Jewish journalist who founded modern **Zionism** after covering the Dreyfus Affair in France. Concluded that Jews could never be safe in Europe and needed a homeland of their own. Organized the First Zionist Congress (Basel, 1897) and lobbied European powers and the Ottoman Empire. His movement culminated decades later in the founding of Israel (1948).

**Alfred Dreyfus (1859–1935)**
French Jewish military officer wrongly convicted of treason in 1894 based on forged documents. The **Dreyfus Affair** exposed the depth of anti-Semitism in France and galvanized support for Zionism across Europe. Dreyfus was eventually pardoned (1906).

## Key States and Their Roles

| State | Enlightenment Role |
|-------|-------------------|
| France | Philosophes (Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu, Diderot); French Revolution; feminist figures (de Gouges) |
| Britain | Empiricism (Bacon); social contract (Hobbes, Locke); constitutional monarchy admired by Montesquieu and Voltaire; classical liberalism; abolitionism |
| Scotland / Britain | Adam Smith; laissez-faire economics; Robert Owen's utopian communities |
| Prussia | Frederick the Great — "enlightened despot" who corresponded with Voltaire |
| Russia | Catherine the Great — "enlightened despot"; abolished serfdom delayed until Alexander II (1861) |
| United States | Applied Enlightenment most directly in founding documents (Declaration of Independence, Constitution); Seneca Falls movement |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently pairs primary sources from these thinkers with questions asking you to identify their philosophical tradition or connect their ideas to specific events. Know Locke → natural rights → Declaration of Independence; Montesquieu → separation of powers → U.S. Constitution; Rousseau → General Will → French Revolution. These are the three most tested connections.

> [!tip]
> A useful contrast for comparison questions: Hobbes and Locke both used "social contract" but reached opposite conclusions — Hobbes justified strong government; Locke justified revolution. The French Revolution initially aligned with Locke's ideas, then shifted toward Rousseau's more radical popular sovereignty during the Terror.
