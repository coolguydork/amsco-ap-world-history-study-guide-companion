# Enlightenment Overview

**AP Themes:** Cultural Developments & Interactions (CDI) · Governance (GOV)
**Period:** c. 1750–c. 1900 | **AMSCO pp. 275–284**

## What Was the Enlightenment?

The Enlightenment was an 18th-century intellectual movement that emphasized **reason, individualism, and natural law** over tradition and religious authority. Growing out of the Scientific Revolution and Renaissance humanism, Enlightenment thinkers believed that applying rational inquiry to the social and political world could produce progress. While traditional religion did not disappear, it became less central to public life.

The Enlightenment gave rise to what historians call "the Age of Isms" — a flowering of competing ideologies (liberalism, conservatism, socialism, nationalism, feminism, abolitionism, Zionism) that would drive revolutions and reform movements across the Atlantic world and beyond.

## Intellectual Roots

The Enlightenment did not appear from nowhere. Three prior traditions fed directly into it:

**The Scientific Revolution** taught that the natural world operates according to discoverable laws — gravity, planetary motion, chemistry. Thinkers like Francis Bacon (1561–1626) pushed **empiricism**: the idea that knowledge comes from *observed* experience and experiment, not from tradition or scripture. This method could be applied to society just as readily as to physics.

**Renaissance Humanism** (15th–16th c.) shifted focus toward human achievement and secular inquiry, away from purely theological questions. Figures like Erasmus modeled how learned men could engage problems of ethics and governance through reason.

**Scholasticism** (medieval Europe, see [[Unit-1.6/Medieval Europe Overview|Medieval Europe Overview]]) had actually preserved and extended Greek rationalism — but within a framework subordinate to church authority. The Enlightenment freed reason from that subordination.

## Core Thinkers

### Hobbes and Locke: The Social Contract

**Thomas Hobbes** (*Leviathan*, 1651) argued that humans in a "state of nature" lived in perpetual war — life was "nasty, brutish, and short." The solution: a **social contract** whereby people surrendered some freedoms to a powerful central authority (a sovereign) in exchange for law and order. Hobbes's model justified strong, even authoritarian, government.

**John Locke** (*Two Treatises of Government*, c. 1690) agreed that government rested on a social contract, but drew opposite conclusions. People possess **natural rights** — life, liberty, and property — that government cannot legitimately take away. If a government violates these rights, citizens have the right, even the responsibility, to **revolt**. Locke also proposed in *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (c. 1690) that the mind begins as a "blank slate" (*tabula rasa*), shaped entirely by experience and education — a radical challenge to the belief that social rank was fixed by birth.

> [!exam-tip]
> Locke's natural rights — life, liberty, property — directly inspired both the American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789). Jefferson paraphrased Locke but changed "property" to "pursuit of happiness." Know this connection cold.

### The Philosophes

The **philosophes** were 18th-century French-language writers who popularized Enlightenment ideas for a broad reading public. Key figures:

**Baron Montesquieu** (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748) praised Britain's limited monarchy and argued for **separation of powers** into executive, legislative, and judicial branches — each checking the others. His model shaped both the U.S. Constitution and post-revolutionary French governments.

**Voltaire** (François-Marie Arouet, 1694–1778) championed **religious tolerance** and civil liberties. Exiled to England, he returned to France with admiration for its constitutional monarchy. His wit and his articles in Diderot's *Encyclopédie* reached across Europe; he corresponded with "enlightened despots" Catherine the Great and Frederick the Great.

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (*The Social Contract*, 1762) developed the concept of the **General Will** — the collective expression of a free people — and argued that a sovereign's obligation was to carry it out. He believed human nature was fundamentally good and that corrupt institutions were to blame for society's problems. His ideas became a touchstone for French revolutionaries.

### Adam Smith and Economic Enlightenment

**Adam Smith** (*The Wealth of Nations*, 1776) attacked [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilism]] and argued for **laissez-faire** ("leave alone") economics. He believed that if individuals pursued their own economic interests, an "invisible hand" would guide markets to produce outcomes beneficial to all. Smith's work provided the intellectual foundation for **capitalism** — private ownership of the means of production operated for profit. His ideas will resurface in **Topic 5.7** (Economic Developments & Innovations).

### Deism

Some Enlightenment thinkers concluded that God existed but did not intervene in the world. **Deists** compared the deity to a watchmaker who sets a mechanism in motion and then steps back — natural laws ran the universe, discoverable through reason rather than scripture. **Thomas Paine** (*The Age of Reason*, 1794) was a militant deist; his earlier *Common Sense* (1776) had made him a hero of the American Revolution, but his anti-church writings damaged his reputation.

## The Age of Isms

As industrialization intensified social problems — urban poverty, political exclusion, discrimination — Enlightenment principles generated a cluster of reform ideologies.

**Conservatism** rejected rapid change, favoring traditional institutions and practical experience over ideological blueprints. Popular among European ruling classes reacting to revolutionary upheaval.

**Utopian Socialism** envisioned ideal communities that could eliminate poverty and exploitation:
- **Henri de Saint-Simon** (France): scientists, engineers, and businesses leading a rational, efficient society; proposed the Suez Canal
- **Charles Fourier**: encouraged human "passions" to make work enjoyable; harmonious communal living over class struggle
- **Robert Owen** (Britain/U.S.): founded intentional communities in New Lanark, Scotland and New Harmony, Indiana; championed child education and communal property

Later in the 19th century, the **Fabian Society** (Britain) pursued gradual socialist reform through parliament, counting H. G. Wells, Virginia Woolf, and George Bernard Shaw among its members.

**Classical Liberalism** combined natural rights, constitutional government, laissez-faire economics, and reduced state spending on armies and churches. In Britain, classical liberals backed the Reform Bills of 1832, 1867, and 1884, which progressively expanded male suffrage.

**Feminism** applied Enlightenment logic to women's exclusion. **Olympe de Gouges** (France, 1791) issued a *Declaration of the Rights of Woman*, directly mirroring the Declaration of the Rights of Man — she was executed during the Reign of Terror. **Mary Wollstonecraft** (*A Vindication of the Rights of Women*, 1792) argued that women deserved equal education as essential to the progress of society. In 1848 at the **Seneca Falls Convention** (New York), **Lucretia Mott** and **Elizabeth Cady Stanton** issued a "Declaration of Sentiments" demanding women's suffrage, property rights, and legal equality.

**Abolitionism** extended rights arguments to enslaved people and serfs. Denmark (1807), Britain (1807), and the U.S. (1808) banned the slave trade first; slavery itself followed within decades in most of the Americas. Brazil was the last to abolish slavery, in 1888. In Europe, serfdom declined as economies industrialized — Alexander II abolished Russian serfdom in 1861, the largest single emancipation in history.

**Nationalism** — the belief that people who share a language and culture should live in an independent nation-state — threatened multiethnic empires across Europe and became a driver of both revolution and war in the 19th century (see **Topic 5.2**).

**Zionism** emerged in the late 19th century as many European Jews, facing centuries of **anti-Semitism** and **pogroms** (violent attacks on Jewish communities), concluded that safety required a homeland of their own. **Theodor Herzl** led the movement. The **Dreyfus Affair** (1894–1906) — in which a Jewish French military officer was wrongly convicted of treason on forged evidence — galvanized Zionist sentiment even in supposedly tolerant France. Zionists sought to re-establish a state in Palestine, then part of the Ottoman Empire. The modern state of Israel was founded in 1948.

## Broader Impact

The Enlightenment's political ideas reached far beyond Europe. They provided the vocabulary — natural rights, popular sovereignty, separation of powers — for revolutionaries in America, France, Haiti, and Latin America. They also supplied the ideological tools for both the defenders and the critics of European imperialism (see **Topic 6.1**).

> [!tip]
> The Enlightenment is a **cause** topic. On the AP exam, every revolution in Unit 5 (American, French, Haitian, Latin American) draws on it as a cause. Practice writing that causal link explicitly: "Enlightenment ideas of natural rights and popular sovereignty challenged monarchical authority, leading to…"

> [!warning]
> Do not conflate the Enlightenment with the Scientific Revolution. The Scientific Revolution (16th–17th c.) changed *how* people understood nature. The Enlightenment applied that *method* (reason, empiricism) to *society, politics, and economics*. The Enlightenment builds on the Scientific Revolution but is a distinct movement.
