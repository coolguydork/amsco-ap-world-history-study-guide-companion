# Topic 5.1 — The Enlightenment: Overview

**AP Unit:** 5 — Revolutions | **Period:** 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900) | **AMSCO:** pp. 275–281

**Essential Question:** How did the Enlightenment shape the intellectual and ideological thinking that affected reform and revolution after 1750?

---

## What Was the Enlightenment?

The Enlightenment was an 18th-century intellectual movement that prioritized **reason, individualism, and natural law** over tradition and religious authority. Growing out of the [[Concept Notes/Scientific Revolution|Scientific Revolution]] and Renaissance humanism, Enlightenment thinkers believed that applying reason to society would produce progress. These ideas planted the seeds of revolution across the Atlantic world and beyond.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to connect Enlightenment ideas to specific revolutions (American, French, Haitian, Latin American). Know which thinker influenced which revolution and how.

---

## Intellectual Roots

| Era | Thinkers | Key Ideas |
|-----|----------|-----------|
| Medieval Scholasticism | St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) | Reason defends faith; relied on Aristotle; little experimentation |
| Renaissance Humanism | Erasmus (1466–1536), Mirandola (1463–1494) | Human achievement; secularism; the individual |
| Scientific Revolution | Francis Bacon (1561–1626), Isaac Newton (1642–1727) | Empiricism; natural laws; reason leads to progress |
| Enlightenment | Hobbes, Locke, Montesquieu, Voltaire, Rousseau, Smith | Social contract; natural rights; constitutional government |

**Empiricism** (Francis Bacon): knowledge comes from sensed experience and observation — not tradition or religion. This method became the foundation of Enlightenment reasoning.

## The Core Thinkers (Quick Reference)

See [[Unit-5.1/People & Thinkers]] for full profiles.

- **Thomas Hobbes** — Social contract; strong central government; life naturally "nasty, brutish, and short"
- **John Locke** — Right to revolt against unjust government; natural rights (life, liberty, property); *tabula rasa*
- **Montesquieu** — Separation of powers; checks and balances → directly shaped the U.S. Constitution
- **Voltaire** — Civil liberties; religious freedom; satirized church and monarchy
- **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** — General Will; sovereign must serve the people; inspired late 18th-century revolutionaries
- **Adam Smith** — *Wealth of Nations* (1776); laissez-faire; capitalism; free trade vs. mercantilism

## The "Age of Isms"

The Enlightenment spawned competing ideologies — each one applying reason to a specific social problem:

| Ideology | Core Belief | Key Figures |
|----------|-------------|-------------|
| Classical Liberalism | Natural rights, constitutional gov, laissez-faire | Professionals, writers, academics |
| Conservatism | Traditional institutions; practical experience over theory | Edmund Burke; Joseph de Maistre |
| Utopian Socialism | Public/worker ownership; ideal communities | Saint-Simon, Fourier, Robert Owen |
| Feminism | Equal rights and education for women | Gouges, Wollstonecraft, Stanton |
| Abolitionism | End the slave trade and slavery | Atlantic-wide movement |
| Zionism | Jewish homeland in the Middle East | Theodor Herzl |

> [!tip]
> Think of the "Age of Isms" as the Enlightenment's children. Each "ism" is an attempt to apply reason to a social problem. The AP exam loves asking you to trace how these ideologies connect to Enlightenment thinking.

## Reform Movements

### Abolitionism
- Slave trade bans: Denmark (1803), Britain (1807), United States (1808)
- Most of the Americas abolished slavery within 30 years of ending the trade
- Brazil: last country in the Americas to end slavery (1888)
- **Unusual case:** U.S. enslaved population *increased* after the importation ban

### End of Serfdom
- England: Queen Elizabeth I abolished serfdom (1574)
- France: abolished feudal rights of nobility (1789)
- Russia: Alexander II abolished serfdom (1861) — **largest single emancipation in history** (23 million serfs)

### Feminism
- Olympe de Gouges (France, 1791): *Declaration of the Rights of Woman*
- Mary Wollstonecraft (England, 1792): *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* — argued for equal education
- Seneca Falls Convention (1848): Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton — demanded voting rights, property rights, guardianship
- British women's full suffrage: 1928

### Zionism
- Centuries of persecution (pogroms, anti-Semitism) led many Jews to conclude only a homeland would ensure safety
- Theodor Herzl led the movement; galvanized by the **Dreyfus Affair** (1894 — Jewish officer falsely convicted of treason in France)
- Modern Israel founded: 1948

## Why It Matters for the Exam

The Enlightenment is the **intellectual engine** driving all of Unit 5. Every revolution in 5.2 traces its ideas back here. Every reform movement of the 19th century is the Enlightenment applied to a specific injustice.

> [!exam-tip]
> For LEQ/DBQ prompts about revolutions, always name 1–2 specific Enlightenment thinkers and show exactly HOW their ideas caused change. "Locke's natural rights inspired Jefferson's Declaration of Independence" beats "Enlightenment ideas caused the American Revolution."
