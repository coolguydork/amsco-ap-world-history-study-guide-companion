# Themes and Causation — Unit 5.1: The Enlightenment

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## Governance

The Enlightenment's most transformative impact was on political legitimacy. By arguing that government authority derives from the *consent of the governed* rather than from God or hereditary right, Locke and Rousseau provided a new standard against which existing monarchies were found wanting. Montesquieu's separation of powers offered a structural blueprint for preventing tyranny. These ideas did not immediately topple governments — the Enlightenment was an intellectual movement, not a mass uprising — but they gave educated elites, colonial merchants, and dissatisfied aristocrats a principled language for challenging existing authority. The American and French Revolutions (Topic 5.2) translated Enlightenment political theory into constitutional documents, making the governance theme the most direct causal link from 5.1 to 5.2.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP causation questions often ask you to link a specific Enlightenment idea to a specific political event. Practice the chain: Locke's natural rights → Declaration of Independence → American Revolution → other Atlantic revolutions.

## Economic Systems

Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) challenged mercantilism — the dominant economic doctrine of the era, in which states regulated trade to accumulate bullion — with the argument that free markets produce greater wealth through specialization, competition, and the "invisible hand." Smith's ideas were not immediately adopted as policy (Britain retained many mercantile practices through the early 19th century), but they provided the intellectual foundation for the **free trade movement** that would reshape global commerce after 1840. The economic Enlightenment is also connected to industrialization: the celebration of rational self-interest, private property, and market exchange aligned perfectly with the needs of the emerging industrial bourgeoisie.

## Cultural Developments and Religion

The Enlightenment challenged the authority of the Catholic Church (and organized religion more broadly) without necessarily producing atheism. **Deism** — the belief in a rational creator who does not intervene in human affairs — was the typical Enlightenment religious position, compatible with science and hostile to "superstition." Voltaire's attacks on Church corruption and religious intolerance, and the *Encyclopédie*'s implicit secularism, contributed to the gradual weakening of religious authority in public life that historians call **secularization**. This process was uneven: it was faster in France than in Britain or the Americas, and it provoked fierce conservative and religious counter-reactions. The Enlightenment also produced a new **print culture** — newspapers, pamphlets, and novels — that spread ideas beyond the clergy and aristocracy to urban middle classes and artisans.

## Social Structures

The Enlightenment carried profound tensions between its universal language and its actual social reach. Most Enlightenment thinkers assumed that "natural rights" applied to property-owning men of European descent — and many openly excluded women, enslaved people, and colonized peoples. Mary Wollstonecraft used Enlightenment logic to argue for women's rights, but she remained exceptional. The enslaved people of Saint-Domingue (Haiti) would ultimately take Enlightenment universalism more seriously than Jefferson or the French National Assembly did. The Enlightenment also contributed to the emergence of a new **public sphere** of coffeehouses, salons, and print media that created a literate, politically engaged middle class — the social group that would provide the leadership for Atlantic Revolutions.

---

## Technology and Environment

The Enlightenment and the Scientific Revolution reinforced each other: Newton's laws proved that rational inquiry could unlock the secrets of nature, and Enlightenment thinkers extended this confidence to all human problems. The application of reason to the natural world contributed directly to the technological innovations of the Industrial Revolution. **Empiricism** — the idea that knowledge comes from observation and experiment — drove the practical improvements to steam engines, looms, and iron smelting that powered industrialization. The Enlightenment's faith in progress and human perfectibility also shaped how early industrialists thought about their work: factory discipline, rational management, and the systematic improvement of production processes all reflect Enlightenment rationalism applied to economic life.

---

## Human Migration

The Enlightenment does not directly cause mass migration, but it shapes its consequences. **Enlightenment ideas about individual liberty** made hereditary serfdom and slavery increasingly difficult to defend in public discourse — a tension that produced both emancipation movements and fierce pro-slavery ideological counter-attacks. The idea of **religious toleration** contributed to the willingness of some states (notably the USA) to accept diverse immigrant populations, laying a cultural foundation for later mass immigration in the 19th century. The Enlightenment's concept of the nation — a political community defined by shared rights, not shared blood — also set the conceptual stage for the nationalist mass politics of the 19th century.
