# Topic 5.1 – Practice and Review

**Unit 5: Revolutions | Period 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900)**

## Multiple Choice Practice

**1.** Which Enlightenment thinker most directly influenced the separation of powers in the U.S. Constitution?

- A) John Locke
- B) Jean-Jacques Rousseau
- C) Baron Montesquieu
- D) Thomas Hobbes

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> A is wrong — Locke influenced natural rights and consent of the governed, not the three-branch structure specifically.
> **C is correct** — Montesquieu's *Spirit of Laws* (1748) praised the British division of governmental power and directly shaped the U.S. three-branch system.
> B is wrong — Rousseau's General Will influenced the French Revolution, not the U.S. constitutional framework.
> D is wrong — Hobbes argued for an absolute sovereign, the opposite of separation of powers.

---

**2.** The *tabula rasa* concept proposed by John Locke was radical in the 17th century primarily because it

- A) rejected the existence of God
- B) argued that environment and education, not ancestry, shape a person
- C) called for the abolition of serfdom
- D) supported a strong central government

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — Locke was not an atheist; *tabula rasa* applied to human minds, not theology.
> **B is correct** — In a world where intelligence and fate were thought determined by birth and ancestry, Locke's emphasis on environment was genuinely radical and undermined the logic of hereditary aristocracy.
> C is wrong — *tabula rasa* was about human development and learning, not labor systems.
> D is wrong — Locke argued for *limited* government with the right of revolution, not a strong central authority.

---

**3.** Which of the following best describes the "invisible hand" concept in Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776)?

- A) Government should regulate trade to maximize national wealth
- B) Workers must organize to receive fair wages from factory owners
- C) Individual self-interest, when unregulated, naturally benefits society
- D) Religion provides the moral foundation for economic activity

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> A is wrong — Smith's invisible hand was an argument *against* heavy government regulation; mercantilism was his target.
> B is wrong — this reflects later socialist or labor movement ideas, not Smith.
> **C is correct** — Smith argued the market, not government planning, would guide self-interested individuals toward socially beneficial outcomes as an unintended consequence.
> D is wrong — Smith's framework was secular economic reasoning, not religious morality.

---

**4.** Mary Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792) most directly argued that

- A) women should lead political revolutions
- B) women should receive the same education as men
- C) slavery and the subjugation of women were morally equivalent
- D) the French Revolution had failed to address women's rights

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — Wollstonecraft focused on education reform and rational citizenship, not revolutionary leadership.
> **B is correct** — Wollstonecraft argued that equal education would allow women to participate in political and professional society as rational, equal citizens.
> C is wrong — while she drew parallels between women's and enslaved people's subjugation, this was not her central argument.
> D is wrong — while she was critical of the Revolution's limits, the *Vindication*'s primary argument was the positive case for female education and rights.

## Short Answer Question (SAQ) Practice

**Question**: Using specific evidence, briefly explain ONE way that Enlightenment ideas about natural rights contributed to social reform movements in the period 1750–1900.

**Model Response**: One way Enlightenment ideas about natural rights contributed to social reform was by fueling the abolitionist movement of the 18th and 19th centuries. John Locke had argued that all human beings possessed natural rights to life and liberty — rights that could not be taken away without just cause. Abolitionists on both sides of the Atlantic applied this logic directly: if all people possessed natural rights, then enslaving people was a fundamental violation of those rights. This reasoning animated campaigns in Britain, where figures like Granville Sharp and later William Wilberforce invoked natural rights language in the effort that abolished the British slave trade in 1807 and slavery itself in 1833. The same logic underpinned the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), in which enslaved people drew explicitly on the Declaration of the Rights of Man to justify their own liberation.

## Document-Based and Long Essay Connections

**For DBQs**: Topic 5.1 often appears in documents about:
- Enlightenment treatises and their political applications (Locke, Rousseau, Smith, Wollstonecraft excerpts)
- Declarations and constitutions that used Enlightenment language (U.S. Declaration of Independence, French Declaration of the Rights of Man)
- Abolitionist pamphlets and petitions citing natural rights
- Women's rights documents (Declaration of Sentiments, Seneca Falls 1848)

**For LEQs**: Common prompts connected to 5.1:
- "Evaluate the extent to which Enlightenment ideas caused political revolutions in the period 1750–1830."
- "Compare the ways two different Enlightenment ideologies responded to social inequalities in the period 1750–1900."
- "To what extent did the Enlightenment produce both change and continuity in political systems in the period 1750–1900?"

> [!exam-tip]
> When writing about Enlightenment ideas and their effects, always connect a specific idea to a specific outcome — don't just say "Locke influenced democracy." Say: "Locke's concept of natural rights, as developed in his *Second Treatise of Government*, provided the philosophical justification for the Declaration of Independence's claim that governments derive 'just powers from the consent of the governed.'" The AP rewards precise evidence, not vague references to "Enlightenment thinking."

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning]
> **Hobbes ≠ Democrat.** Hobbes believed in the social contract but argued it required a nearly absolute sovereign — he was NOT arguing for democracy or limited government. His *Leviathan* (1651) was a response to civil war that defended strong central authority. Students often confuse Hobbes and Locke because both used "social contract" language.

> [!warning]
> **"Enlightenment ideas caused all revolutions" is too simple.** Ideas interacted with material conditions — debt, taxation, famine, colonial resentment. A strong AP argument identifies BOTH intellectual and material causes and explains how they interacted.

> [!warning]
> **Adam Smith did NOT oppose all government.** He supported some regulations, public works, and taxes. His main target was *mercantilism* — not all government intervention. Calling Smith a pure "free market" thinker oversimplifies his actual argument.

## Quick Review: Key Enlightenment Thinkers

| Thinker | Key Idea | Key Work | Historical Impact |
|---------|----------|----------|-------------------|
| John Locke | Natural rights (life, liberty, property); tabula rasa; consent of governed | *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) | U.S. Declaration of Independence; constitutionalism |
| Montesquieu | Separation of powers; checks and balances | *Spirit of Laws* (1748) | U.S. Constitution's three-branch system |
| Rousseau | General Will; popular sovereignty | *Social Contract* (1762) | French Revolution; radical democracy |
| Adam Smith | Laissez-faire; invisible hand; free trade | *Wealth of Nations* (1776) | Capitalism replaces mercantilism |
| Voltaire | Religious toleration; free speech; anti-clericalism | *Candide* (1759) | Secular governance; church-state separation |
| Mary Wollstonecraft | Women's equal education and rational rights | *A Vindication of the Rights of Women* (1792) | Women's suffrage movement; feminism |
| Thomas Hobbes | Social contract requires strong sovereign | *Leviathan* (1651) | Justified absolutism; contrasted with Locke |
