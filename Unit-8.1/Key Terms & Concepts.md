# Key Terms & Concepts

*10 terms — Topic 8.1 | Printed pp. 547–553*

---

**Big Three**
The three main Allied leaders during WWII: US President Franklin Roosevelt (later Harry Truman), British PM Winston Churchill (later Clement Attlee), and Soviet Premier Joseph Stalin. They met at Tehran (1943), Yalta (1945), and Potsdam (1945) to coordinate strategy and plan the post-war world. Their agreements — and disagreements — over Eastern Europe directly set the stage for the Cold War.

> [!tip]
> The Big Three conferences follow a clear arc: Tehran = military coordination → Yalta = post-war planning with vague promises → Potsdam = open confrontation. This arc is the arc of the Cold War's origins.

---

**Tehran Conference**
November 1943 Allied summit in Iran where Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin coordinated strategy. Key outcomes: the USSR would focus on liberating Eastern Europe; Britain and the US would open a second front (D-Day); Poland's borders would shift westward. The first major meeting between all three Big Three leaders.

---

**Yalta Conference**
February 1945 summit at a Black Sea resort. Key outcomes: vague Soviet promise of free elections in Eastern Europe (broken almost immediately); agreement on the UN structure; Soviet pledge to enter the Pacific war after Germany's defeat; confirmation that Germany would be divided into occupation zones. Revealed the growing tension between Western democracy and Soviet control over Eastern Europe.

> [!warning]
> Students often blame Roosevelt for "giving away" Eastern Europe at Yalta. But Soviet troops already occupied most of Eastern Europe, and the US public was unlikely to support military confrontation with the USSR. Roosevelt had limited leverage — Stalin held the facts on the ground.

---

**Potsdam Conference**
July 1945 — the last Big Three summit, held in Germany. Truman replaced Roosevelt (who died April 1945); Attlee replaced Churchill mid-conference. Stalin refused Truman's demand for free elections in Soviet-occupied Eastern Europe. The conference confirmed Germany's division into occupation zones and issued Japan a surrender ultimatum. It is the clearest marker of the Cold War's beginning — the wartime alliance was effectively over.

---

**Cold War**
Political, ideological, and military competition (1947–1991) between the US and USSR that never escalated into direct military conflict between the two superpowers. Played out through proxy wars, arms races, propaganda, covert operations, and economic competition. The term "cold" distinguishes it from a "hot" (shooting) war — though for millions in Korea, Vietnam, Angola, and elsewhere, the proxy conflicts were very much hot wars.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Cold War's defining feature for AP purposes: the two superpowers never fought each other directly. All the major hot conflicts — Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan — were **proxy wars** where one or both superpowers armed and supported one side.

---

**Harry Truman**
US President (1945–1953) who succeeded Roosevelt. Authorized atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945); issued the Truman Doctrine (1947) committing the US to containing communist expansion; oversaw the Marshall Plan and the formation of [[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]]. The key architect of early Cold War containment policy.

---

**Dwight Eisenhower**
US President (1953–1961) and former WWII Supreme Allied Commander. Oversaw the Korean War armistice (1953); promoted "massive retaliation" (threatening nuclear response to any Soviet aggression) and covert CIA operations as Cold War tools. Famous for his 1961 farewell address warning against the growing **military-industrial complex**.

---

**self-determination**
The principle that peoples have the right to choose their own political status and form of government. Used by Woodrow Wilson after WWI to justify redrawing European borders, but denied to colonial peoples in Asia and Africa. After WWII, the hypocrisy of denying self-determination to colonized peoples — who had fought for Allied powers — became impossible to sustain, fueling the decolonization wave of the 1950s–1970s.

> [!tip]
> Self-determination is a cross-topic thread: Wilson's Fourteen Points (7.3) → denied to colonial peoples (5.x, 6.x) → revived after WWII → decolonization (8.5–8.6). On an LEQ, connect these across units.

---

**hydrogen bomb**
Thermonuclear weapon far more powerful than the atomic bombs dropped on Japan. The US tested its first H-bomb in 1952; the USSR followed in 1953. Escalated the nuclear arms race to weapons capable of destroying entire cities and led to the doctrine of **mutually assured destruction (MAD)** — the deterrence logic that a nuclear first strike would guarantee total retaliation, making nuclear war irrational for both sides.

---

**military-industrial complex**
Term coined by President Eisenhower in his 1961 farewell address to describe the dangerous informal alliance between the defense industry, the military, and the government. Eisenhower warned that their combined political influence could distort national priorities and sustain Cold War spending beyond genuine security needs. A key AP example of how Cold War mobilization reshaped American domestic society and politics.

> [!exam-tip]
> Eisenhower's warning is frequently used as a primary source in AP practice. His argument is essentially: the Cold War created structural incentives for permanent military buildup that would persist even when the security threat didn't justify it.
