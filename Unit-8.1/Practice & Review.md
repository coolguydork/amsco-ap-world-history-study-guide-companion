## Multiple-Choice Practice

**Questions 1–3** refer to the following table (Nuclear Weapons Stockpiles, 1945–2015):

| Year | United States | Soviet Union |
|------|--------------|-------------|
| 1945 | 6 | 0 |
| 1955 | 3,057 | 200 |
| 1965 | 31,982 | 6,129 |
| 1975 | 27,826 | 19,055 |
| 1985 | 24,237 | 39,197 |
| 1995 | 12,144 | 27,000 |
| 2005 | 10,295 | 17,000 |
| 2015 | 7,100 | 7,700 |

*Source: Hans M. Kristensen and Robert S. Norris, "Global Nuclear Stockpiles, 1945–2006," Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists; Arms Control Association*

---

**1.** The data in the chart best supports which of the following claims?

A. The Soviet Union was primarily responsible for the escalation of the arms race in the 1950s and 1960s.
B. The Soviet Union and the United States usually had about the same number of nuclear weapons.
C. The Soviet Union was behind in stockpiling weapons until about 1980, when it overtook the United States in the number of weapons.
D. The Soviet Union conceded defeat in the arms race when the United States had a large enough lead in the number of weapons.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The data clearly shows the US leading in stockpiles from 1945 through the mid-1970s, with the USSR overtaking the US around 1985. Option A is unsupported — the US escalated dramatically first (6 → 31,982 weapons 1945–1965). Option B is wrong — stockpiles diverged widely for decades. Option D has no support in the data.

---

**2.** Who predicted that increases in weapons stockpiles in the Soviet Union and the United States would have significant political and economic consequences?

A. President Franklin Roosevelt at the Yalta Conference
B. Several leaders of anti-colonial movements after World War II
C. President Dwight Eisenhower, who described a military-industrial complex
D. President Harry Truman, who insisted on free elections for Eastern European countries

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Eisenhower's 1961 farewell address warned specifically that the alliance between the defense industry and government would sustain weapons buildups beyond genuine security needs — a political and economic warning, not just a military one. Roosevelt at Yalta was focused on post-war Europe, not weapons stockpiles. Truman's Eastern Europe demands had no connection to arms race consequences.

---

**3.** Which accurately describes an effect of the changes shown in the table?

A. Both countries saw little change in their leadership as a result of the increase in the number of nuclear weapons during this period.
B. People around the world supported the buildup of nuclear weapons and agreed it was the best way to ensure peace.
C. The stockpiling of nuclear weapons actually strengthened the economies of both the United States and the Soviet Union.
D. The arms race was distressing to many around the world, and a strong antinuclear movement was established.

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The AMSCO narrative explicitly notes that citizens in many countries protested the stockpiling of nuclear weapons — this is the only answer supported by the text. Option A is wrong — the arms race did affect leadership (Eisenhower's military-industrial complex warning was itself a political development). Option C inverts the reality — massive defense spending distorted both economies. Option B contradicts the antinuclear movements mentioned in the narrative.

## Short-Answer Practice

**SAQ 1** refers to the following passage:

> "On November 1, 1952, the United States successfully detonated 'Mike,' the world's first hydrogen bomb, on the Elugelab Atoll in the Pacific Marshall Islands. The 10.4-megaton thermonuclear device... instantly vaporized an entire island and left behind a crater more than a mile wide... Three years later, on November 22, 1955, the Soviet Union detonated its first hydrogen bomb... Both superpowers were now in possession of the 'hell bomb,' as it was known by many Americans, and the world lived under the threat of thermonuclear war for the first time in history."
>
> *— History.com, 2018*

**A.** Identify ONE way in which the hydrogen bomb shifted the global balance of power.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The hydrogen bomb shifted the global balance of power by making both superpowers capable of mutual annihilation, which led to the doctrine of mutually assured destruction (MAD). Once both the US (1952) and USSR (1953) possessed thermonuclear weapons, neither could launch a first strike without guaranteeing its own destruction. This paradoxically stabilized the Cold War at the superpower level — direct war became irrational — but also intensified the psychological terror of living under existential threat and drove proxy conflicts elsewhere.

**B.** Explain ONE reason why the development of atomic weapons was important to the United States and the Soviet Union.

> [!tip] Model Response — Context
> Atomic weapons were important to both superpowers as instruments of deterrence and status. For the United States, the atomic monopoly (1945–1949) provided a strategic advantage that allowed it to reduce conventional forces while still threatening the USSR. For the Soviet Union, achieving atomic capability in 1949 was a matter of existential security — Stalin viewed the US monopoly as an intolerable threat. Each state's atomic capability functioned as the ultimate guarantee that the other would not attempt a conventional military defeat. Atomic weapons thus defined the Cold War's ceiling: neither side could push the other to total war without risking annihilation.

**C.** Explain ONE way the hydrogen bomb contributed to Cold War tensions after 1952.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The hydrogen bomb escalated Cold War tensions by accelerating the arms race and inspiring the antinuclear movement. Once both superpowers possessed H-bombs capable of vaporizing entire cities, the sheer destructive capacity sparked grassroots protest movements in Western Europe, the US, and elsewhere. Citizens began demanding arms limitation treaties and protesting government nuclear policy. This public pressure created new political tensions within Western democracies — governments had to balance the security logic of deterrence against popular demands to reduce nuclear arsenals. Eisenhower's warning about the military-industrial complex (1961) was itself a product of the anxieties the hydrogen bomb generated.

**SAQ 2** (no stimulus)

**A.** Identify ONE way in which hopes for self-government after World War I differed from hopes for self-government after World War II.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> After World War I, hopes for self-government were largely limited to European peoples — Wilson's Fourteen Points promised self-determination, but colonial peoples in Asia and Africa were excluded at the Paris Peace Conference. After World War II, the demand for self-determination had genuinely globalized: colonial peoples who had served in Allied armies, and nationalist movements that had organized during the interwar years, now articulated independence claims that could not be dismissed as regional concerns. The founding of the United Nations (1945) and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) provided an international framework that legitimized colonial independence in a way that post-WWI arrangements had not.

**B.** Explain ONE historical development that contributed to the dissolution of empires after World War II.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> World War II so weakened the major European colonial powers — Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium — that they lacked the economic and military resources to suppress large-scale independence movements. Britain, for instance, emerged from the war heavily indebted to the United States and unable to sustain the military garrisons needed to hold India against a mass civil disobedience movement. France, having been occupied and liberated, had neither the political unity nor the financial capacity to fight major colonial wars without external support. The simple material exhaustion of colonial powers was a necessary precondition for decolonization — without it, the other factors (nationalist movements, Cold War leverage) might not have been sufficient.

**C.** Explain ONE way in which economic gains contributed to a shift in the global balance of power after World War II.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The United States' economic gains during World War II — its industrial base grew rather than contracted — allowed it to establish post-war economic dominance through the Bretton Woods system and the Marshall Plan. By anchoring the global economy to the US dollar (through Bretton Woods, 1944), the US institutionalized a structural advantage: all international trade and finance was conducted in a currency only the US could print. The Marshall Plan then deployed this economic power diplomatically, rebuilding Western European economies in ways that bound them to the US-led capitalist system. This economic dominance translated directly into political and military power — the ability to fund NATO, sustain a global military presence, and support anti-communist governments worldwide — a form of influence no other state could match.
