# Setting the Stage for the Cold War & Decolonization

**Essential Question:** How did World War II set the stage for both the Cold War and the dismantling of colonial empires?

**AP Period 4 | Printed pp. 547–553 | AP Themes: GOV, ECN, CDI | AP Skill: Causation**

## The Big Picture

World War II destroyed the old world order and created two new ones simultaneously. The war so devastated Europe that the traditional colonial powers — Britain, France, the Netherlands — emerged weakened and unable to sustain their empires. At the same time, it elevated two ideologically opposed superpowers whose rivalry would define global politics for the next four decades. These two outcomes — the Cold War and decolonization — are both *caused* by WWII, and they are deeply entangled: colonial independence movements would repeatedly become proxy battlegrounds for US-Soviet competition.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the **causes** of the Cold War and decolonization. The key argument: WWII created a power vacuum in Europe and Asia, enabling both Soviet expansion and anti-colonial independence movements. The US and USSR filled the vacuum left by weakened European empires — and their rivalry gave colonial nationalists leverage they hadn't had before.

## The Wartime Conferences: Sowing the Seeds of Cold War

The wartime summits among the Big Three (US, UK, USSR) produced the post-war framework — and planted the tensions that would become the Cold War.

The **Tehran Conference** (November 1943) set the strategic division: the USSR would liberate Eastern Europe, while the West would open a second front (D-Day). The Soviets also extracted a concession shifting Polish territory westward.

The **Yalta Conference** (February 1945) addressed post-war governance as Germany's defeat neared. Roosevelt wanted democratic elections in Eastern Europe; Stalin wanted a buffer zone against future Western invasion. Stalin promised free elections but delivered only vague assurances — a promise he would break almost immediately. The conference also confirmed Soviet entry into the Pacific war and agreed to the UN framework.

The **Potsdam Conference** (July 1945) was the last Big Three summit. Truman replaced Roosevelt; Attlee replaced Churchill mid-conference. Truman demanded free elections in Eastern Europe — but Soviet troops already occupied the region. Stalin refused. The conference confirmed Germany's division into occupation zones and issued Japan an ultimatum. It also made clear that the wartime alliance was over.

> [!warning]
> Students often assume the Cold War began at a single moment. It didn't — it hardened gradually from Yalta through Potsdam into the Truman Doctrine (1947). The conferences reveal *escalating* distrust, not a sudden break.

## The Shifting Balance of Power

WWII killed between 40–60 million people and destroyed the industrial infrastructure of Europe and Asia. The uneven distribution of destruction matters for the AP exam:

The **Soviet Union** suffered the most — 20–27 million dead, vast territories devastated. Yet it emerged with the largest land army and occupied Eastern Europe. The **United States** suffered the least — no fighting on the mainland, an industrial base that actually *grew* during the war, and exclusive possession of atomic weapons until 1949. By the late 1940s, only these two nations could project global power.

Western European powers like Britain and France maintained democratic institutions and educated populations, giving them a foundation to rebuild — but they would no longer dominate global affairs. The destruction of European power created the conditions for both the Cold War rivalry and colonial independence.

> [!tip]
> **Useful comparison:** After WWI, the US retreated into isolationism. After WWII, it stayed engaged — the Marshall Plan, [[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]], Truman Doctrine. Why the difference? The atomic bomb, the Soviet threat, and the lesson of appeasement all made inaction seem more dangerous than involvement.

## The Start of the Cold War

The US and USSR shared a mutual distrust rooted in ideological difference: American capitalism and liberal democracy vs. Soviet communism and one-party rule. After WWII, neither wanted direct military conflict — the costs of the hot war were too fresh. Instead, they waged a **Cold War**: propaganda, proxy conflicts, arms races, and secret operations.

The arms race escalated rapidly. The US tested the hydrogen bomb in 1952; the Soviets followed in 1953. By the 1960s, both sides possessed enough nuclear weapons to destroy civilization. President **Dwight Eisenhower** warned in his 1961 farewell address against the growing **military-industrial complex** — the informal alliance between defense contractors, the military, and government — arguing it distorted national priorities and sustained dangerous levels of Cold War spending.

## The Breakdown of Empires

WWII created three structural conditions for decolonization:

1. **Anti-colonial movements strengthened.** Colonial peoples had fought in the war; the promises of [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]] (used to justify the war against fascism) could not be denied indefinitely. The rhetoric of freedom and human rights rang hollow when applied only to Europeans.

2. **Colonial powers were weakened.** Britain, France, and the Netherlands emerged from the war economically devastated, with fewer resources to maintain far-flung empires against determined independence movements.

3. **The Cold War gave nationalists leverage.** The US and USSR both opposed European colonialism (though for different reasons) and actively courted independence movements. Colonial leaders could play one superpower against the other to extract support.

India and Pakistan's independence (1947) was the first major break, demonstrating that the British Empire could be dismantled — and that independence could be achieved through mass political organization, not just armed rebellion. The full wave of African and Asian decolonization would follow in Topics 8.5–8.6.

> [!exam-tip]
> LEQ and DBQ prompts frequently ask students to explain the causes of decolonization or evaluate the relative significance of different factors. Practice the argument: WWII weakened colonial powers AND strengthened anti-colonial movements AND gave nationalists Cold War leverage — all three factors reinforce each other.
