# People, States & Movements

*Topic 8.1 | Printed pp. 547–553*

---

## Key Figures

**Harry S. Truman**
US President (1945–1953). Succeeded FDR after Roosevelt's death in April 1945. Represented the US at Potsdam — where he confronted Stalin over Eastern Europe and signaled the end of the wartime alliance. Authorized the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945), the only use of nuclear weapons in warfare. Issued the **Truman Doctrine** (1947): a formal US commitment to "contain" communist expansion by supporting free peoples resisting subjugation. Oversaw the Marshall Plan and the founding of NATO. Truman transformed US foreign policy from post-WWII caution to permanent Cold War engagement.

> [!exam-tip]
> Truman is the hinge figure between WWII and the Cold War. His decisions in 1945–1947 define US Cold War strategy for decades: atomic monopoly → Soviet breakout → containment → aid programs → alliances.

---

**Joseph Stalin**
Soviet Premier (1924–1953). Arrived at Tehran, Yalta, and Potsdam with a clear strategic goal: Soviet security through control of Eastern Europe as a buffer zone against Western invasion (referencing Napoleon and Hitler). Extracted Soviet-friendly borders and influence at each conference, then systematically installed communist governments in Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Bulgaria, Romania, and East Germany. His refusal to accept the Marshall Plan (and pressure on Eastern European states to reject it) hardened the Iron Curtain. Died in 1953, but the Soviet system he built shaped the Cold War for decades.

> [!warning]
> Don't reduce Stalin to simply "evil dictator." His demand for an Eastern European buffer zone had a genuine strategic logic — the USSR had been invaded from the west twice in living memory. Understanding his perspective explains why the Cold War happened, not just that it happened.

---

**Winston Churchill**
British Prime Minister through most of WWII; replaced by Clement Attlee mid-way through Potsdam. Churchill's most significant post-war contribution: his 1946 "Iron Curtain" speech in Fulton, Missouri, which publicly named the Cold War division of Europe and called on the West to resist Soviet expansion. Though out of office, Churchill gave the ideological framework of containment its most memorable phrase.

---

**George Marshall**
US Secretary of State (1947–1949). Architect of the **Marshall Plan** — the $13 billion US aid program to rebuild Western European economies after WWII. The plan had twin goals: humanitarian rebuilding AND preventing communist parties from gaining electoral power in war-devastated democracies. The Marshall Plan is the economic pillar of early Cold War containment (Topic 8.2).

---

**Dwight D. Eisenhower**
US President (1953–1961); Supreme Allied Commander during WWII. Managed the Korean War armistice (1953). His farewell address (January 1961) introduced the term **military-industrial complex**, warning that the alliance between the defense industry, military, and government had grown powerful enough to distort American democracy and sustain Cold War spending for its own sake — not just for genuine security.

---

**Franklin D. Roosevelt**
US President through most of WWII; died April 12, 1945 — before Germany's surrender. Roosevelt's approach at Tehran and Yalta was pragmatic: he believed he could manage Stalin through personal diplomacy and that the US public would not support a war with the USSR over Eastern Europe's democracy. His death left Truman — who was far less inclined toward accommodation — to deal with Stalin at Potsdam.

---

**Lal Bahadur Shastri**
Indian independence movement leader quoted in the chapter's epigraph: "We would consider it our moral duty to lend all support to the ending of colonialism and imperialism." Represents the broader generation of colonial nationalists who connected the defeat of fascism in WWII to the illegitimacy of colonial rule — if self-determination was a universal principle, it applied to India, not just Europe.

## Key States & Blocs

**The United States (post-WWII)**
Emerged from WWII as the dominant global power: the only major combatant whose mainland was untouched, whose industrial base grew during the war, and who held an atomic monopoly until 1949. Economic strength allowed the Marshall Plan; military strength enabled NATO and containment. The US also led the creation of the Bretton Woods system (IMF, World Bank, US dollar as global reserve currency), cementing US economic dominance.

**The Soviet Union (post-WWII)**
Suffered catastrophic losses (20–27 million dead) but emerged with the world's largest land army and occupation of Eastern Europe. Stalin converted that occupation into a sphere of influence — satellite states with communist governments. The USSR successfully tested an atomic bomb in 1949, ending US monopoly and triggering the H-bomb race. By the late 1940s, the Soviet Union was the only power capable of challenging the US.

**Western Europe (post-WWII)**
Physically devastated but institutionally resilient — strong democratic traditions, educational systems, and surviving corporations gave Western Europe a foundation for rebuilding. The Marshall Plan accelerated recovery. But Western Europe's global power was fundamentally diminished: it could no longer project imperial authority across Asia and Africa at pre-war levels. This weakness directly enabled decolonization.

## The Anti-Colonial Movement (Post-WWII Context)

The post-WWII conditions created a structural opening for anti-colonial independence movements:

Colonial peoples had served in Allied armies. The wartime rhetoric of freedom and democracy applied to European peoples but not colonial subjects — a contradiction that nationalists like Shastri, [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]], and others could exploit in the court of international opinion.

The founding of the **United Nations** (1945) and the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (1948) provided an international legal and moral framework that anti-colonial leaders would invoke repeatedly. Self-determination was now, at least in principle, a recognized international norm.

The Cold War created leverage: the US opposed colonialism (in theory) as a form of the tyranny it claimed to fight; the USSR actively supported anti-colonial movements as a way to weaken Western capitalist empires. Colonial leaders could play the superpowers against each other to accelerate independence.

> [!tip]
> India-Pakistan independence (1947) is the paradigm case: the British were too economically and politically exhausted to hold on; the independence movement was too large and well-organized to suppress; and international pressure (including from the US) made continued colonialism diplomatically costly. Every later decolonization story has some version of these factors.
