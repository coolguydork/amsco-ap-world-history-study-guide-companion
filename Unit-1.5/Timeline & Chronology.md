# Timeline & Chronology — Topic 1.5: Developments in Africa

Use this timeline to understand the **sequence of causation** in Sub-Saharan Africa, c. 500–1450. Dates here represent landmarks in state formation, religious diffusion, trade expansion, and cultural development.

---

## Pre-AP Background (Before c. 1200)

| Date | Event | Significance |
|------|-------|--------------|
| c. 5th century CE | Kingdom of **Ghana founded** | First major West African kingdom; predates Islam by centuries; demonstrates indigenous African state capacity |
| c. 500–1000 | **Bantu migrations** complete southward/eastward spread | Agriculture adopted across Sub-Saharan Africa; kin-based networks become the baseline political structure |
| 8th–11th centuries | **Ghana peaks** | Thriving gold-ivory trade with North Africa; capital at Koumbi Saleh; iron-armed military |
| c. 9th century | **Zimbabwean chiefs begin stone construction** | "Zimbabwe" (Bantu: "dwellings") built in stone; precursor to Great Zimbabwe's massive walls |
| **869–883** | **Zanj Rebellion**, Mesopotamia | ~15,000 enslaved East Africans (zanj) revolt in Mesopotamia; capture Basra; hold it 10 years — one of history's largest slave revolts; demonstrates early Indian Ocean slave trade |

> [!exam-tip]
> The Zanj Rebellion is a striking fact that AP sources sometimes reference. Remember: this revolt happened **in Mesopotamia** (modern Iraq), not in Africa itself. The enslaved people were taken from East Africa via the Indian Ocean slave trade to work on Mesopotamian sugar plantations.

---

## Core AP Period: c. 1200–c. 1450

| Date | Event | Significance |
|------|-------|--------------|
| c. 1000+ | **Larger kingdoms grow in prominence** in Sub-Saharan Africa | Kin-based networks persist but centralized kingdoms expand; population growth and competition drive political consolidation |
| Before c. 1000 | **Hausa Kingdoms** form (Nigeria) | Seven loosely connected city-states; no central authority; rely on trans-Saharan trade; specialized by function (military defense, cotton production) |
| c. 12th century | **Ghana weakens** from wars with neighbors | Opens space for Mali's rise; key pattern of "replacement" rather than collapse: one trading empire succeeds another |
| c. 12th century | **Mali rises** under Sundiata | Sundiata, a Muslim, uses Islamic merchant networks to build the gold trade; Mali becomes the dominant West African power |
| c. 12th century | **New Christian kingdom** emerges in Ethiopia | 11 massive rock-carved churches built; Ethiopia develops as an "island of Christianity" independent of Rome and Constantinople |
| By end of 13th century | **Great Zimbabwe** completed | Massive stone wall (30 ft tall, 15 ft thick, no mortar) encircles the capital; largest pre-mortar stone structure on the continent |
| 14th century | **Islam introduced to Hausa Kingdoms** | Via missionaries from the trans-Saharan network; illustrates Islam's slow, peaceful penetration of interior Africa |
| c. 1324 | **Mansa Musa's pilgrimage to Mecca** | Mali's most famous ruler travels with enormous gold retinue; causes inflation in Egypt and North Africa; puts Mali on Islamic and European maps |
| c. 1352 | **Ibn Battuta visits Mali** | Moroccan scholar's travelogue provides one of the few written primary sources for West African society in this period — but from an outsider's Islamic perspective |
| Late 15th century | **Great Zimbabwe abandoned** | Overgrazing degrades the surrounding environment; ~20,000 residents relocate; kingdom declines |

> [!tip]
> Notice the **pattern of succession**: Ghana → Mali (West Africa) and **Zimbabwe** as a separate, parallel story in East Africa. These are not sequential empires in the same territory — they are simultaneous, regionally distinct developments linked by the same underlying driver: **gold and trade**.

## Chronological Causation Chain

```
Bantu migrations complete (by c. 1000)
    ↓ agriculture → kin-based networks → population growth
    ↓ competition increases → larger kingdoms needed
        ↓ WEST AFRICA: gold + trans-Saharan trade
            → Ghana (8th–11th c.)
            → Mali (12th–14th c.)
                → Mansa Musa pilgrimage (1324)
                → Timbuktu as Islamic scholarship center
                    → [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade|Topic 2.4]]
        ↓ EAST AFRICA: gold + Indian Ocean trade
            → coastal city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu)
            → Zimbabwe (12th–15th c.)
                → Swahili language develops
                → Great Zimbabwe (13th c. walls)
                    → [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Exchange|Topic 2.3]]
        ↓ INLAND AFRICA: kin-based networks persist
            → Hausa Kingdoms (before c. 1000)
            → Islam introduced (14th c. missionaries)
        ↓ ETHIOPIA: Christianity as state identity
            → Rock-carved churches (12th c.)
            → Independent Christianity blended with local traditions
```

## CCOT Framework: Africa c. 1200–c. 1450

| Category | c. 1200 | c. 1450 | Change or Continuity? |
|----------|---------|---------|----------------------|
| West Africa | Ghana weakening | Mali dominant; Timbuktu rising | **Change**: Mali replaces Ghana; Islamic scholarship grows |
| East Africa | Zimbabwe rising; Indian Ocean trade active | Zimbabwe declining (overgrazing) | **Change**: Environmental degradation ends a major kingdom |
| Religion | Animism, Christianity (Ethiopia), early Islam in coastal areas | Islam spread into Hausa Kingdoms; Mali deeply Islamic | **Change**: Islam expands; continuity of animism and Ethiopian Christianity |
| Political structure | Mix of kin-based networks and early kingdoms | Larger kingdoms dominant in most regions | **Change**: Gradual centralization in core trade zones |
| Social structures | Kinship, age grades, gender division of labor | Same structures intact | **Continuity**: Social organization fundamentally unchanged |
| Trade networks | Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean routes active | Both routes expand; Swahili coast grows | **Continuity and acceleration**: Trade ongoing; volume and integration increase |

## Key People — Chronological

**Sundiata** (fl. 12th–13th century) — Founding ruler of Mali; Muslim; used Islamic trade networks to build gold commerce. His story is West Africa's most famous oral epic, preserved by [[Concept Notes/Griots|griots]].

**Mansa Musa** (ruled c. 1312–1337) — Mali's most internationally famous ruler. His 1324 pilgrimage caused gold inflation across North Africa and placed Mali on Islamic world maps. His reign represents the peak of Mali's wealth and prestige.

**Ibn Battuta** (visited Mali c. 1352) — Moroccan Islamic scholar and traveler. His travelogue is the most important written primary source for West African society in this period. Note the source limitation: it reflects an Islamic outsider's perspective, not an indigenous West African voice.

## Connections to AP Timeline Landmarks

| AP Event | Connection to Topic 1.5 |
|----------|------------------------|
| Mongol Empire (c. 1206–1368) | Mongols did **not** penetrate Sub-Saharan Africa — the Sahara was too large a barrier; Africa's trajectory was shaped by trade networks, not Mongol disruption |
| Black Death (c. 1347–1353) | Sub-Saharan Africa largely spared — another consequence of relative isolation from Eurasian trade networks |
| 1324 Mansa Musa pilgrimage | Places Mali in international consciousness; Mali appears on the Catalan Atlas (1375) — a European map — after this event |
| Indian Ocean slave trade | Predates the Atlantic slave trade by centuries; this continuity from Topic 1.5 extends forward to Topic 4.3 and beyond |

> [!tip]
> The **Black Death's absence from Sub-Saharan Africa** is itself an AP argument: the same trade networks that created African wealth (Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan) did not carry the plague southward at the same intensity. Africa's relative geographic isolation from the Black Death's epicenters in Central Asia was, in this case, protective.
