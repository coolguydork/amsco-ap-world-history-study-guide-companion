# Topic 1.5 — Developments in Africa

*AMSCO pp. 43–52 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

> [!exam-tip]
> Africa in this period is defined by three overlapping patterns: (1) the diversity of political forms, from stateless kin-based societies to powerful trading kingdoms; (2) the gold-salt trade as the engine of West African state power; and (3) Islam's expansion into sub-Saharan Africa and how Africans adapted it to local traditions. The AP exam frequently asks about **continuity** in Africa — specifically how pre-Islamic traditions persisted even as Islam spread.

## Big Picture

Sub-Saharan Africa in Period 1 presents a vivid contrast to the centralized empires of Asia and the Americas. Most African societies were **kin-based and decentralized** — organized around family and clan networks rather than state bureaucracies. Yet alongside these stateless societies, powerful trading kingdoms flourished: Mali in the west grew fabulously wealthy on gold-salt trade; Great Zimbabwe in the east thrived on Indian Ocean commerce; Ethiopia maintained its ancient Christian identity. Islam spread throughout the continent, but African societies absorbed it selectively — keeping their traditions alive within an Islamic framework.

## Political Structures in Inland Africa (Stateless Societies)

The **Bantu migration** — the centuries-long expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples from west-central Africa outward across sub-Saharan Africa — shaped the political landscape by spreading agriculture and kin-based governance structures. By c. 1000 CE most of sub-Saharan Africa had adopted agriculture, and with it the need for more complex political organization.

But unlike most of Asia or Europe, **sub-Saharan African societies did not typically centralize power under one ruler**. Instead, communities formed **kin-based networks** — families governed themselves; a male chief mediated conflicts; groups of chiefs resolved district-level problems collectively. This is sometimes called a **stateless society** or **acephalous** (headless) political structure.

As populations grew, kin-based networks faced pressure. Competition increased between villages; small communities became more vulnerable. Larger kingdoms grew in prominence, especially after 1000. But kin-based organization remained the dominant form for much of sub-Saharan Africa throughout the period.

**The Hausa Kingdoms** (northern Nigeria, before 1000 CE): Seven loosely connected city-states of the Hausa ethnic group, each with its own specialty — cotton production, military defense, crafts. They profited from trans-Saharan trade but lacked a central authority, leaving them vulnerable to outside domination. Islam arrived via missionaries in the 14th century.

> [!warning]
> "Stateless" doesn't mean chaotic or without governance — it means governance was organized at the kinship/village level rather than through a formal state apparatus. Many kin-based African societies were remarkably stable and well-governed within their scale.

## West Africa: Ghana and Mali

**Ghana** (5th–11th centuries; not the same as modern Ghana): Located between the Sahara and the tropical rainforest of West Africa, Ghana controlled the gold-salt trade at its peak from the 8th to 11th centuries. The king ruled from **Koumbi Saleh** with a centralized government, nobles, and iron-equipped army. Ghana's rulers sold gold and ivory to Muslim North African traders in exchange for salt, copper, cloth, and tools. By the 12th century, warfare with neighbors permanently weakened Ghana, and **Mali** rose in its place.

**Mali** (c. 1235–1600): Founded by **Sundiata Keita** (c. 1235), who used Muslim trade connections to build a thriving gold trade. Under his nephew **Mansa Musa** — who made a legendary pilgrimage (*hajj*) to Mecca in **1324**, distributing so much gold along the route that he reportedly depressed gold prices across North Africa and the Middle East — Mali became the wealthiest kingdom in the world. Mali's story continues in Topic 2.4, where the growth of Timbuktu and the Songhai Empire are covered in depth.

> [!tip]
> **Gold-salt trade logic:** West Africa had abundant gold but almost no salt (essential for human survival in hot climates); the Saharan and North African regions had abundant salt but little gold. Trade across the Sahara was mutually beneficial and made rulers who controlled the exchange points enormously wealthy.

## East Africa: Great Zimbabwe and the Swahili Coast

**Great Zimbabwe** (12th–15th centuries): Situated between the Zambezi and Limpopo rivers (modern Zimbabwe/Mozambique), Great Zimbabwe was the most powerful East African kingdom of the period. Its wealth came from gold, agriculture, and grazing — and crucially, from taxing gold transport to the Swahili coastal city-states. Unlike Ghana and Mali, Great Zimbabwe was connected to the **Indian Ocean trade network** through ports like Mombasa, Kilwa, and Mogadishu.

Its defining architectural achievement: a massive stone wall — 30 feet tall, 15 feet thick — surrounding the capital, built **without mortar**. At its peak, nearly 20,000 people lived within the capital. But **overgrazing** so damaged the surrounding environment that residents abandoned the capital by the end of the 1400s — an environmental collapse parallel to Maya deforestation.

**Swahili city-states** (Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu, Malindi): East African port cities where Bantu and Arabic traders mixed to develop the **Swahili language** — a Bantu grammatical structure with extensive Arabic vocabulary. These cities were deeply integrated into the Indian Ocean trading network, connecting East Africa to the Middle East, South Asia, and East Asia. Swahili today is spoken by tens of millions across East Africa.

## Ethiopia: An Island of Christianity

**Axum** (ancient) → **Ethiopia** (c. 12th century onward): The kingdom of Axum, in what is today Ethiopia, adopted Christianity in the **4th century CE** (under King **Ezana**) and became one of the oldest Christian kingdoms in the world. From the 12th through 16th centuries, Ethiopia was an isolated **Christian island on a continent increasingly shaped by Islam**. Cut off from both the Roman Catholic Church (western Europe) and the Eastern Orthodox Church, Ethiopian Christianity developed independently — blending traditional practices like ancestor veneration and spirit beliefs with Christianity to create a distinctive faith.

In the 12th century, Ethiopian rulers built **11 monumental churches carved entirely from rock** — massive structures cut downward into the earth, featuring labyrinths, reservoirs, and tunnels. These were both religious monuments and defensive structures. They remain standing today as UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

## Social Structures of Sub-Saharan Africa

Sub-Saharan African societies were organized around **kinship, age, and gender**:

- **Kinship:** Clan/family identity came first. Land was not privately owned in most kin-based societies — people owned other people (through enslaved workers) as a marker of wealth and status.
- **Age grades:** Communities divided work by age — older people provided advice and guidance; younger people performed heavy labor. This age-set system created social cohesion across families.
- **Gender:** Men dominated skilled occupations (metalworking, leather-tanning); women engaged primarily in agriculture, food gathering, and domestic work.

**Slavery in sub-Saharan Africa:** Slavery had deep roots in Africa before European contact — prisoners of war, debtors, and criminals could all be enslaved. AMSCO describes three forms: chattel (ownership of persons as legal property), domestic (household service), and debt bondage. The **Indian Ocean slave trade** — East Africans (called *zanj* in Arabic) enslaved and sent to the Middle East — predates the Atlantic slave trade by centuries. The **Zanj Rebellion** (869–883 CE), in which enslaved East Africans captured the city of Basra and held it for ten years, is one of the largest and most successful slave revolts in history.

## Cultural Life: Griots and Oral Tradition

Sub-Saharan Africa's literary tradition was **oral**, not written. **Griots** (male) and **griottes** (female) were professional oral historians, genealogists, and musicians. They memorized family lineages, the deeds of rulers, and the histories of their communities. They sang their stories and accompanied themselves on instruments (drums, the 12-string kora). Griots counseled kings on political matters; griottes performed at ceremonies like weddings. The famous saying: "When a griot dies, it is as if a library has burned."

This oral tradition was functional, culturally rich, and deeply integrated into political life — but it also meant that much pre-colonial African history was not recorded in text and is therefore harder for historians to reconstruct. This connects directly to the sourcing challenges in the SAQ and MCQ for this topic.

> [!exam-tip]
> The griot tradition is frequently used on AP exams to test knowledge of oral history as a valid form of historical evidence, and to probe questions about the limitations of historical knowledge (what we know and don't know about African history before extensive written records). Use it to argue both that oral tradition is sophisticated AND that it creates interpretive challenges.

## Islam in Sub-Saharan Africa

Islam spread into sub-Saharan Africa primarily through **trade contacts** along the trans-Saharan routes and the Indian Ocean. The process was gradual and syncretic: African societies that adopted Islam kept many of their traditions, blending them with Islamic practice. Ibn Battuta's observation (1352) that Mali's court retained pre-Islamic customs while practicing Islam illustrates this perfectly.

> [!tip]
> **Islam in Africa :: Islam in Southeast Asia** — In both regions, Islam spread primarily through **trade, not conquest**, and converts adapted Islam to local customs rather than abandoning pre-existing traditions. This contrasts with Islam in northern India, where military conquest (Delhi Sultanate) preceded and shaped peaceful conversion.
