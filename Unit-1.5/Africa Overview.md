# Topic 1.5 — Developments in Africa

> [!summary] Essential Question
> How and why did **states develop in Africa** and **change over time**?

## Context: A Continent of Diversity

Africa is not a monolith. In this period (c. 1200–c. 1450), Sub-Saharan Africa contained radically different political structures — from kin-based village networks with no central authority to sophisticated kingdoms growing rich from gold and trade. Three major religions — animism, Christianity, and Islam — all had a presence across the continent.

The primary driver of state formation in Africa was **trade**: trans-Saharan trade (connecting West Africa to North Africa and the Mediterranean) and Indian Ocean trade (connecting East Africa to the Middle East, South Asia, and East Asia).

---

## Political Structures: From Kin-Based to Centralized

### Kin-Based Networks (the Baseline)

The development of Sub-Saharan Africa was shaped by the **Bantu migrations** — the gradual spread of Bantu-speaking peoples outward from west-central Africa. By 1000 CE, most of Sub-Saharan Africa had adopted agriculture. Agriculture required more complex political arrangements than foraging, but Sub-Saharan Africa did not follow the Asian or European model of centralization under a single ruler.

Instead, communities formed **kin-based networks**: families governed themselves; a chief mediated conflicts; groups of chiefs managed district-level problems. These structures were flexible but vulnerable — as populations grew and competition increased, larger kingdoms became necessary for survival.

> [!tip]
> The kin-based network structure is fundamentally different from Chinese, Islamic, or European political organization. Don't describe it as "primitive" on an AP essay — describe it as a distinct model of political organization suited to decentralized, agrarian societies.

### The Hausa Kingdoms (before 1000 CE, Nigeria)

Seven loosely connected Hausa city-states in what is now Nigeria, linked by kinship but lacking central authority. Each city-state had a specialty (e.g., cotton growing, military defense). Benefited from trans-Saharan trade. Vulnerable to outside domination due to lack of central authority. Islam introduced in the 14th century by missionaries.

## West Africa: Ghana and Mali

### Ghana (peaked 8th–11th centuries)

- Located between the Sahara and the tropical rainforests — not in the location of modern Ghana
- Founded at least two centuries before Muhammad (5th century CE)
- Rulers sold **gold and ivory** to Muslim traders in exchange for salt, copper, cloth, and tools
- Central government aided by nobles and an iron-armed military
- Capital: **Koumbi Saleh**
- Weakened by 12th century wars with neighboring societies

### Mali (12th–14th centuries)

Mali arose as Ghana weakened. The founding ruler **Sundiata** — a Muslim — used his religious connections to establish trade relationships with North African and Arab merchants, building a thriving gold trade. His nephew **Mansa Musa** became famous across the Islamic world for his 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca, during which his lavish displays of gold caused inflation in Egypt and North Africa.

> [!exam-tip]
> Mansa Musa's pilgrimage is one of the most tested events in AP World History for this period. It demonstrates: (1) the wealth of West African states from gold trade; (2) the integration of West Africa into Islamic trade and cultural networks; (3) the gold trade's global economic impact.

For the later developments of Mali (Timbuktu, the Songhai Empire), see [[Unit-2.4/West African Empires]].

## East Africa: Zimbabwe and the Indian Ocean

### Zimbabwe (12th–15th centuries)

Located between the Zambezi and Limpopo rivers (modern Zimbabwe and Mozambique), Zimbabwe built its prosperity on **agriculture, grazing, trade, and gold**. Unlike Ghana and Mali (which used trans-Saharan routes), Zimbabwe traded through **East African coastal city-states** — Mombasa, Kilwa, Mogadishu — connecting to the **Indian Ocean trade network** linking East Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, and East Asia.

In East Africa, traders blended Bantu and Arabic languages to create **Swahili** — still spoken widely in East and Central Africa today.

The kingdom's power is most visible in its architecture: a massive stone wall (30 feet tall, 15 feet thick) surrounding the capital city — built **without mortar**, the first large such structure on the continent. This was the **Great Zimbabwe**. In the late 15th century, nearly 20,000 people lived within it. The city was abandoned in the 1400s due to **overgrazing** damaging the surrounding environment.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse Great Zimbabwe (the archaeological site) with the modern nation of Zimbabwe. The site gave the country its name — but the people who built Great Zimbabwe are a distinct historical subject.

### Ethiopia

Christianity spread from Egypt southward into **Axum** (modern Ethiopia), where a prosperous kingdom traded goods from India, Arabia, the Roman Empire, and interior Africa. Beginning in the 7th century, the spread of Islam made the region more religiously diverse.

In the 12th century, a new Christian kingdom in Ethiopia emerged and expressed its power through architecture: **11 massive churches carved entirely from rock**. From the 12th through 16th centuries, Ethiopia was "an island of Christianity on the continent of Africa" — separated from both Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches, developing an **independent form of Christianity** that blended traditional faith traditions (ancestor veneration, beliefs in spirits) with Christian theology.

## Social Structures in Sub-Saharan Africa

Sub-Saharan African communities were organized around three key social structures: **kinship, age, and gender**.

- **Kinship**: People identified first as members of a clan or family
- **Age sets/grades**: Communities divided work according to age — younger people had physical capacity but relied on elders' wisdom
- **Gender**: Men dominated skilled crafts (leather tanning, blacksmithing); women engaged in agriculture, food gathering, domestic work, and child-rearing

### Slavery

Slavery had a long history in Africa (prisoners of war, debtors, criminals). **Three forms of slavery** coexisted in the broader world:

| Form | Where Common | Hereditary? |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| Chattel (legal property) | Americas (16th–19th c.) | Yes |
| Household service | Classical Greece/Rome, Middle East | Often not |
| Debt slavery | East Africa before 15th c., European colonies | Not in theory (but often permanent) |

In East Africa, a specific slave trade linked East African enslaved people to the Middle East. **Enslaved East Africans (zanj)** worked on sugar plantations in Mesopotamia. Between **869 and 883**, they and Arab workers staged the **Zanj Rebellion** — capturing the city of Basra and holding it for 10 years before defeat. One of the largest and longest-lasting slave revolts in history.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Indian Ocean slave trade predated the Atlantic slave trade by centuries. It's an important continuity: East African enslaved labor was exported to the Islamic world from the 9th century onward — eventually including the 19th century, long after the Atlantic trade had formally ended.

## Cultural Life: Music, Art, and Griots

### Arts as Religious and Social Practice

African music and visual arts served religious and social purposes beyond entertainment. Because traditional African religions included **ancestor veneration**, songs provided a means of communicating with the spirit world. African music featured distinctive rhythmic patterns with percussive elements (handclaps, bells, pots, gourds).

Visual arts served religious purposes: metalworkers in Benin (West Africa) created bronze and iron sculptures of past rulers so royalty could seek guidance from ancestors. These sculptures were so sophisticated that 19th-century Europeans initially refused to believe Africans had made them.

### Griots and Griottes

**Griots** were oral storytellers — the conduits of history and memory for their communities. They possessed encyclopedic knowledge of family lineages and leaders' deeds. Griots were also musicians, singing stories accompanied by instruments like the **kora** (12-string harp) and drums.

Griots held enormous social power — "a griot could sing your success or sing your downfall." Kings sought their counsel. **When a griot died, it was as though a library had burned.**

**Griottes** were women who performed the same function, particularly at significant social occasions (weddings, coming-of-age ceremonies). They empowered women within patriarchal societies by providing them with a voice.

> [!tip]
> Griots represent **oral tradition** as an alternative to written records. AP documents about Sub-Saharan Africa often come from outside observers (like Ibn Battuta) or archaeological evidence — indigenous perspectives are underrepresented in written form. This is itself an AP point about limitations of the historical record.
