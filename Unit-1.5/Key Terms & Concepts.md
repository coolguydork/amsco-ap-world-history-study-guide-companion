# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.5

*AMSCO pp. 43–52 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Political & Social Terms

**Kin-based networks (stateless societies)**
The dominant form of political organization in sub-Saharan Africa. Communities governed themselves through family and clan relationships — a male chief mediated conflicts, and groups of chiefs resolved district-level problems. No centralized ruler governed large territories. Sometimes called "acephalous" (headless) political structure. (AMSCO pp. 43–44)

**Age grades (age sets)**
Social divisions within African communities based on age, which determined roles and responsibilities. Younger people provided heavy labor; older people provided advice and governance. Age grades cut across family lines, creating community-wide bonds.

**Chief**
In kin-based African societies, the male head of a kinship network who mediated conflicts and dealt with neighboring groups. Chiefs were selected from within the community and held authority through consensus, not conquest. (AMSCO p. 43)

**Hausa Kingdoms**
Seven loosely connected city-states in what is now northern Nigeria, formed before 1000 CE. Each had a different economic specialty; they benefited from trans-Saharan trade but lacked central authority and were frequently dominated by outside powers. Islam arrived via missionaries in the 14th century. (AMSCO p. 44)

## Trade & Economic Terms

**Trans-Saharan trade**
The network of trade routes crossing the Sahara Desert connecting sub-Saharan West Africa to North Africa and the Mediterranean world. West African kingdoms — Ghana, Mali, Songhai — grew wealthy by controlling the exchange of gold and salt across these routes. Camel caravans made this trade possible after c. 300 CE. (AMSCO p. 44)

**Gold-salt trade**
The specific exchange that drove West African state power. West African kingdoms possessed abundant gold but scarce salt; North African and Saharan regions had salt but needed gold. Rulers who controlled the exchange points became enormously wealthy. Ghana and Mali were built on this trade. (AMSCO p. 45)

**Indian Ocean slave trade**
The trade in enslaved East Africans (*zanj*) to the Middle East, which preceded the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] by centuries. Enslaved East Africans provided labor on sugar plantations in Mesopotamia. The Zanj Rebellion (869–883 CE) — in which enslaved people captured and held the city of Basra for a decade — was one of the largest slave revolts in history. (AMSCO p. 48)

**Swahili**
A Bantu-based language with significant Arabic vocabulary, developed along the East African coast through centuries of interaction between Bantu-speaking Africans and Arab merchants. Today spoken by tens of millions across East Africa. The language itself is a product of cultural synthesis through trade. (AMSCO p. 46)

## State & Kingdom Terms

**Ghana** (c. 5th–11th centuries CE)
West African kingdom that preceded Mali; not in the same location as modern Ghana. Controlled the gold-salt trade at its peak (8th–11th centuries). Capital: Koumbi Saleh. Centralized government with iron-armed military; sold gold and ivory to Muslim traders. Permanently weakened by 12th-century warfare. (AMSCO p. 45)

**Mali Empire** (c. 1235–1600)
West African empire that succeeded Ghana; founded by Sundiata Keita. Controlled trans-Saharan gold trade. Mansa Musa's hajj (1324) displayed its wealth to the world. See [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview|Topic 2.4]] for Timbuktu and Songhai. (AMSCO p. 46)

**Great Zimbabwe** (12th–15th centuries)
Most powerful East African kingdom of the period; located between the Zambezi and Limpopo rivers. Built prosperity on gold, grazing, and taxation of Indian Ocean trade passing through coastal ports. Defining monument: a massive dry-stone wall (30 ft. tall, 15 ft. thick) surrounding the capital — built without mortar. Collapsed due to overgrazing/environmental degradation by end of 1400s. (AMSCO pp. 46–47)

**Ethiopia** (ancient Axum through 16th c.)
Christian kingdom in the Horn of Africa; one of the world's oldest Christian kingdoms (converted in 4th century CE under King Ezana). From the 12th–16th centuries, an isolated Christian nation on a continent increasingly Islamic. Ethiopian Christianity developed independently from both Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions. Known for 11 rock-hewn churches built in the 12th century. (AMSCO pp. 46–47)

**Swahili city-states** (Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu, Malindi)
East African port cities deeply integrated into the Indian Ocean trade network; connected East Africa to the Middle East, South Asia, and East Asia. Served as the commercial link through which Great Zimbabwe and the interior accessed global trade. (AMSCO p. 46)

## Cultural Terms

**Griots / Griottes**
Professional oral historians, genealogists, and musicians in West Africa. Griots (male) and griottes (female) memorized family lineages and rulers' deeds; sang their histories to music; counseled kings on political matters. Served as the primary vehicle for preserving and transmitting historical knowledge in the absence of a written literary tradition. The famous saying: "When a griot dies, it is as if a library has burned." (AMSCO p. 49)

**Animism**
Belief that elements of the physical world — rivers, mountains, objects — have spiritual power. Widely practiced in sub-Saharan Africa before and during the arrival of Islam. African societies that converted to Islam often maintained animist practices alongside Islamic ones — a form of religious syncretism. (See also [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|Topic 1.4]] — Inca animism: *huaca*)

**Bantu-speaking peoples**
The Bantu migration — the centuries-long expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples from west-central Africa southward and eastward — shaped sub-Saharan Africa's political, linguistic, and agricultural landscape. By c. 1000 CE, Bantu-speakers had carried agriculture and kin-based governance across most of sub-Saharan Africa. (AMSCO p. 43)

**Ancestor veneration**
The practice of honoring deceased ancestors, often through ritual, as a means of maintaining a relationship with the spiritual world. Central to traditional sub-Saharan African religions. Also practiced in Ethiopia, where it was blended into Christian practice. (Compare: Inca *royal ancestor veneration* in [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|Topic 1.4]])

**Zanj Rebellion** (869–883 CE)
A major revolt of enslaved East Africans (*zanj*) in Mesopotamia (modern Iraq). About 15,000 enslaved people captured the city of Basra and held it for ten years before defeat. One of the largest slave revolts in history. Demonstrates both the scale of the pre-Atlantic slave trade and the agency of enslaved people in resisting their condition. (AMSCO p. 48)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam tests **slavery in Africa** in the context of showing that the Atlantic slave trade was not the beginning of African enslavement — it was a new phase of a long-standing institution. Know the three forms of slavery AMSCO describes (chattel, domestic, debt bondage) and how they differed in terms of permanence, inheritance, and rights.
