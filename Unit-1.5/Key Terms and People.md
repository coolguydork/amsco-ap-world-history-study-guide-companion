# Topic 1.5 — Key Terms and People

## Core Vocabulary

**Bantu migrations**
The gradual spread of Bantu-speaking peoples outward from west-central Africa, reaching most of Sub-Saharan Africa by 1000 CE. They carried agriculture southward and eastward, enabling more complex political organization.

**Kin-based networks**
The dominant form of political organization in Sub-Saharan Africa — communities governed by families, with a chief mediating conflicts. More decentralized than Asian or European political systems. Not inferior — a distinct model suited to the social structure.

**Trans-Saharan trade**
Trade routes crossing the Sahara Desert connecting West Africa to North Africa, the Mediterranean, and the Islamic world. The engine of wealth for Ghana and Mali. Key goods: gold (from West Africa) and salt (from the Sahara), plus cloth, copper, tools.

**Ghana (c. 5th century–12th century)**
The first major West African kingdom. Sold gold and ivory to Muslim traders for salt, copper, cloth, and tools. Centralized government; capital at Koumbi Saleh. Peaked in the 8th–11th centuries. Weakened by wars with neighbors.

**Mali (c. 12th–14th centuries)**
West African empire that replaced Ghana. Founded by Sundiata; famous for Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca. Thriving gold trade. Center of Islamic scholarship (Timbuktu). See also [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade|Trans-Saharan Trade (Topic 2.4)]].

**Sundiata**
Founding ruler of Mali. A Muslim who used Islamic connections to build trade relationships with North African and Arab merchants.

**Mansa Musa**
Sundiata's nephew; ruler of Mali who made a famous pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, accompanied by an entourage carrying enormous amounts of gold. Caused inflation across North Africa and the Middle East by distributing so much gold. Put Mali "on the map" of the Islamic world — literally (Mali appears on Islamic and European maps after his pilgrimage).

**Great Zimbabwe**
The capital city of the Zimbabwe kingdom, encircled by a massive stone wall (30 feet tall, 15 feet thick, no mortar). At its peak, ~20,000 residents. Abandoned in the late 1400s due to overgrazing of the surrounding environment.

**Swahili**
A language blending Bantu and Arabic, developed among traders on the East African coast. Reflects the cultural synthesis produced by Indian Ocean trade. Still widely spoken across East and Central Africa today.

**Indian Ocean slave trade**
The trade of enslaved East Africans (zanj) to the Middle East, predating the Atlantic slave trade by centuries. These enslaved people worked primarily on sugar plantations in Mesopotamia.

**Zanj Rebellion (869–883)**
A massive revolt of enslaved East Africans and Arab workers in Mesopotamia. Captured the city of Basra and held it for 10 years. One of history's largest and most successful slave revolts.

**Griots / Griottes**
Oral historians and storytellers in West Africa. Possessed encyclopedic knowledge of lineages and leaders' deeds; served as musicians, counselors to kings, and keepers of historical memory. Women (griottes) performed similar roles at social occasions. "When a griot died, it was as though a library burned."

**Ancestor veneration**
The practice of honoring deceased ancestors, particularly in African traditional religions. Informed the arts (sculptural busts of rulers), oral traditions (griots communicating with the spirit world), and Ethiopian Christianity (which blended ancestor veneration with Christian theology).

**Axum / Ethiopia**
Kingdom in what is today Ethiopia that adopted Christianity. In the 12th century, rulers ordered the construction of 11 churches carved entirely from rock. Ethiopia remained a Christian island in Africa from the 12th through 16th centuries, developing an independent form of Christianity.

**Age sets / age grades**
Social organization based on age groups in Sub-Saharan African communities. Divided work (physical labor for young; wisdom/decision-making for elders) and created social bonds across kin groups.

## Key People

**Ibn Battuta (c. 1352, Morocco)**
Islamic scholar and traveler from Morocco who visited Mali and wrote detailed accounts of its society. A primary source for West African history in this period — but written from an outside Islamic perspective, not an indigenous West African one.

**Sundiata**
Mali's founding ruler. A Muslim who used Islamic trade connections to expand the gold trade. His story (told by griots) is the most famous oral epic in West African history.

**Mansa Musa**
The most internationally famous African ruler of this period. His 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca demonstrated Mali's extraordinary wealth and placed it on the world map — literally.
