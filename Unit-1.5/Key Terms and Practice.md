# Topic 1.5 — Key Terms, Concepts & Practice

## Core Vocabulary

**Bantu migrations**
The gradual spread of Bantu-speaking peoples outward from west-central Africa, reaching most of Sub-Saharan Africa by 1000 CE. They carried agriculture southward and eastward, enabling more complex political organization.

**Kin-based networks**
The dominant form of political organization in Sub-Saharan Africa — communities governed by families, with a chief mediating conflicts. More decentralized than Asian or European political systems. Not inferior — a distinct model suited to the social structure.

**Trans-Saharan trade**
Trade routes crossing the Sahara Desert connecting West Africa to North Africa, the Mediterranean, and the Islamic world. The engine of wealth for Ghana and Mali. Key goods: gold (from West Africa) and salt (from the Sahara), plus cloth, copper, tools.

**Ghana (c. 5th century–12th century)**
The first major West African kingdom. Sold gold and ivory to Muslim traders for salt, copper, cloth, and tools. Centralized government; capital at Koumbi Saleh. Peaked in the 8th–11th centuries. Weakened by wars with neighbors.

**Mali (c. 12th–14th centuries)**
West African empire that replaced Ghana. Founded by Sundiata; famous for Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca. Thriving gold trade. Center of Islamic scholarship (Timbuktu). See also [[Unit-2.4/West African Empires]].

**Sundiata**
Founding ruler of Mali. A Muslim who used Islamic connections to build trade relationships with North African and Arab merchants.

**Mansa Musa**
Sundiata's nephew; ruler of Mali who made a famous pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, accompanied by an entourage carrying enormous amounts of gold. Caused inflation across North Africa and the Middle East by distributing so much gold. Put Mali "on the map" of the Islamic world — literally (Mali appears on Islamic and European maps after his pilgrimage).

**Great Zimbabwe**
The capital city of the Zimbabwe kingdom, encircled by a massive stone wall (30 feet tall, 15 feet thick, no mortar). At its peak, ~20,000 residents. Abandoned in the late 1400s due to overgrazing of the surrounding environment.

**Swahili**
A language blending Bantu and Arabic, developed among traders on the East African coast. Reflects the cultural synthesis produced by Indian Ocean trade. Still widely spoken across East and Central Africa today.

**Indian Ocean slave trade**
The trade of enslaved East Africans (zanj) to the Middle East, predating the Atlantic slave trade by centuries. These enslaved people worked primarily on sugar plantations in Mesopotamia.

**Zanj Rebellion (869–883)**
A massive revolt of enslaved East Africans and Arab workers in Mesopotamia. Captured the city of Basra and held it for 10 years. One of history's largest and most successful slave revolts.

**Griots / Griottes**
Oral historians and storytellers in West Africa. Possessed encyclopedic knowledge of lineages and leaders' deeds; served as musicians, counselors to kings, and keepers of historical memory. Women (griottes) performed similar roles at social occasions. "When a griot died, it was as though a library burned."

**Ancestor veneration**
The practice of honoring deceased ancestors, particularly in African traditional religions. Informed the arts (sculptural busts of rulers), oral traditions (griots communicating with the spirit world), and Ethiopian Christianity (which blended ancestor veneration with Christian theology).

**Axum / Ethiopia**
Kingdom in what is today Ethiopia that adopted Christianity. In the 12th century, rulers ordered the construction of 11 churches carved entirely from rock. Ethiopia remained a Christian island in Africa from the 12th through 16th centuries, developing an independent form of Christianity.

**Age sets / age grades**
Social organization based on age groups in Sub-Saharan African communities. Divided work (physical labor for young; wisdom/decision-making for elders) and created social bonds across kin groups.

## Key People

**Ibn Battuta (c. 1352, Morocco)**
Islamic scholar and traveler from Morocco who visited Mali and wrote detailed accounts of its society. A primary source for West African history in this period — but written from an outside Islamic perspective, not an indigenous West African one.

**Sundiata**
See above. Mali's founding ruler. His story (told by griots) is the most famous oral epic in West African history.

**Mansa Musa**
See above. The most internationally famous African ruler of this period.

## AP Multiple Choice Practice

**Question 1.** The wealth of West African kingdoms like Ghana and Mali was most directly based on:

A) Naval control of Atlantic Ocean trade routes  
B) Agricultural surplus from the Sahara region  
C) Control of gold and trans-Saharan trade routes connecting West Africa to North Africa  
D) Conquest and tribute from North African Islamic states

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Ghana and Mali sat at the southern end of trans-Saharan trade — controlling the flow of gold northward in exchange for salt and manufactured goods. A is wrong (Atlantic trade came much later). B is wrong (the Sahara is not agriculturally productive). D reverses the power relationship.

---

**Question 2.** The Great Zimbabwe and the Mansa Musa's pilgrimage to Mecca are both best understood as evidence of:

A) The weakness of African states in the face of Islamic expansion  
B) The wealth and sophistication of African kingdoms built on trade connections  
C) The dependence of African kingdoms on European technology  
D) The decline of sub-Saharan Africa due to environmental degradation

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Great Zimbabwe's massive stone walls demonstrate architectural sophistication and urban wealth (from Indian Ocean gold trade). Mansa Musa's lavish gold display demonstrates Mali's extraordinary wealth from trans-Saharan gold trade. Both are evidence of sophisticated African states, not weakness or dependence.

---

**Question 3.** The role of griots in West African societies most closely parallels which function in other societies?

A) The role of priests in maintaining religious rituals  
B) The role of scribes and scholars in preserving written records  
C) The role of merchants in facilitating long-distance trade  
D) The role of military commanders in defending territory

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Griots were the primary keepers of historical and genealogical knowledge — the functional equivalent of scribes and scholars in literate societies. Their medium was oral rather than written, but their social function (preserving, transmitting, and interpreting the past) was the same.

## SAQ: State-Building in Africa

**Prompt:** Explain how trade contributed to state-building in Africa in the period c. 1200–c. 1450. Use at least TWO specific pieces of evidence.

**Model Response:**
Trade was the primary driver of state formation in Sub-Saharan Africa during this period. Two examples illustrate this clearly.

First, in West Africa, control over the trans-Saharan gold trade enabled Ghana and later Mali to build wealthy, centralized states. Ghana's rulers taxed the movement of gold northward through their territory, funding a centralized government, a noble class, and an iron-armed military. Mali's Sundiata used Islamic merchant networks to expand the gold trade further, and under Mansa Musa, Mali became internationally famous for its wealth — demonstrated by the 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca that caused inflation across North Africa.

Second, in East Africa, Zimbabwe built its wealth through the Indian Ocean trade. Its rulers taxed the flow of gold through coastal city-states like Kilwa and Mombasa, connecting East Africa to the Middle East, South Asia, and East Asia. The surplus wealth funded construction of the Great Zimbabwe — a massive stone-walled capital city housing nearly 20,000 people. Both cases show that access to and control of trade routes translated directly into political power and state complexity in Africa during this period.

## CCOT Frame: Sub-Saharan Africa c. 1200–c. 1450

| Category | Continuity | Change |
|----------|------------|--------|
| Political structure | Kin-based networks persisted in many areas | Larger kingdoms grew (Mali replacing Ghana; Zimbabwe rising) |
| Trade | Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade continued | Mali's gold trade expanded dramatically under Mansa Musa |
| Religion | Animism, ancestor veneration in many areas | Islam spread into West and East Africa via trade and missionaries |
| Architecture | Earthen and wooden construction | Stone construction emerged (Great Zimbabwe; Ethiopian rock churches) |
| Language | Bantu languages widely spoken | Swahili developed (Bantu + Arabic) as a trade lingua franca |
