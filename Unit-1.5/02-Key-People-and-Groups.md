# Unit 1.5: Key People and Groups — State Building in Africa (1200–1450)

## Individuals and Groups

### Sundiata Keita
**Period:** r. c. 1235–1255 | **Region:** West Africa (Mali Empire)

- Legendary founder of the Mali Empire who united Mande-speaking peoples and defeated the Sosso king Sumanguru Kante at the Battle of Kirina (c. 1235)
- His story, preserved in the Epic of Sundiata by griots, combines historical fact with mythological elements, demonstrating how oral traditions served as West Africa's primary historical record
- While Sundiata himself likely practiced a blended religion (traditional African beliefs combined with Islam), he established the framework within which later Mali rulers would embrace Islam more fully

---

### Mansa Musa
**Period:** r. 1312–1337 | **Region:** Mali Empire, West Africa

- The most famous ruler of the Mali Empire; his 1324–1325 hajj to Mecca is one of history's most spectacular demonstrations of royal wealth and power
- His caravan reportedly included 60,000 men, 12,000 enslaved servants, and 80 camels carrying gold — distributing so much gold in Cairo that Egyptian gold prices collapsed for a decade afterward
- He returned from Mecca with Islamic scholars and the Andalusian architect Abu Ishaq al-Sahili, who designed mosques and palaces in Mali including the famous Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu
- Put Mali on European maps for the first time (appearing on the 1375 Catalan Atlas as a magnificent golden king)

> [!exam-tip]
> Mansa Musa is one of the AP exam's most-tested African figures. Be ready to discuss his hajj as an example of: (1) Islamic connectivity, (2) West African wealth, (3) cultural exchange, and (4) the global consequences of individual actions.

---

### Mali Provincial Governors and Administrators
**Period:** 13th–14th century | **Region:** Mali Empire

- Mali's administrative system relied on provincial governors (farari) who were often members of the royal family or loyal military commanders
- Mali's governance combined traditional Mande political structures with Islamic administrative practices introduced by Muslim scholars at Timbuktu and Djenné
- The empire's stability depended on controlling the gold fields of Bambuk and Bure and the trans-Saharan trade routes — the mansa who controlled these controlled West Africa

---

### Kilwa Sultanate
**Period:** c. 957–1513 | **Region:** Swahili Coast, East Africa

- The dominant city-state on the Swahili Coast, controlling the gold trade from the Zimbabwe Plateau
- Ibn Battuta visited in 1331 and described it as one of the most beautiful cities in the world; its ruler received him as a fellow Muslim and treated him to formal hospitality reflecting Islamic cosmopolitan culture
- Kilwa's prosperity was built on its role as the entrepôt between inland gold producers (Great Zimbabwe and successor states) and Indian Ocean merchants from Arabia, Persia, and India

---

### Great Zimbabwe State
**Period:** c. 1100–1450 | **Region:** Zimbabwe Plateau, Southern Africa

- A powerful African state centered on the Great Zimbabwe stone enclosures, controlling gold production and trade to the Swahili Coast
- Built without mortar using precisely fitted granite stones; the Great Enclosure's walls are over 11 meters high and 250 meters in circumference — one of sub-Saharan Africa's greatest architectural achievements
- Its rulers (likely the Shona-speaking Karanga people) extracted tribute from surrounding agricultural communities and taxed the gold trade passing through their territory
- European colonial-era archaeologists refused to believe Africans had built Great Zimbabwe, attributing it to Phoenicians or other outside groups — a racist misconception long since disproven

---

### Zagwe and Solomonid Dynasties (Ethiopia)
**Period:** c. 900–1270 (Zagwe) / 1270–present (Solomonid) | **Region:** Ethiopian Highlands

- The Zagwe dynasty commissioned the extraordinary rock-hewn churches of Lalibela in the 12th–13th centuries, presenting them as the "New Jerusalem" for Ethiopian Christian pilgrims who could not travel to the actual Jerusalem during the Crusades
- The Solomonid dynasty (restored 1270) claimed descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba through Menelik I, providing powerful divine legitimacy
- Ethiopia maintained its Christian identity and political independence despite being surrounded by expanding Islamic states — a remarkable example of African cultural resilience

---

### Sunni Ali and Songhai (context)
**Period:** Songhai rising in the 15th century | **Region:** West Africa

- While Songhai's dominance comes after 1450, its rise is foreshadowed in this period as Mali's power weakened following Mansa Musa's death and internal succession conflicts
- Understanding the *beginning* of Mali's decline (mid-14th century) is important for understanding the transition to Songhai Empire under Sunni Ali (1464–1492)
- Demonstrates how West African empire-building was cyclical: Ghana → Mali → Songhai, each built on the same gold-salt trade foundation

---

### Women in African States
**Period:** 1200–1450 | **Region:** Various African regions

- The Queen Mother (or Queen Sister) played a formal political role in several West African states, including serving as a check on the king's power and participating in governance
- Among the Swahili Coast communities, women participated in trade and owned property in ways that reflected both African and Islamic influences
- In Great Zimbabwe, archaeological evidence suggests women played important roles in ritual life associated with the royal enclosure
- African women's roles were generally more varied and sometimes more powerful than stereotypes suggest
