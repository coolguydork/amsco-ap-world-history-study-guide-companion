# Unit 1.5: Developments In Africa (1200–1450)

**AMSCO Unit:** Unit 1 — The Global Tapestry
**AP Exam Period:** Period 1 (1200–1450)
**Key Regions:** West Africa (Mali Empire), East Africa (Swahili Coast), Southern Africa (Great Zimbabwe), Northeast Africa (Ethiopia/Axum)

## Summary

Africa in 1200–1450 was home to some of the world's most powerful and sophisticated states, yet it is often underrepresented in world history courses. The Mali Empire in West Africa controlled vast gold resources and hosted one of the most wealthy and culturally vibrant courts in the world, as demonstrated by Mansa Musa's spectacular 1324 hajj. The Swahili city-states along the East African coast were thriving Islamic commercial centers fully integrated into the Indian Ocean trading network. In southern Africa, the Zimbabwe Plateau supported the Great Zimbabwe state, whose stone enclosures remain among Africa's most impressive architectural monuments. Ethiopian Christianity maintained a distinct African Christian tradition. These states demonstrate that Africa was not isolated from global networks — it was deeply embedded in them.

Beyond state politics and trade, African societies of this period were structured by **kinship systems** — lineage groups, clans, and age grades that organized labor, settled disputes, and transmitted cultural memory in both state and stateless societies. Many Bantu-speaking communities across sub-Saharan Africa lived in decentralized, kinship-based societies with no central government, demonstrating that complex social organization does not require a state. **Oral traditions** — preserved by griots in West Africa and analogous specialists elsewhere — were the primary vehicle for historical knowledge, religious practice, and cultural identity. **Gender roles** varied by region, but women in many West African societies participated in markets, led religious rituals, and occasionally held recognized political authority as queen mothers. **Religious syncretism** was widespread: Islam, introduced via trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean merchants, blended with indigenous African spiritual traditions rather than replacing them, producing hybrid religious practices that persist today.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain the diversity of African states and their connections to global trade networks
- Analyze how Islam shaped governance and culture in West and East African states, including patterns of religious syncretism
- Describe the role of kinship, age grades, and stateless societies alongside centralized states in African social organization
- Evaluate the significance of oral traditions, gender structures, and cultural life in African societies of this period
- Explain the role of the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade networks in African state formation and cultural exchange

## AP World History Key Concepts

- **KC-1.2.I** — Islam's spread to sub-Saharan Africa shaped political authority and cultural identity
- **KC-1.3.II** — Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade networks integrated African states into global commerce
- **KC-1.2.II** — African rulers used religious ideology (Islam, indigenous religions, Christianity) to legitimize political authority
