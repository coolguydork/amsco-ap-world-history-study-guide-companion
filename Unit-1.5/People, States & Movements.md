# People, States & Movements — Topic 1.5

*AMSCO pp. 43–52 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Key People

### Sundiata Keita (r. c. 1235–1255) — Mali Empire
Founding ruler of the Mali Empire. A Muslim, Sundiata used his religious connections with other Muslims to establish trade relationships with North African and Arab merchants, building Mali's gold trade. His life is preserved in the **Sundiata epic** — one of the most famous works of West African oral literature, passed down by griots for centuries. Embodies both the political-economic and the cultural dimensions of this topic: state-building through trade, and history preserved through oral tradition.

### Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337) — Mali Empire
The most famous ruler of Mali. His **hajj** (pilgrimage) to Mecca in **1324** is one of the most celebrated events in medieval African history. He traveled with an enormous entourage — estimates range from 60,000 to 80,000 people — and distributed so much gold along the route through Cairo and the Middle East that he reportedly caused inflation (gold devaluation) across North Africa. The image of Mansa Musa holding a gold coin is the primary visual source used for the Topic 1.5 SAQ. His hajj demonstrated Mali's immense wealth to the world and deepened Mali's connection to Islam. **Critical person to know for the AP exam.**

### Sunni Ali (r. 1464–1492) — Songhai Empire
Ruler of the Songhai Empire who conquered Timbuktu (1468) and overthrew Mali's dominance of the western Sudan. Belongs primarily to [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview|Topic 2.4]], but is introduced here as context for Mali's eventual decline. His conquests demonstrated that the trans-Saharan trade zone would continue to generate powerful states — just not always the same one.

### Ibn Battuta (1304–1368/1369)
Moroccan Muslim scholar and traveler who visited Mali around 1352 and wrote extensively about its society, government, and Islamic practices. His travelogue is the opening primary source for Topic 1.5. **Key AP source for "point of view" questions** — he was a scholar steeped in Islamic law (sharia), so he was both impressed by Mali's Islamic piety and critical when Malian practices deviated from strict Islamic norms. He noted that pre-Islamic customs survived even in the Islamic court.

### Ezana of Axum (r. c. 330–356 CE)
King of Axum who converted the kingdom to Christianity in the 4th century CE — establishing Ethiopia as one of the world's oldest Christian kingdoms. While this is before AP Period 1, Ezana's conversion is the foundational context for understanding Ethiopia's unique Christian identity in Topic 1.5. Ethiopia's Christianity in 1200–1450 is a **direct continuity** from Ezana's conversion.

## Key States & Kingdoms

| State | Dates | Region | Political Form | Wealth Source | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ghana** | c. 5th–11th c. | West Africa (modern Mali/Mauritania) | Centralized monarchy | Gold-salt trade | First great trans-Saharan trading kingdom |
| **Mali Empire** | c. 1235–1600 | West Africa | Centralized monarchy | Gold trade; trans-Saharan | Mansa Musa's hajj; Timbuktu (see Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview) |
| **Hausa Kingdoms** | pre-1000 CE+ | Northern Nigeria | Loose city-states (no center) | Trans-Saharan trade | Seven specialized city-states; no central authority |
| **Great Zimbabwe** | 12th–15th c. | East Africa (Zimbabwe/Mozambique) | Monarchy | Gold; Indian Ocean trade | Dry-stone architecture; overgrazing collapse |
| **Swahili city-states** | c. 8th–15th c. | East African coast | Independent merchant city-states | Indian Ocean trade | Swahili language; connected Africa to Asia |
| **Ethiopia** | ancient–present | Horn of Africa | Christian monarchy | Agriculture; trade with Red Sea | Independent Christianity; rock-hewn churches |
| **Kin-based societies** | throughout | Sub-Saharan Africa | Stateless / kin-based | Agriculture | Dominant form of African political organization |

> [!tip]
> Notice the geographic split: **West African kingdoms** (Ghana, Mali) drew their power from **trans-Saharan trade**; **East African kingdoms** (Great Zimbabwe, Swahili city-states) drew their power from **Indian Ocean trade**. The Sahara and Indian Ocean were Africa's two great commercial arteries in this period.

## Key Movements

### Islam in Sub-Saharan Africa
Islam entered sub-Saharan Africa through trade contacts — merchants traveling the trans-Saharan routes and Indian Ocean lanes were the primary vectors. The process was gradual and syncretic: African societies adopted Islam but retained pre-Islamic traditions. Ibn Battuta's criticism that Malian court customs were incompatible with sharia, while Malians considered themselves good Muslims, illustrates this perfectly. The same pattern of **local adaptation** appears in South Asia and Southeast Asia [[Unit-1.3/South & Southeast Asia Overview|South & Southeast Asia Overview]] — Islam as a universalizing religion that shaped itself to local contexts.

### Bantu Migration
Centuries-long expansion of Bantu-speaking peoples from west-central Africa across sub-Saharan Africa, spreading agriculture, the Bantu language family, and kin-based political structures. By c. 1000 CE, this migration had shaped the political and linguistic map of sub-Saharan Africa. The Swahili language is a product of Bantu-Arabic contact on the East African coast.

### The Oral Tradition / Griot System
Functioning as both a cultural institution and a political system, the griot tradition was how sub-Saharan Africa stored and transmitted historical knowledge. Griots were not just entertainers — they were archivists, advisors, and judges of reputation. The tradition's survival across centuries, even into the post-colonial era, makes it one of the great continuities of African cultural history.
