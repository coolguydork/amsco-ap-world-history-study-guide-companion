# Practice & Review — Topic 1.5

*AMSCO pp. 50–52 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to an image of the ruins of the stone wall at Great Zimbabwe. (AMSCO p. 50)*

> **Image description:** The Great Zimbabwe stone enclosure wall — a massive, curving structure built of dry-stacked granite blocks without mortar, roughly 30 feet tall and 15 feet thick at the base.

**1.** One achievement represented by the structure in the image was that it was built

(A) with techniques used by Arabs and the Portuguese
(B) without mortar
(C) without slave labor
(D) away from any large settlement of people

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> AMSCO explicitly states that the Great Zimbabwe wall was "the first large one on the continent that people built without mortar" — dry-stone construction requiring precise fitting of granite blocks. Option A is wrong — the wall predates significant Portuguese contact and was an indigenous achievement, not a product of Arab/Portuguese technique. Option C is wrong — enslaved labor was common in African societies. Option D is wrong — nearly 20,000 people lived within the capital at its peak.

---

**2.** What characteristic of cities around the world from 1200–1450 is reflected in this image?

(A) Cities were reliant on walls for protection against attackers.
(B) Cities were being founded in increasingly dry regions.
(C) Cities were becoming less dependent on trade.
(D) Cities were founded in open areas that were easier to defend.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The Great Zimbabwe wall is a defensive structure surrounding the capital city — consistent with the global pattern of walled cities in this period. Contemporaneous walled cities include Tenochtitlan (with its island geography as defense), Constantinople, and cities throughout China, Europe, and the Middle East. Option B is wrong — Great Zimbabwe was not in an increasingly dry region; overgrazing caused the environmental collapse, not aridity. Option C is wrong — Great Zimbabwe's power was entirely trade-dependent. Option D is wrong — defensive walls typically surround, not open, a city; and Great Zimbabwe's location was determined by gold deposits and trade routes, not openness.

---

**3.** One factor that contributed to the declines of both Great Zimbabwe and of the Maya was

(A) invasions by neighbors with iron weapons
(B) contact with Europeans
(C) environmental damage
(D) ethnic conflicts within the community

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Great Zimbabwe collapsed due to **overgrazing** that destroyed the surrounding environment's carrying capacity. The Maya declined due in significant part to **deforestation and soil exhaustion**. Both are examples of environmental degradation — a recurring pattern across multiple AP topics. Option A is wrong — neither the Maya nor Great Zimbabwe fell to iron-armed invaders. Option B is wrong — European contact did not cause either collapse (it preceded meaningful European presence). Option D is possible for the Maya but not specifically documented for Great Zimbabwe.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Visual Source (Mansa Musa Painting)

**Source:** A 1375 painting by Abraham Cresques showing Mansa Musa holding a gold coin and wearing a gold crown. Buildings in the background have domes characteristic of Middle Eastern architecture.

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which the painting shows a continuity in the sources of wealth of the kingdoms of Ghana and Mali.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity of Wealth
> Both Ghana and Mali derived their wealth primarily from **gold** — Ghana by taxing the gold trade flowing northward across the Sahara, and Mali by controlling the gold-producing regions directly. The painting shows Mansa Musa holding a large gold coin, which directly represents Mali's gold wealth. Since this same gold-based wealth defined Ghana before Mali, the painting illustrates a continuity in the source of West African economic power across both kingdoms.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which the painting shows a connection between the Middle East and West Africa.

> [!tip] Model Response — Middle East Connection
> The dome-shaped buildings in the painting's background are characteristic of Middle Eastern Islamic architecture — not traditional West African building styles. Their presence in a painting of Mansa Musa's court reflects the **cultural influence of Islam** on Mali's ruling class, acquired through trade and pilgrimage connections with North Africa and the Middle East. Mansa Musa's hajj to Mecca in 1324 was the most direct expression of this connection — he traveled through Cairo and the Middle East, absorbing Islamic cultural influence that he brought back to Mali, including new architectural ideas visible in the buildings around him in the painting.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which the painting shows the importance of Mansa Musa.

> [!tip] Model Response — Importance of Mansa Musa
> The painting shows Mansa Musa as a figure of **extraordinary wealth and Islamic legitimacy** — he holds a gold coin (demonstrating Mali's riches), wears a gold crown (marking royal authority), and is surrounded by Islamic architectural symbols (demonstrating his position as a Muslim ruler of a prosperous kingdom). His 1324 hajj to Mecca, on which he reportedly distributed enough gold to depress gold prices across North Africa, made Mali's wealth famous throughout the Islamic world and Europe. The painting's fame — it appeared in a 1375 European atlas — shows how thoroughly Mansa Musa's hajj had placed Mali on the global map of important polities.

### SAQ 2 — Non-Source-Based (African Political Structures)

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which the political structures in inland Africa **differed** from those in East Africa in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Political Difference
> In inland sub-Saharan Africa, most political organization was **kin-based and decentralized** — communities were governed through kinship networks, local chiefs, and collective decision-making among groups of villages, with no central authority controlling large territories. In contrast, East Africa's Swahili coast featured **city-state governments** — urban centers like Kilwa and Mombasa controlled by merchant elites integrated into the Indian Ocean trade network. These city-states had more formalized governance structures oriented toward administering commercial activity, not kinship-based social networks.

**(B)** Identify ONE way in which the political structures in West Africa were **similar** to those in East Africa in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Political Similarity
> Both West African trading kingdoms (like Mali) and East African kingdoms (like Great Zimbabwe) derived their **political power from control of trade** rather than solely from military conquest or agricultural dominance. Mali's rulers taxed gold and salt caravans crossing their territory; Great Zimbabwe's rulers taxed gold transported to the Swahili coastal ports. In both cases, the ability to tax and control commerce was the primary source of state wealth and thus political power.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which **slavery affected relations among African states** in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Slavery and Inter-State Relations
> The demand for enslaved people in the Middle East created an Indian Ocean slave trade that connected East African states to external markets, creating economic incentives for inter-state conflict within Africa. States that captured prisoners in warfare could sell them to Arab merchants, making warfare a potentially profitable enterprise — not just for territory but for human commodities. This economic incentive for capturing and selling enslaved people from neighboring communities contributed to tension and conflict among African states in the period, as some polities raided others specifically to obtain enslaved people for sale into the Indian Ocean trade network.

## Historical Reasoning Practice — Continuity

*AMSCO p. 52 | AP Skill: Explain the Historical Concept of Continuity*

Continuity describes processes and developments that **endure over time** — patterns that persist despite other changes. Applying it to Africa:

> [!exam-tip]
> **Continuity ≠ unchanging.** A continuity can evolve in its details while maintaining its core pattern. The trans-Saharan gold trade is a continuity even though the specific kingdoms controlling it changed (Ghana → Mali → Songhai). The *trade pattern* persisted; the *ruling powers* changed. Always distinguish what continued from what changed within the same phenomenon.

**Practice Statements — apply the concept of continuity:**

1. "For more than 500 years, trans-Saharan trade brought considerable wealth to the societies of West Africa, particularly the kingdoms of Ghana and Mali."

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity 1
> This illustrates continuity in **economic systems**: the trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt persisted as the primary source of West African state wealth across multiple centuries and multiple ruling kingdoms. Even as political power transferred from Ghana to Mali to Songhai, the underlying trade network — and the pattern of kings taxing that trade — remained constant. The trade's durability demonstrates how geography (the Sahara as barrier requiring specific crossing points) created enduring economic structures that outlasted individual states.

2. "Sub-Saharan societies that had converted to Islam did not adopt all of its norms concerning gender."

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity 2
> This illustrates continuity in **social structures and gender roles**. As Islam spread into sub-Saharan Africa, it introduced new religious practices and political concepts — but it did not fundamentally alter the existing gender organization of African societies. Just as Topic 1.3 notes that Islam in Southeast Asia adapted to local gender norms (where women had more independence), Islam in sub-Saharan Africa adapted to existing patterns of gender-based division of labor. Pre-Islamic gender roles — women in agriculture and domestic work; men in specialized skilled trades — persisted through and beyond the period of conversion.
