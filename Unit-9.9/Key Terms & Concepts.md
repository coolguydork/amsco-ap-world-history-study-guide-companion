# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 9.9 — Continuity & Change in a Globalized World | AMSCO pp. 707–720**

> [!exam-tip]
> This is a synthesis chapter — terms here are drawn from the narrative and AP skill framework, not a YAML key terms list. Focus on the CCOT vocabulary and the analytical concepts used to frame arguments about change and continuity across 1900–present.

---

**Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)**
The core AP historical reasoning skill for this topic. CCOT analysis requires identifying what transformed and what endured across a defined time period, explaining *why* changes occurred and *why* some things persisted. Strong AP responses name specific continuities alongside changes — not just "things got better" but "X changed while Y remained constant."

**Big Bang theory**
The dominant scientific theory explaining the origin of the universe as emerging from a single cosmic event. Alongside advances in atomic and subatomic science, it reshaped human understanding of the physical world during the 20th century.

**antibiotics**
Medicines, beginning with penicillin (discovered 1928, deployed widely by WWII), that kill or inhibit bacterial infections. One of the most significant medical advances in history; enabled people to survive previously fatal infections and contributed to rising life expectancy globally.

**birth control**
Reliable contraceptive technologies that gave women agency over family size, contributing to fertility decline in developed countries and enabling women to delay or forgo childbearing in favor of careers. A key driver of changing gender roles in the 20th century.

**[[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]**
The global agricultural transformation from the 1940s–1970s in which scientists developed genetically modified, high-yield, disease-resistant crop varieties. Fed a rapidly growing global population — especially in developing countries — but reduced biodiversity as local crop varieties were displaced by monocultures.

**nuclear power**
Energy generated by nuclear fission, developed as a significant electricity source in the mid-20th century. Offered high energy output with low carbon emissions but raised concerns about safety (Chernobyl, Fukushima) and nuclear waste.

**renewable energy**
Energy sources — solar, wind, thermal, hydroelectric — that replenish naturally and do not deplete finite resources. By 2018 the International Renewable Energy Agency predicted renewables would be consistently cheaper than fossil fuels by 2020, marking a potential long-term shift away from carbon-based energy.

**brain drain**
The emigration of highly educated and skilled workers from less-developed to more-developed countries, attracted by better economic opportunities. Improved transportation made migration easier; the result was a loss of human capital for developing nations.

**core-periphery model**
The framework describing the persistent economic relationship in which industrialized core nations extract raw materials from and sell manufactured goods to peripheral (developing) nations. A continuity from the colonial era through the age of globalization.

**Four Modernizations**
Deng Xiaoping's post-Mao reform program (1970s–1980s) that opened China's economy to foreign investment and market mechanisms, transforming China into the world's second-largest economy. A landmark case of state-directed economic liberalization.

**supply-side economics**
The economic theory, applied by Reagan (USA) and Thatcher (UK) in the 1980s, that cutting taxes on businesses and high earners stimulates investment, growth, and ultimately broad prosperity. Produced economic growth but also increased income inequality.

**Arab Spring**
The wave of pro-democracy protests, uprisings, and revolutions that swept North Africa and the Middle East beginning in 2010–2011. Demonstrated the continuing power of mass political protest movements and the role of social media in organizing resistance; outcomes were mixed — some regimes fell, others survived.

**laissez-faire economics**
The 19th-century liberal economic principle of minimal government intervention in markets. A continuity contested throughout the 20th century: welfare states and Keynesian stimulus represented departures from it, while neoliberal reforms of the 1980s sought to restore it.

**cultural imperialism**
The dominance of one culture's values, media, and consumer goods over others through economic and technological power. A continuity of unequal cultural exchange in which Western (especially American) culture flowed more powerfully outward than cultures flowed back — though bidirectional exchange increased after 1900.

**climate change**
The long-term shift in global temperatures and weather patterns, accelerated by human-caused greenhouse gas emissions from fossil fuels. An environmental continuity with industrial-era pollution, but reaching existential-threat scale in the late 20th and 21st centuries; a major challenge requiring global cooperation.

**noncommunicable diseases (NCDs)**
Chronic, non-infectious conditions such as heart disease, diabetes, and cancer. A growing health burden in the 21st century — in developed nations, nearly all disease burden; increasingly prevalent in developing nations even as communicable diseases (cholera, malaria, HIV) remain threats. The rise of NCDs in developing countries is a new challenge layered onto old ones.
