# Continuity & Change in a Globalized World

**Unit 9 | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 707–720**

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 9.9 is the CCOT synthesis chapter for Unit 9. The AP exam will test your ability to identify BOTH changes AND continuities across 1900–present. For every change you cite, ask: what stayed the same? For every continuity, ask: what was different from earlier eras?

## The Big Picture

The period from 1900 to the present represents the most accelerated era of change in human history. Scientific and technological breakthroughs reshaped medicine, communication, transportation, agriculture, and energy — driving population growth from 1.6 billion to 8 billion people and doubling average life expectancy from ~35 to ~70 years. These changes cascaded across every domain of human life: societies became more urban and interconnected, economies globalized and shifted power toward Asia, politics oscillated between democracy and authoritarianism, cultures blended in an increasingly two-way exchange, and the environment faced unprecedented human-caused stress.

Yet continuities are equally striking. Economic inequality between developed and developing nations persisted despite changing faces. Patriarchal structures endured even as women gained legal rights. States continued to compete for power, suppress dissent, and manipulate economies. Diseases — whether cholera in sub-Saharan Africa or HIV/AIDS globally — still tracked the fault lines of poverty and underdevelopment. The CCOT skill demand here is to hold both transformation and persistence in view simultaneously.

> [!warning]
> A common AP mistake is to treat this era as purely progressive. The AP exam rewards nuance: argue that changes were *uneven*, benefiting developed nations more than developing ones, or that new technologies produced both positive outcomes (vaccines) and negative consequences (environmental damage, pandemics spreading faster).

## Advances in Science and Technology

The pace of scientific discovery after 1900 was unprecedented. In **physics**, the Big Bang theory explained the origin of the universe and led to breakthroughs in nuclear science. **Wave science** — understanding radio, light, sound, and microwaves — powered telecommunications revolutions from radio to cellular networks to satellite internet.

**Medical science** produced antibiotics (penicillin), vaccines for polio and tuberculosis, treatments for cancer and chronic disease, and reliable birth control. The result was a dramatic extension of human life expectancy. **Agricultural science** produced the [[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]] — genetically modified crops with higher yields that fed a growing global population but reduced biodiversity and created dependency on agribusiness.

**Energy technologies** shifted from coal to oil to nuclear power to renewable sources. The [[Concept Notes/Internet\|Internet]] and cellular technology compressed information exchange across the globe. Jet aviation and containerized shipping collapsed physical distances, fueling global trade.

> [!tip]
> Connect science and technology to the AP theme of **TEC** (Technology & Innovation). The AP exam frequently links technological change to unintended consequences — the Green Revolution fed billions but also increased monoculture vulnerability; fossil fuels powered growth but caused climate change.

## Social Changes: Change and Continuity

**Change:** Population exploded, particularly in developing countries, driven by agricultural and medical technology. Women gained voting rights, entered professions, and exercised reproductive choice through birth control — especially in developed nations. Migration increased as improved transportation made movement easier, causing "brain drain" from developing to developed countries.

**Continuity:** In many parts of the world, especially developing nations, women's status improved little. Gender inequality, enforced by cultural and religious patriarchal systems, persisted. Socioeconomic inequality tracked closely to the colonial-era divide between developed and developing worlds — the greatest population growth occurred in the lower socioeconomic classes of developing countries.

## Economic Changes: Change and Continuity

**Change:** The center of gravity of global manufacturing shifted. Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore used export-led industrialization to challenge Western economic dominance. China, after Deng Xiaoping's Four Modernizations, became the world's second-largest economy. India became a global hub for software and engineering services.

Governments experimented with sharply contrasting economic policies — from communist five-year plans (Stalin, Mao) to Keynesian welfare states (New Deal, British welfare expansion) to neoliberal privatization ([[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Thatcher]], Reagan) — reflecting an ongoing ideological contest over state vs. market.

**Continuity:** The fundamental structure of core-periphery exploitation continued. More-developed nations still extracted raw materials from less-developed ones and used them as markets for finished goods. Economic globalization intensified this dynamic rather than overturning it.

> [!exam-tip]
> The economic policy table (p. 710) is a prime AP source for LEQ evidence. Know the goals and results of: Lenin's NEP, Stalin's Five-Year Plans, Mao's Great Leap Forward, Deng's Four Modernizations, Roosevelt's New Deal, Reagan's supply-side cuts, and Thatcher's privatization.

## Political Changes: Change and Continuity

**Change:** Mass protest movements drove political transformation. [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Gandhi's]] nonviolent resistance in India became a model replicated globally — in the U.S. civil rights movement, anti-apartheid struggle in South Africa, and Arab Spring uprisings. Women's rights movements, anti-war protests, and democracy movements reshaped political norms.

Governments expanded their economic role, moving away from laissez-faire toward regulated capitalism or welfare states — a significant structural change from pre-1900 liberal economics.

**Continuity:** Governments persistently suppressed protest through persecution, imprisonment, and violence. Authoritarianism reasserted itself in the 21st century after post-Cold War optimism about democratic spread. The state remained the dominant unit of political power throughout.

## Cultural Changes: Change and Continuity

**Change:** Cultural exchange accelerated dramatically. Western (especially American) popular culture — movies, television, music, fashion — spread globally through mass media. Crucially, this exchange became increasingly bidirectional: East Asian cuisine, music (anime, K-pop), and fashion found audiences in the West. The Internet enabled real-time cultural diffusion at a scale and speed previously impossible.

**Continuity:** Cultural imperialism debates persisted — the flow of culture remained asymmetric, with Western (particularly American) content dominating global media. Resistance to cultural homogenization continued across the period.

## Environmental Changes: Change and Continuity

**Change:** Humans overcame environmental constraints through jet travel, satellite communication, and space exploration. Petroleum extraction made energy abundant. However, human activity also transformed the environment negatively: airborne and water pollution increased, climate change emerged as an existential threat, and biodiversity declined from industrial agriculture.

**Continuity:** Human exploitation of the natural environment for economic gain continued — the scale intensified but the pattern repeated earlier industrial-era behavior.

> [!warning]
> **Developed vs. developing divide** cuts across every domain. On the AP exam, using this as an analytical frame earns complexity points. Medical advances saved millions — but the UN's 1996 vaccine report showed protection only reached developing countries' children in the mid-1980s, 15 years after the vaccines existed.

## CCOT Argument Framework

For AP essays, structure your argument around this schema:

**Thesis template:** "While [X changed significantly] between 1900 and the present, [Y remained a persistent continuity], demonstrating that globalization transformed [domain] unevenly — accelerating change in developed nations while leaving [inequality/patriarchy/environmental exploitation] largely intact in developing ones."

**Top AP evidence clusters:**
- Technology → population growth → social strain (TEC + SIO)
- Decolonization → new states → economic neo-colonialism persists (GOV + ECN)
- Women's rights gains vs. persistent patriarchy (SIO + CDI)
- Bilateral cultural exchange vs. continued Western dominance (CDI)
- Environmental promises of technology vs. accelerating climate damage (ENV + TEC)
