## Key Individuals (from narrative and documents)

**Francis Fukuyama** (born 1952)
Political scientist whose opening quote frames the chapter: "Today, no country can ever truly cut itself off from the global media." Associated with the post-Cold War "end of history" thesis — that liberal democracy had triumphed. The 9.9 narrative implicitly qualifies this by noting that authoritarianism reasserted itself in the 21st century.

**John Maynard Keynes** (1883–1946)
Economist whose 1919 reflection on pre-WWI globalization appears in the CCOT exercise. Keynes described how London residents in 1914 could order goods from around the world by telephone — used here as a baseline for measuring change and continuity since 1900. His economic framework (government stimulus during downturns) shaped Roosevelt's New Deal.

**Edward Jenner** (1749–1823)
English physician credited with discovering vaccines (smallpox, late 18th century). Referenced in Document 1 (UNICEF, 1996) as the historical foundation for the modern vaccination program that saves ~9 million lives per year. Appears here as a continuity — the principle of vaccination was 18th-century science bearing fruit in 20th-century global health.

**Jane Barratt**
Secretary General of the International Federation on Aging. Author of Document 3 on global aging trends — argues that while longevity is an achievement, governments must address the quality of life for aging populations, especially Japan, South Korea, and Germany.

## Key States

**China**
Following Deng Xiaoping's Four Modernizations (1970s), China relaxed state control, attracted foreign investment, and grew into the world's second-largest economy. A major exporter of manufactured goods. Represents the most dramatic economic rise of a non-Western state in the 20th century.

**India**
Developed a globally competitive labor force in software development and engineering, becoming an economic powerhouse in the service sector. Demonstrates a different development path from China's manufacturing model — specialization in knowledge-economy services.

**Asian Tigers** (Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore)
Export-led industrial economies that rose to challenge Western economic dominance. Leveraged inexpensive labor and high-quality manufacturing to compete in consumer goods and high-tech products. A major change in the global economic order.

**South Africa**
Site of the apartheid system, under which racial segregation was legally enforced from 1948 until 1994. Protests, international sanctions, and internal resistance — linked to figures like Desmond Tutu and Nelson Mandela — brought it down. A major political change of the late 20th century. See [[Unit-9.5/Calls for Reform & Responses Overview\|Calls for Reform & Responses Overview]].

**Egypt / Arab Spring States**
Egypt's Tahrir Square protests (2011) symbolized the Arab Spring — mass democracy movements that swept North Africa and the Middle East. Over 1 million gathered on February 9, 2011, demanding the removal of President Hosni Mubarak. Some states achieved democratic transitions; others saw authoritarian restoration.

## Key Movements

**Women's Rights Movements**
A global continuity-and-change story: women gained voting rights, entered professions, and accessed birth control — primarily in developed nations after 1900. Women's rights movements emerged in Western democracies and spread globally. In many developing nations, however, patriarchal structures persisted, making this change geographically uneven. See [[Unit-9.5/Calls for Reform & Responses Overview\|Calls for Reform & Responses Overview]].

**Civil Rights Movements**
Activists championed civil rights in the United States, Northern Ireland, Canada, and other countries. [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Gandhi's]] model of nonviolent resistance proved globally influential — adopted by MLK in the U.S. and anti-apartheid activists in South Africa. A transnational pattern of political change through organized protest.

**Anti-War Movements**
Protests against military conflicts erupted in the United States and Western Europe, particularly during the Vietnam War. Represented growing popular opposition to state violence and militarism — a form of political continuity (opposition to war) expressed through new mass-media-amplified tactics.

**Democracy Movements / Arab Spring**
Protest movements demanding political representation and an end to authoritarian rule swept multiple regions in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Outcomes were mixed — South Korea, Taiwan, and Eastern Europe successfully democratized; Arab Spring states often reverted to authoritarianism.

**Global Environmental Movement**
Emerged in the late 20th century in response to climate change, pollution, and biodiversity loss. Organizations like [[Concept Notes/Green Belt Movement\|Wangari Maathai's Green Belt Movement]], Greenpeace, and Earth Day (est. 1970) represented civic responses to environmental degradation. See [[Unit-9.3/Technology & the Environment Overview\|Technology & the Environment Overview]].
