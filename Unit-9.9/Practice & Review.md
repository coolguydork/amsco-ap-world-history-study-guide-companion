### LEQ 1 — Causation (Technology → Political & Social Change)

**Prompt:** From 1900 to the present, changes in transportation and communication led to a more interconnected world. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which these technological advances resulted in political and social changes in that era.

**Key claim options:**
- **To a significant extent:** Jet aviation, internet, and cellular technology enabled rapid spread of protest movements (Arab Spring organized via social media), facilitated migration that changed social demographics, and created global consumer culture that homogenized values.
- **Qualified:** Technology enabled change but political outcomes depended on government responses — states that embraced connectivity democratized; authoritarian states used the same technologies for surveillance and control.

> [!tip] Model Thesis
> While transportation and communication technologies after 1900 enabled unprecedented political mobilization — from anti-colonial independence movements to the Arab Spring — their political effects were ultimately shaped by state responses, as authoritarian governments used the same technologies to surveil and suppress dissent, demonstrating that technology was a catalyst for change rather than a guarantee of it.

**Evidence to deploy:**
- Jet aviation / containerized shipping → brain drain from developing nations (social change)
- Internet / social media → Arab Spring coordination (political change)
- Cellular technology → cultural exchange even in remote areas (social change)
- Governments imprisoned or attacked protesters despite (or because of) global visibility
- China's Great Firewall — same internet, restricted political use

### LEQ 2 — Comparison (1918 Flu vs. HIV/AIDS)

**Prompt:** From 1900 to the present, increasing globalization has made transportation faster and easier, but it has also caused pandemics to become more widespread. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which the effects of the influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 were similar to, or different from, the HIV/AIDS epidemic that began in the 1980s.

**Key comparison framework:**

| Dimension | 1918 Influenza | HIV/AIDS (1980s–present) |
|-----------|----------------|--------------------------|
| Spread mechanism | Airborne; spread by WWI troop movements | Blood/fluid contact; spread by global mobility & migration |
| Death toll | ~50 million in months | ~36.7 million living with HIV (2016); 1M deaths/year |
| Geographic impact | Global but hit hardest in military populations | Worst in Eastern/Southern Africa (19.4M cases, 420K deaths) |
| State response | Wartime censorship suppressed early reporting | Slow response; stigma delayed action in many states |
| Technology | No cure; some masks/quarantine | ART drugs control (not cure); global inequality in access |
| Economic/Social | Disrupted war economies; labor shortages | "Brain drain" effect in hardest-hit regions; caregiving burden |

**Similarity thesis:** Both pandemics exploited global connectivity to spread rapidly, disproportionately harmed vulnerable populations, and exposed the limits of existing medical science.

**Difference thesis:** While influenza struck suddenly and briefly across all demographics, HIV/AIDS became a chronic, geographically concentrated crisis — worst in sub-Saharan Africa — that reflected persistent inequality between developed and developing nations.

> [!tip] Model Thesis
> Although both the 1918 influenza pandemic and the HIV/AIDS epidemic spread through global networks of human movement, they differed significantly in duration, demographic impact, and geographic concentration — with HIV/AIDS becoming a chronic crisis disproportionately devastating sub-Saharan Africa, revealing how global inequality shapes disease outcomes as much as the disease itself.

### LEQ 3 — Comparison (Partition of India vs. Partition of Palestine)

**Prompt:** After 1900, religious and ideological differences led to the partition of some states in Eurasia so their peoples could have independence. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which the partition of India in 1947 and the partition of Palestine in 1948 were similar or different.

**Key comparison framework:**

| Dimension | Partition of India (1947) | Partition of Palestine (1948) |
|-----------|--------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Cause | Hindu-Muslim religious division; British withdrawal | Zionist-Arab conflict over territory; British withdrawal |
| Colonial power | British India → India + Pakistan | British Mandate → Israel + Palestinian territory |
| Violence | Massive communal violence; ~1–2 million killed, 14M displaced | 1948 Arab-Israeli War; ~700,000 Palestinian refugees (Nakba) |
| Outcome | Two independent states (India, Pakistan) | Israeli state established; Palestinian statehood unresolved |
| Ongoing conflict | India-Pakistan nuclear rivalry; Kashmir dispute | Arab-Israeli conflict continues; Palestinian statelessness |

**Similarity:** Both partitions resulted from British decolonization, involved religious/ethnic conflict, produced massive refugee flows, and created unresolved territorial disputes that persist.

**Difference:** India produced two functioning states; Palestine produced one recognized state and an unresolved stateless population — divergent outcomes from similar causes.

### LEQ 4 — Comparison (Partition of Germany vs. Partition of Korea)

**Prompt:** After World War II, capitalist and communist powers divided up and heavily influenced the territories of Germany and Korea. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which the partition of Germany and the partition of Korea were similar or different between 1945 and the present.

**Key comparison framework:**

| Dimension | Germany (1945–1990) | Korea (1945–present) |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Cause | Allied occupation zones after WWII defeat | US/Soviet occupation zones after Japanese defeat |
| Cold War alignment | West Germany (NATO) vs. East Germany (Warsaw Pact) | South Korea (US-backed) vs. North Korea (Soviet/China-backed) |
| Conflict | Berlin Blockade (1948–49); Berlin Wall (1961) | Korean War (1950–53); ongoing armistice |
| Outcome | Reunification (1990) after Cold War ends | Remains divided; North Korea under Kim dynasty |
| Economic divergence | West Germany far outpaced East Germany | South Korea became Asian Tiger; North Korea isolated, impoverished |

**Similarity:** Both partitions were imposed by Cold War superpowers, created ideologically opposed states, produced periods of military confrontation, and left one side far more economically developed.

**Difference:** Germany reunified in 1990 with the end of the Cold War; Korea remains divided — with North Korea maintaining one of the world's most closed authoritarian states.

## Document-Based Question (DBQ)

**Prompt:** Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which human health is linked to a country's status as a developing or a developed nation.

**Historical Background:** Developing nations are characterized by less developed industrial bases, low access to safe water and sanitation, high pollution, and widespread poverty. Developed nations have advanced infrastructure, developed economies, greater wealth, and higher living standards.

---

### Document 1 — UNICEF Vaccine Report (1996)

**Source:** UNICEF, "Vaccines Bring 7 Diseases Under Control," 1996

**HAPP Analysis:**

- **Historical situation:** Published after the mid-1980s global vaccination push; Cold War over; increased multilateral health cooperation
- **Audience:** Global health policymakers and donors; intended to demonstrate the ROI of vaccination investment
- **Purpose:** Advocate for continued/increased vaccine funding by showing impact (9M lives/year; polio eradication target)
- **POV:** Optimistic about technology's potential — but reveals that protection only reached developing-world children in the mid-1980s, 15+ years after vaccines existed

**Key evidence for DBQ:**
- Vaccines save ~9 million lives/year globally
- Seven major diseases controlled; smallpox eradicated (5M lives/year saved)
- Only ~25% of developing-world children were immunized before mid-1980s
- Savings from polio eradication would be $270M/year (USA) and $200M/year (Western Europe) — cost to *developed* nations

> [!tip]
> **Complexity argument:** Use this document to show that even a technological solution (vaccines) was delayed reaching developing nations by decades — technology alone does not resolve the developed/developing divide.

---

### Document 2 — Developing Countries & Disease (Scientific American, 2016)

**Source:** Mark Fischetti, Scientific American, 2016

**HAPP Analysis:**

- **Historical situation:** 2016 — decades into globalization; developing nations now facing the "double burden" of communicable AND noncommunicable diseases
- **Audience:** Educated general readers; policymakers; health professionals
- **Purpose:** Alert readers to the emerging double burden; advocate for integrated health policy
- **POV:** Data-driven, academic; slightly alarmist about developing nations' trajectory

**Key evidence for DBQ:**
- Life expectancy rising globally — but *quality* of life declining as people live more years with chronic disease
- Developed nations: burden almost entirely noncommunicable (heart disease, cancer)
- Developing nations: increasing NCDs *on top of* still-present communicable diseases (malaria, diarrhea)
- "Double burden" = worse than either alone

> [!exam-tip]
> This document directly answers the DBQ prompt: developing nations bear a *double* health burden. Pair with Documents 1 and 4 to argue that health outcomes track development status as a persistent pattern.

---

### Document 3 — Global Aging (International Federation on Aging, 2017)

**Source:** Jane Barratt, Secretary General, International Federation on Aging, 2017

**HAPP Analysis:**

- **Historical situation:** Post-demographic transition; developed nations aging rapidly; global life expectancy milestone reached
- **Audience:** Governments; international health organizations; aging-sector advocates
- **Purpose:** Call for government policy action on aging populations
- **POV:** Celebrates longevity as achievement but urges governments to address quality-of-life gap

**Key evidence for DBQ:**
- For the first time, people in high-, middle-, AND low-income countries likely to reach age 60
- But living longer ≠ living healthier; older people not necessarily living active, healthy lives
- Countries projected to have oldest populations by 2050: Japan, South Korea, Germany (all developed/developed-adjacent)
- Quality of life in old age linked to country's aging population policies (developed nations have better capacity)

> [!tip]
> **Qualify your argument:** This document shows the developed/developing divide is narrowing in *some* respects (longevity reaching all income levels) but persisting in quality of life and health system capacity.

### Document 4 — Cholera in Africa (The Conversation, 2017)

**Source:** "Cholera: how African countries are failing to do even the basics," The Conversation, 2017

**HAPP Analysis:**

- **Historical situation:** 2017 — decades after cholera was largely eliminated in developed nations; persists in Africa
- **Audience:** General public; policymakers; health advocates
- **Purpose:** Explain why cholera persists in Africa despite available prevention tools; argue for political will
- **POV:** Critical of African governments ("lack of political maturity," "greed for political power") — note POV limitation: academic article may oversimplify political obstacles

**Key evidence for DBQ:**
- 1.3–4.0 million cholera cases/year globally; 2/3 in developing countries, mostly sub-Saharan Africa
- Cholera called "disease of poverty" — linked to lack of safe water, sanitation, poor infrastructure
- Natural disasters and civil unrest make prevention harder — conditions correlated with underdevelopment
- Vaccines exist but rarely used; "not a priority" for governments
- Population growth in urban slums compounds the challenge

> [!exam-tip]
> **Best document for the core argument:** Cholera is essentially eliminated in developed nations but kills hundreds of thousands in developing ones annually — despite an existing vaccine. This directly demonstrates health-development linkage.

---

### Document 5 — HIV/AIDS Statistics (UN Report, 2018)

**Source:** United Nations Report on HIV/AIDS, Regional Statistics, 2018

**HAPP Analysis:**

- **Historical situation:** 2018 — HIV/AIDS epidemic in its 4th decade; antiretroviral therapy exists but distribution unequal
- **Audience:** Governments; international health organizations; donors
- **Purpose:** Document the current scale and geographic distribution of HIV/AIDS globally
- **POV:** Statistical/neutral; produced by UN for accountability; reflects UN mandate for transparency on global health

**Key evidence for DBQ:**

| Region | HIV Cases | Deaths |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Eastern & Southern Africa | 19.4 million | 420,000 |
| Western & Central Africa | 6.1 million | 310,000 |
| Asia & Pacific | 5.1 million | 170,000 |
| North America + Western Europe | 2.1 million | 18,000 |
| World Total | 36.7 million | 1.0 million |

> [!tip]
> **Key comparison:** Eastern/Southern Africa has 19.4M cases and 420K deaths; North America + Western Europe have 2.1M cases and only 18K deaths. Same disease, vastly different mortality — because of ART access. This is the strongest evidence that health outcomes are linked to development status.

### DBQ Model Thesis Options

> [!tip] Thesis Option A (Change emphasis)
> While advances in medical science, vaccines, and international health organizations improved health outcomes globally after 1900, human health remained strongly linked to a country's development status — as developing nations suffered disproportionately from both persistent communicable diseases (cholera, HIV/AIDS) and the emerging burden of noncommunicable conditions, while developed nations benefited from greater access to vaccines, treatments, and health infrastructure.

> [!tip] Thesis Option B (Continuity emphasis)
> Despite significant improvements in global life expectancy and the development of vaccines, antibiotics, and antiretroviral therapies after 1900, the link between human health and development status persisted as a fundamental continuity — demonstrated by cholera's near-elimination in developed nations while killing hundreds of thousands annually in sub-Saharan Africa, and by HIV/AIDS mortality rates dramatically higher in Africa than in Western nations despite the existence of life-saving treatments.

**Body paragraph structure for the DBQ:**
1. **Medical technology improved health globally but unequally** → Docs 1 + 3 (vaccines reached developing nations 15 years late; longevity is spreading but quality lags)
2. **Communicable diseases persist as a *developing-nation problem*** → Docs 4 + 5 (cholera = disease of poverty; HIV/AIDS mortality gap between Africa and North America)
3. **Double burden — NCDs compound communicable disease in developing nations** → Doc 2 (developing nations now face both; developed nations only NCDs)
4. **Outside evidence:** Green Revolution increased food security but also environmental tradeoffs; international institutions (WHO, UNICEF) helped but resource constraints limited reach
