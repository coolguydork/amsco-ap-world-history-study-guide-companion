# Key People and Groups — Unit 5.6: Industrialization: Government's Role

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## Otto von Bismarck (1815–1898) — Germany
**Region:** Prussia/Germany | **Period:** 1862–1890

- As Chancellor, Bismarck used protective tariffs (especially the "iron and rye" tariff coalition of 1879, protecting both steel manufacturers and grain-growing landlords), state-owned railroads, and government-linked investment banks to accelerate German industrialization.
- Introduced Europe's first social welfare legislation (1883–1889) as a deliberate strategy to undercut socialist organizing by making workers economically dependent on the state.
- His model — aggressive state direction of economic development combined with social welfare to manage political consequences — became a template for 20th-century developmental states.

---

## Meiji Oligarchs (Ōkubo Toshimichi, Itō Hirobumi, et al.) — Japan
**Region:** Japan | **Period:** 1868–1912

- Directed the most comprehensive state-led industrialization program in 19th-century history: abolished feudalism, built state-owned factories and railroads, established banks, created universal education, drafted a constitution (1889), and hired thousands of Western experts.
- Used state power to create industries where no private capital existed, then transferred enterprises to zaibatsu at favorable prices — combining state initiation with private operation.
- Their model inspired later "developmental state" theories applied in South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and China in the 20th century.

---

## Richard Cobden (1804–1865) and the Anti-Corn Law League — Britain
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** 1838–1846

- Cobden and John Bright organized the Anti-Corn Law League, which successfully campaigned for the repeal of agricultural tariffs (Corn Laws) in 1846.
- Their victory marked Britain's formal adoption of free trade as national policy — and the political defeat of the landed aristocracy by the industrial bourgeoisie.
- Illustrates how government policy is shaped by competing economic interests: landowners wanted protection; manufacturers wanted cheap food (lower wages) and open foreign markets.

---

## Abraham Lincoln and the Republican Party — United States
**Region:** USA | **Period:** 1860s

- The Republican Party (founded 1854) championed both the abolition of slavery and the protection of Northern manufacturing through high tariffs.
- Lincoln's Morrill Tariff (1861) and the National Banking Acts (1863–64) created the financial and trade policy framework for post-Civil War American industrialization.
- Lincoln's presidency illustrates the connection between political ideology and economic policy: the Republican "free labor" ideology combined opposition to slave labor with support for protective tariffs for Northern manufacturers.

---

## Sergei Witte (1849–1915) — Russia
**Region:** Russia | **Period:** 1892–1903

- Finance Minister who directed Russia's industrial "big push" of the 1890s: attracted French investment, built the Trans-Siberian Railroad, established protective tariffs, and promoted heavy industry in a few urban centers.
- Created rapid industrial growth but also severe social tension — a concentrated proletariat, harsh working conditions, and an autocratic political system refusing reform.
- His dismissal in 1903 and the 1905 Revolution illustrate the political risks of state-directed industrialization without corresponding political reform.

---

## Herbert Spencer (1820–1903) — Britain/USA
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** 1850s–1900s

- British philosopher who coined the phrase "survival of the fittest" and applied Darwinian evolutionary theory to human societies (Social Darwinism).
- Argued that government assistance to the poor was harmful because it kept "unfit" individuals alive and interfered with natural social selection.
- His ideas were enormously popular among American industrial capitalists who used them to justify opposing unions, welfare legislation, and business regulation.

---

## The Indian Civil Service — Britain/India
**Region:** Britain/India | **Period:** Throughout 19th century

- The British imperial government in India used its administrative power to de-industrialize India — imposing free trade policies that exposed Indian textile manufacturers to competition from Lancashire mills, while simultaneously extracting raw cotton for British factories.
- India's experience illustrates the inverse of state-directed industrialization: deliberate *deindustrialization* imposed on a colonial economy to serve the metropolitan power's industrial interests.
