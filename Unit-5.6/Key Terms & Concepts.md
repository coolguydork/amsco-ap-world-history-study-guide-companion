# Key Terms & Concepts

**AMSCO pp. 317–324**

---

**Mamluks**
Formerly enslaved Turks who formed a military ruling class in Egypt; governed Egypt for roughly 600 years until an Ottoman–Mamluk conflict in the early 1800s allowed Muhammad Ali to seize power (c. 1805). Their long dominance explains why the Ottoman sultan had so little real authority in Egypt — a vacuum Muhammad Ali exploited.

**Muhammad Ali**
Albanian Ottoman officer who became governor of Egypt around 1805; launched the most ambitious state-sponsored industrialization project in the Ottoman world — building textile factories, armaments plants in Cairo, and a naval shipyard in Alexandria. He also established schools and sent military officers to study in France. Called the first great modern ruler of Egypt. His model of government-owned industry contrasts sharply with Britain's private-market industrialization.

> [!exam-tip]
> Muhammad Ali is a prime example the AP uses to illustrate **government-directed industrialization outside of Europe**. Expect SAQ or LEQ prompts asking you to compare Egypt's approach with Japan's or Britain's. The key contrast: state ownership vs. private enterprise.

**Commodore Matthew Perry**
US naval officer who sailed warships into Tokyo Bay in 1853, demanding trade access for the United States. His "gunboat diplomacy" — using military threat to force open a market — shocked Japanese leaders, who recognized they lacked the military technology to resist. Perry's arrival is the direct catalyst for the Meiji Restoration: Japanese reformers used the crisis he created to overthrow the shogunate and begin rapid modernization.

**Meiji Restoration**
Japanese political transformation of 1868 in which reformers overthrew the shogunate (the military government that had ruled since the 12th century) and restored power to the emperor — Emperor Meiji (Mutsuhito). The Restoration launched Japan's rapid state-directed industrialization: within a generation, Japan had a modern army, a constitutional government, national railways, and subsidized industries. The word "Meiji" means "enlightened rule." See also: [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] concept note.

**Charter Oath**
Five-clause proclamation issued by Emperor Meiji in 1868 that formally abolished feudalism, guaranteed equality before the law, committed Japan to seeking knowledge worldwide, and established deliberative assemblies. It was the founding constitutional statement of the Meiji program — a promise that Japan would reshape itself by selectively adopting the best Western institutions.

**zaibatsu**
Powerful Japanese family-owned business conglomerates (e.g., Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo) that purchased government-built industries once they were profitable. The Meiji government used public funds to establish key industries, then sold them to zaibatsu, combining state initiative with private efficiency. Zaibatsu dominated the Japanese economy well into the 20th century and supported Japan's imperial expansion.

> [!tip]
> Zaibatsu = Japan's version of the large industrial conglomerates that dominated Western economies (US Steel, Standard Oil). The difference: they emerged from government sell-offs, not purely private entrepreneurship.

**automatic loom**
Power loom that automatically stopped when a thread broke, preventing defective cloth. Pioneered by Sakichi Toyoda at Toyoda Loom Works (founded 1906) — a classic example of Meiji-era **private innovation** building on government-subsidized industrialization. The company later pivoted to automobiles and became Toyota Motor Company, illustrating how Japan's early textile industry seeded 20th-century industrial giants.

## Thematic Concepts

**State-sponsored industrialization**
A pattern in which the central government — not private entrepreneurs — drives industrial development through subsidies, tariffs, state-owned enterprises, and infrastructure investment. This model was used by Muhammad Ali in Egypt, the Meiji government in Japan, and Tsar Alexander II's successors in Russia. It contrasts with Britain's **laissez-faire** approach.

**Gunboat diplomacy**
The use of military force (or the threat of it) to compel weaker states to open their markets or sign unequal treaties. Perry's 1853 mission to Japan is the textbook example. Compare with the British using gunboats in the First Opium War (1839–42) to force China to accept opium imports.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse "Tanzimat" with "Meiji." Both are 19th-century state modernization programs, but Tanzimat (Ottoman, c. 1839–1876) largely failed to halt decline, while Meiji (Japan, 1868+) succeeded dramatically. The difference: Japan had a coherent, centralized state willing to radically restructure itself; the Ottoman state was too corrupt and fragmented.

**"Sick man of Europe"**
Nickname for the declining Ottoman Empire in the 19th century, used by European powers to describe its weakness and their expectation of its eventual collapse. The phrase signals why Europeans — especially Russia — saw an opportunity to expand into Ottoman territory, and why Ottoman modernization was so urgent (and ultimately insufficient).
