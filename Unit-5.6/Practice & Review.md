# Topic 5.6 — Practice & Review

**Unit 5: Industrialization and Its Effects | Period 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900)**

## Multiple Choice Practice

**1.** Which of the following best explains why Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] was more successful at industrialization than China's Self-Strengthening Movement?

- A) Japan had access to more natural resources than China
- B) Japan's central government implemented comprehensive institutional reform, while China's reform was blocked by conservative elites
- C) China faced more significant foreign military pressure than Japan did
- D) Japan received direct financial assistance from Western powers

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — natural resources were not the decisive variable; China actually had substantial resources of its own.
> **B is correct** — The Meiji government abolished feudalism, reformed education and the military top to bottom, and built industrial infrastructure nationwide. China's Self-Strengthening Movement attempted only technological borrowing without institutional change, and [[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Empress Cixi]] crushed the Hundred Days of Reform before deeper reforms could take hold.
> C is wrong — both nations faced serious Western pressure; China's was arguably more severe, but pressure alone does not explain outcomes.
> D is wrong — Japan financed its industrialization primarily through its own heavy agricultural taxes, not Western loans or gifts.

**2.** [[Concept Notes/Muhammad Ali of Egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]]'s economic reforms most closely resemble which of the following?

- A) British laissez-faire capitalism, which relied on private entrepreneurs to drive growth
- B) State-sponsored industrialization in Meiji Japan, in which the government directed economic development
- C) The mercantile system of the 17th century, which relied on colonial extraction
- D) The socialist programs of [[Concept Notes/Karl Marx|Karl Marx]], which sought collective ownership of production

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — Muhammad Ali's Egypt was the opposite of laissez-faire; the state owned factories, controlled cotton production, and directed all major economic decisions.
> **B is correct** — Both Muhammad Ali and the Meiji government used state authority to build factories, fund infrastructure, reorganize the military, and direct economic activity. Government investment, not private markets, drove growth in both cases.
> C is wrong — mercantilism involved extracting wealth from colonies; Egypt was not extracting from colonies but building domestic industry.
> D is wrong — Marx's socialism sought collective ownership by workers; Muhammad Ali concentrated ownership in the state under his personal authority, not in the hands of working people.

**3.** The phrase "Sick Man of Europe" in the 19th century referred to the [[Concept Notes/Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Empire]]'s:

- A) High rate of epidemic disease among its civilian population
- B) Military defeats against Napoleon Bonaparte
- C) Political weakness, economic decline, and inability to resist Western pressure
- D) Failure to adopt Christianity despite European missionary efforts

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> A is wrong — the phrase was metaphorical, not literal; it referred to political and economic decline, not disease.
> B is wrong — Napoleon's Egyptian campaign (1798–1801) predated the height of this phrase's use, and military defeat by Napoleon was not the primary referent.
> **C is correct** — The phrase captured Ottoman political corruption, economic dependency on European finance, ethnic nationalist unrest among subject peoples (Greeks, Slavs, Armenians, Arabs), and steady territorial losses to Russia and others.
> D is wrong — religion played no role in this characterization; the phrase was about political and military weakness.

**4.** Which of the following was a shared feature of Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and [[Concept Notes/Muhammad Ali of Egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]]'s reforms?

- A) Both established constitutional republics modeled on the United States
- B) Both used high taxes on the agricultural sector to fund industrialization
- C) Both invited European colonial powers to administer their economies
- D) Both abolished the monarchy and established representative governments

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — Japan created a constitutional monarchy modeled on Prussia, not a republic; Egypt remained under Muhammad Ali's autocratic authority.
> **B is correct** — Both Japan and Egypt extracted revenue from agriculture to fund industrial development. Japan imposed a high agricultural tax that channeled revenue to the central government; Muhammad Ali seized control of cotton production and taxed peasants heavily, often displacing them from their land.
> C is wrong — both explicitly aimed to *prevent* colonial domination, not invite it. Inviting colonial administration would have defeated the entire purpose of their reform programs.
> D is wrong — both maintained concentrated, non-representative government; Meiji Japan's constitutional system was modeled on authoritarian Prussia, and Muhammad Ali ruled as an autocrat.

**5.** The [[Concept Notes/zaibatsu|zaibatsu]] in Meiji Japan were most similar to which of the following in Western industrialization?

- A) Medieval guilds that regulated craft production
- B) Labor unions that organized industrial workers
- C) Large conglomerates or monopolies like Rockefeller's Standard Oil
- D) Government-owned enterprises that monopolized state production

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> A is wrong — guilds were pre-industrial craft organizations that regulated a single trade; [[Concept Notes/zaibatsu|zaibatsu]] were large, diversified, industrial conglomerates spanning multiple industries.
> B is wrong — [[Concept Notes/zaibatsu|zaibatsu]] were business organizations owned by powerful families, not worker organizations.
> **C is correct** — [[Concept Notes/zaibatsu|zaibatsu]] were powerful family-owned business conglomerates that dominated multiple industries simultaneously, functioning like American trusts and monopolies. The Toyoda family's progression from loom manufacturing to automobiles is a perfect example.
> D is wrong — [[Concept Notes/zaibatsu|zaibatsu]] were privately owned by elite families, not by the government; they worked *alongside* the Meiji state rather than being owned by it.

## Short Answer Question (SAQ) Practice

**SAQ 1:**

Use your knowledge of world history to answer all parts of the question.

(a) Briefly describe ONE similarity between Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and [[Concept Notes/Muhammad Ali of Egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]]'s modernization program.

(b) Briefly describe ONE difference between Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and [[Concept Notes/Muhammad Ali of Egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]]'s modernization program.

(c) Briefly explain ONE reason why Japan's industrialization was more successful in the long term than Egypt's.

> [!tip] Model Responses
> **(a)** Both Japan and Egypt pursued **state-sponsored industrialization** — using government authority to build factories, reform the military on European models, and establish modern schools and technical training. Both also financed industrial development through heavy taxation of the agricultural sector, shifting the burden of modernization onto peasant farmers.
>
> **(b)** Japan's reforms were **more comprehensive and institutionally deep**. The Meiji government abolished feudalism entirely, created a constitutional system, reformed the entire educational system from primary schools to universities, and built railroads and telegraph networks nationwide. Muhammad Ali's reforms were more dependent on his personal authority, did not create self-sustaining institutions, and left Egypt's fundamental political and social structures largely intact.
>
> **(c)** Japan was able to industrialize more fully because it **maintained political sovereignty** throughout the reform period, giving its government the freedom and resources to pursue long-term industrial investment. Egypt, by contrast, remained under nominal Ottoman authority and ultimately fell under **British imperial domination** (1882), which redirected Egyptian agricultural revenues to serve British interests rather than Egyptian development — cutting off the very resource base that had funded Muhammad Ali's reforms.

**SAQ 2:**

(a) Briefly describe ONE cause of China's failure to industrialize effectively in the 19th century.

(b) Briefly describe ONE reform China actually attempted during the 19th century.

(c) Briefly explain how the [[Concept Notes/Opium War|Opium War]] affected China's ability to industrialize.

> [!tip] Model Responses
> **(a)** **Conservative resistance** within the Qing court — exemplified by [[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Empress Cixi]]'s coup against the Hundred Days of Reform — blocked the comprehensive institutional changes necessary for effective industrialization. Cixi repealed reform edicts, imprisoned Emperor Guangxu, and blocked the extension of railroads and telegraphs into China's interior to protect traditional Qing power structures and the scholar-gentry class that staffed the bureaucracy.
>
> **(b)** China's **Self-Strengthening Movement** (1860s–1890s) attempted to modernize China's military technology by building shipyards and arsenals, training artisans in modern industrial techniques, and hiring French and British military advisors. China also established a modern diplomatic corps and customs service during this period.
>
> **(c)** The [[Concept Notes/Opium War|Opium War]] severely weakened China's capacity for industrialization by **forcing it into unequal treaties** that opened Chinese markets to foreign goods, imposed crushing financial indemnities, ceded territory (Hong Kong), and established foreign spheres of influence within China itself. These concessions drained Chinese government revenues, reduced central government authority, and locked China into a semi-colonial dependency relationship with Western powers that made sustained industrial investment nearly impossible.

## Document-Based and Long Essay Connections

**For DBQs**: Topic 5.6 commonly appears in documents about:
- Government proclamations and reform edicts (Japan's Charter Oath, China's imperial reform decrees)
- Comparisons of modernization strategies across non-Western states
- Letters and reports from Western observers assessing Ottoman or Chinese "backwardness"
- Business records or economic data showing the outcomes of state-led industrialization

**For LEQs**: Common prompts connected to 5.6:
- "Evaluate the extent to which government policy was the most significant factor in determining the success or failure of industrialization efforts outside of Western Europe in the period 1800–1914."
- "Compare the causes of Japan's successful industrialization with the causes of China's failed industrialization in the 19th century."
- "To what extent did states outside of Western Europe adopt Western models of industrialization during the period 1800–1914?"

> [!exam-tip]
> When writing about state-led industrialization, pair a **specific policy** with a **specific outcome** — then explain what that outcome reveals about government capacity. The best AP essays distinguish between states that attempted reform and those that achieved it, and they explain *why* — not just *that* — outcomes differed. For example: "While both Egypt and Japan pursued state-sponsored industrialization, Japan's success stemmed from its ability to maintain sovereignty throughout the reform period, whereas Egypt's modernization was ultimately redirected by British imperial control after 1882, demonstrating that external political pressure could undermine even ambitious government reform programs."

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning]
> **Japan was not simply "Westernized."** The Meiji reformers adopted Western technology, institutions, and military organization — but deliberately preserved Japanese cultural identity and emperor-centered nationalism. The Charter Oath's openness to foreign knowledge coexisted with a strong assertion of Japanese distinctiveness. Don't write that Japan "became Western"; write that Japan selectively adopted Western tools while asserting its own national identity.

> [!warning]
> **China did not simply "refuse" to modernize.** The Self-Strengthening Movement was a genuine and significant reform effort. The Hundred Days of Reform proposed sweeping change. China's failure was not a lack of effort but a combination of conservative elite resistance, colonial pressure draining resources, and a central government too weakened by unequal treaties to execute systematic reform. The complexity matters on the AP exam.

> [!warning]
> **Muhammad Ali was not a nationalist.** He was an Albanian-born Ottoman officer who pursued modernization to build *his own power*, not to serve Egyptian national interests. His reforms benefited the state he controlled, not the Egyptian peasants who bore the heaviest costs. Don't romanticize him as a proto-nationalist leader — his legacy is complex.

## Quick Review: Government Responses to Western Industrial Pressure

| State | Key Leader(s) | Strategy | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Japan | Emperor Meiji, Meiji oligarchs | Comprehensive top-down reform; abolish feudalism, rebuild military, fund industry | Industrial great power by 1905; defeated China (1895) and Russia (1905) |
| Egypt | [[Concept Notes/Muhammad Ali of Egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]] | State-directed industrialization; military reform, agricultural control, factories | Temporary modernization; British imperialism cut it short (1882) |
| China | Emperor Guangxu, Kang Youwei (blocked by [[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Empress Cixi]]) | Technological borrowing (Self-Strengthening); brief radical reform (Hundred Days) | Reform blocked; semi-colonial status; Qing dynasty collapsed 1911 |
| [[Concept Notes/Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Empire]] | Various sultans | Weak, fragmented reforms; unable to overcome corruption and ethnic nationalism | Continued decline; dismantled after WWI; replaced by Republic of Turkey |
