## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: Okubo Toshimichi, "On the Role of the State in Industrialization," 1874*

> "Generally speaking, the strength or weakness of a country is dependent on the wealth or poverty of its people, and the people's wealth or poverty derives from the amount of available products. The diligence of the people is a major factor in determining the amount of products available, but in the final analysis, it can all be traced to the guidance and encouragement given by the government and its officials. . . . Your subject respectfully recommends that a clear-cut plan be established . . . to determine the priorities under which industries may be encouraged. . . . If the people are adequately wealthy, it follows naturally that the country will become strong and wealthy. . . . If so, it will not be difficult for us to compete effectively against major powers."

**Question 1.** The main idea of this passage is that the government of Japan should

A. persuade people in Japan to work more diligently
B. create a plan to encourage foreign investment in Japanese industry
C. avoid influencing private economic decisions
D. encourage industrialization to enable Japan to compete economically

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> Okubo argues that government guidance of industry is the path to national wealth and competitive strength against "major powers." Option A focuses on individual diligence (a minor point, not the main argument). Option B mentions foreign investment, which is not Okubo's argument — he focuses on government direction of *domestic* industry. Option C is the opposite of his argument (he explicitly calls for government guidance). Option D captures his core claim: state-directed industrialization → national strength → ability to compete with Western powers.

---

**Question 2.** Which possible government policy would most directly support the goal stated by the writer?

A. Reforming the Japanese educational system to increase training of workers and managers
B. Improving the Japanese agricultural system to feed the foreign residents brought into Japan to work in the industrial factories
C. Providing subsidies to the poor so they would not rebel
D. Passing laws that would encourage people to remain on farms

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Okubo wants policies that boost industrial production and national competitiveness. Expanding technical education produces more skilled workers and managers — directly increasing industrial output. Option B is wrong because Meiji Japan used Japanese workers, not foreign laborers. Option C addresses social stability, not industrial production. Option D would keep workers in agriculture, reducing the urban industrial workforce.

**Question 3.** Which list of events related to the topic of the excerpt is in the correct chronological order?

A. Perry's ships arrive in Tokyo Bay, the Meiji Restoration, the Shogunate collapses, Japan industrializes
B. Japan industrializes, the Meiji Restoration, the collapse of the Shogunate, Perry's ships arrive in Tokyo Bay
C. Perry's ships arrive in Tokyo Bay, the Shogunate collapses, the Meiji Restoration, Japan industrializes
D. The Shogunate collapses, Perry's ships arrive in Tokyo Bay, the Meiji Restoration, Japan industrializes

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The correct sequence: Perry arrives (1853) → the crisis Perry created leads reformers to overthrow the shogunate (the shogunate collapses *as part of* the Restoration, but the collapse came after Perry's arrival) → Meiji Restoration declared (1868) → Japan industrializes (1870s–1914). Option A incorrectly puts the Meiji Restoration before the Shogunate's collapse (they are essentially simultaneous — the Restoration *is* the collapse of the Shogunate, but "Restoration" is the framing). The precise order is Perry → shogunate collapses/Restoration (1868) → industrialization. Option C correctly shows Perry first, then the collapse, then Restoration, then industrialization.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1

*Source: Peter Bent, Agrarian Change and Industrialization in Egypt (2015)*

> "It was cotton production, especially, that characterized the beginnings of Egypt's integration into the global capitalist system. . . . Many of the major changes in Egyptian agriculture, and in rural Egyptian society more broadly, can be traced to the development of the global capitalist system. As the British promoted cotton cultivation in Egypt, large estates took over land that had supplied the means of subsistence for peasants under pre-capitalist modes of production. The result was that 'the great majority of the peasantry was by the end of the nineteenth century either landless or land-poor, while a new class of large landowners—an agrarian bourgeoisie—had emerged'."

**Part A.** Identify ONE way in which cotton production changed the economic or social structure of Egypt in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Cotton production transformed Egypt's social structure by displacing the peasant landowning class and creating a new agrarian bourgeoisie. As the passage indicates, the shift to cash-crop cotton cultivation for global markets led large landowners to absorb peasant subsistence plots, leaving the majority of Egyptian peasants landless or land-poor by 1900. This created a sharply stratified rural society — a small wealthy landowning class profiting from cotton exports, and an impoverished landless majority dependent on wage labor.

**Part B.** Explain ONE way the passage reflects a difference between the political or economic policies of Egypt and China in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Difference/Comparison
> The passage reflects Egypt's active integration into the global capitalist economy through cotton production, while China resisted economic integration more stubbornly until forced by military defeat. Muhammad Ali's Egypt deliberately used state power to direct agricultural production toward export markets, creating the cotton-based economy the passage describes. China, by contrast, suffered two Opium Wars (1839–42, 1856–60) precisely because it refused to open its markets to Western goods. While Egypt's rulers chose economic integration (albeit at the cost of peasant displacement), China's Qing government resisted until it was compelled by military force — representing a fundamental difference in policy approach even if the ultimate result (dependency on Western economic interests) was similar.

**Part C.** Explain ONE historical situation in the period 1750–1900, other than the one illustrated in the passage, in which states in Asia or Africa adopted Western policies that affected traditional economic structures.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization
> Japan's Meiji Restoration (1868) is the clearest example. The Meiji government formally abolished Japan's feudal economic structure through the Charter Oath, eliminating the samurai class's hereditary privileges, instituting equality before the law, and redirecting agricultural taxes to fund industrial development. This dismantled Japan's traditional rice-based feudal economy and replaced it with a capitalist industrial structure — subsidized textile mills, shipyards, and railroads — within a single generation. Like Egypt's cotton transition, Meiji industrialization transformed traditional economic relationships (displacing samurai, burdening peasants with high taxes), but Japan maintained political sovereignty while Egypt did not.

### SAQ 2

**Part A.** Identify ONE institution that both the Ottoman Empire and Japan modernized to be more like Western countries.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Both the Ottoman Empire and Japan modernized their **military institutions** on Western models. The Ottomans under the Tanzimat reforms reorganized their army along European lines and purchased Western weapons. Japan's Meiji government rebuilt its military using the Prussian army as a model, instituted universal military conscription (replacing the samurai system), and built a modern navy. Both states recognized that military modernization was the most urgent priority given Western military superiority — though Japan's reforms were more thorough and effective.

**Part B.** Explain ONE historical situation that supports the argument that modernization brought more problems than benefits to people in the Ottoman Empire and Japan.

> [!tip] Model Response — Support
> Japan's industrialization of the textile sector replicated the exploitative labor conditions of early British industrialization. Accounts of female Japanese mill workers in the Meiji era describe long working hours, dangerous machinery, poor ventilation, and wages too low to escape poverty — conditions nearly identical to those British female workers had endured decades earlier. The AMSCO text explicitly notes that "in replicating the methods of Western countries, the Japanese also replicated some of industrial society's problems." For ordinary working women, Meiji modernization brought factory discipline and exploitation, not prosperity — suggesting that the benefits of industrialization flowed primarily to the state and the zaibatsu, not to workers.

**Part C.** Explain ONE historical situation that challenges the argument that modernization brought more problems than benefits to people in the Ottoman Empire and Japan.

> [!tip] Model Response — Challenge
> Japan's creation of a national public education system under the Meiji government provides a strong counter-argument. Before the Restoration, education was restricted to elite samurai-class families; the Meiji school system opened technical and academic education to all Japanese citizens regardless of birth status. By the early 20th century, Japan had one of the highest literacy rates in Asia. This democratization of education created real opportunities for social mobility for ordinary Japanese — particularly for boys from non-samurai backgrounds who could now enter government service, business, or the military. This represents a concrete benefit that modernization delivered to a broad segment of the population, not just the elite.
