# Cross-Topic Connections — Unit 5.6: Industrialization: Government's Role

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## 1. Builds On (Earlier Units)

- **[[Enlightenment Economics]]** ([[Unit-5.1]]) — Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* is the intellectual foundation for laissez-faire; without the Enlightenment, the ideological justification for minimal government would not have existed.
- **[[Industrialization Spreads]]** ([[Unit-5.4]]) — The national case studies in 5.4 (Germany, Japan, USA, Russia) provide the empirical content that 5.6 analyzes through the lens of government policy.
- **[[Mercantilist Empires]]** ([[Unit-4.5]]) — The mercantilism that Britain abandoned in 1846 (Corn Laws repeal) was the pre-industrial predecessor to industrial protectionism; the debate is continuous.
- **[[Atlantic Revolutions]]** ([[Unit-5.2]]) — The political revolutions that created constitutional states (USA, France, eventually Germany) were prerequisites for the policy-making capacity that shaped industrialization.

## 2. Sets Up (Later Topics and Units)

- **[[Reactions to the Industrial Economy]]** ([[Unit-5.8]]) — Bismarck's welfare legislation is a government response to the labor unrest and socialist organizing of 5.8; the two topics are in direct dialogue.
- **[[New Imperialism]]** (Unit 6) — States that directed industrialization also directed imperial expansion; industrial state capacity was both the instrument of imperialism and its justification.
- **[[Russian Revolution]]** (Unit 7) — Witte's industrialization without political reform created the conditions for the 1905 and 1917 revolutions; state-directed industrialization without democratization is unstable.
- **[[Developmental States in the 20th Century]]** (Units 8–9) — The Meiji and Bismarckian models directly influenced 20th-century developmental states in South Korea, Taiwan, China, and India.

---

## 3. Comparative Cases

- **Bismarck's welfare state vs. British laissez-faire** — Germany responded to socialist organizing with welfare legislation; Britain eventually passed similar reforms (National Insurance Act, 1911) under Liberal Party pressure. Both paths arrived at welfare states, but by different routes and timelines.
- **Meiji Japan vs. Qing China** — The sharpest possible contrast: both faced Western imperial pressure in the 1850s–1860s; Japan transformed its political institutions and industrialized; China tried to adopt technology without institutional change and was colonized economically. The variable is *state willingness to transform itself*.
- **American "laissez-faire" vs. German "state capitalism"** — Both produced powerful industrial economies by 1900, but with different social contracts: American extreme inequality with minimal welfare; German more managed inequality with welfare. Debates about which model is "better" continue today.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves the contrast between **laissez-faire ideology** and **actual government practice**. Always be ready to point out that even the most "free market" industrializing nations (Britain, USA) relied on substantial government intervention — they just directed it toward capital rather than labor.
