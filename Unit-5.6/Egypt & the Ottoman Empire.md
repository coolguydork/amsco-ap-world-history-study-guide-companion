# Topic 5.6 — Egypt & the Ottoman Empire

## Two Paths in the Same Region

The [[Concept Notes/Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Empire]] and Egypt in the 19th century illustrate two very different responses to Western industrial and military pressure — even though Egypt was technically part of the Ottoman system. (AMSCO pp. 317–319)

---

## The Ottoman Empire: "Sick Man of Europe"

By the 19th century, the [[Concept Notes/Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Empire]] was in serious trouble:

- **Corruption** drained resources and undermined effective governance
- **Ethnic nationalism** among subject peoples (Greeks, Slavs, Armenians, Arabs) created constant unrest and separatist movements
- **Military weakness** meant the empire could no longer protect its borders
- **Economic dependency** on European finance and trade grew

European powers — especially Russia — recognized the opportunity. The phrase **"Sick Man of Europe"** captured the widespread perception of Ottoman decline. Europeans jockeyed for influence over Ottoman territory and trade.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam may ask you to explain Ottoman decline in the context of 19th-century imperialism. Key causes: internal corruption + ethnic nationalism + military weakness + economic penetration by Europeans. The empire was **dismantled after WWI** and replaced by the **Republic of Turkey** under Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk).

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse Ottoman decline with total irrelevance — the empire lasted until after WWI (1922 formally). It remained a significant player in European diplomacy throughout the 19th century. Its weakness was relative, not absolute.

---

## Egypt: The Muhammad Ali Exception

Egypt tells a dramatically different story. While technically part of the [[Concept Notes/Ottoman Empire|Ottoman Empire]], Egypt under [[Concept Notes/Muhammad Ali of Egypt|Muhammad Ali of Egypt]] became one of the most aggressively modernizing states of the early 19th century.

### Background: The Mamluks and Ottoman Egypt

For roughly **600 years**, Egypt had been ruled by the **Mamluks** — a military caste descended from former Turkish slaves. In 1801, the Ottoman sultan sent an army (including Albanian officers) to retake Egypt after Napoleon's brief occupation. Out of this military force emerged an extraordinary figure: **Muhammad Ali**.

### Muhammad Ali's Rise

Muhammad Ali, an Albanian-born Ottoman officer, proved himself militarily and politically skilled. He maneuvered his way to the position of **governor (pasha) of Egypt** and almost immediately began acting independently of the sultan:

- Launched military campaigns in **Sudan** and **Syria** without Ottoman permission
- Essentially treated Egypt as his own sovereign state while nominally acknowledging Ottoman suzerainty

---

## Muhammad Ali's Reform Program

Muhammad Ali's modernization program was comprehensive and state-directed — a model of top-down industrial development.

### Military Reform
- Reorganized the Egyptian army on the **European model**
- Sent officers to **France** for military training
- Built a modern navy

### Educational Reform
- Established schools (especially military and technical)
- Sent students abroad for training
- Founded the **first newspaper in the Islamic world**

### Economic Reform
- **Taxed peasants heavily** and forced many off their land
- Brought **cotton production under state control** — cotton became Egypt's most valuable export
- **Secularized religious (waqf) lands**, bringing them under state control
- Profited enormously from cotton exports during the **Napoleonic Wars (1799–1815)** when European demand for raw materials was high

### Industrialization
- Built **textile factories** (to compete with France and Britain)
- Established **armaments factories** in Cairo
- Developed **shipbuilding** in Alexandria
- Created **dozens of small workshops** producing uniforms, weapons, and military equipment

> [!tip]
> Muhammad Ali's Egypt is a textbook case of **state-sponsored industrialization**: the government owned the factories, controlled the land and crops, taxed the population to fund development, and directed economic activity. Compare this to Britain's market-driven industrialization or Japan's hybrid approach.

---

## Assessment: Muhammad Ali's Legacy

Muhammad Ali is called **"the first great modern ruler of Egypt"** for good reason. He:

- Built a state-directed industrial and military machine largely from scratch
- Made Egypt temporarily one of the most powerful states in the Middle East
- Created institutions — schools, a press, a bureaucracy — that outlasted him

**Limitations:**
- His reforms depended heavily on his personal authority and did not create self-sustaining institutions
- Peasants bore the heaviest burden (displaced, heavily taxed) while the state captured agricultural profits
- Egypt's industrialization was eventually constrained by continued Ottoman nominal authority and later British imperialism

> [!exam-tip]
> A common AP comparison question: **Muhammad Ali vs. Meiji Japan**. Both used state power to drive industrialization, both reorganized their militaries, both built schools. Key difference: Japan succeeded long-term and became a world power; Egypt's modernization was cut short by imperialism.
