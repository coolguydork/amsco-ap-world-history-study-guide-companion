# Topic 5.6 — China's Reform Efforts

## China's Dilemma: Modernize or Preserve?

China in the 19th century faced a brutal dilemma: industrialize and transform society, or preserve Confucian tradition and risk colonial domination. Unlike Japan, China could never quite resolve this tension — and the consequences were catastrophic. (AMSCO pp. 320–321)

---

## The Context: Humiliation and Weakness

China entered the 19th century as one of the wealthiest, most populous, and most culturally confident civilizations in the world. By mid-century, that confidence had been shattered:

- The **[[Concept Notes/Opium War|Opium War]] (1839–1842)**: Britain forced China to legalize the opium trade, cede Hong Kong, and open treaty ports. The military mismatch was humiliating.
- **Spheres of Influence**: European powers (and later Japan) carved out zones of economic and political control within China, each claiming exclusive rights in their sphere.
- The Qing central government was **too weakened** by these external pressures — and by internal rebellions like the Taiping Rebellion — to mount an effective industrial response.

> [!warning]
> China is often portrayed as simply "refusing" to modernize. The reality is more complex: reform efforts were real and significant, but were blocked by conservative elites AND undermined by the colonial pressure that was draining China's resources and confidence simultaneously.

---

## The Self-Strengthening Movement (1860s–1890s)

China's first serious attempt at reform was the **Self-Strengthening Movement**, launched by reform-minded officials after the disastrous Opium Wars.

### Goals
- Strengthen China's **military technology**
- Train artisans to work in **shipyards and arsenals**
- Hire **French and British advisors** to transfer technical knowledge
- Establish a diplomatic corps and a modern customs service

### Strategy: Grafting, Not Transforming
The central philosophy of the Self-Strengthening Movement was captured in the slogan: **"Chinese learning for the fundamental principles; Western learning for practical application."**

The idea was to adopt Western *technology* (guns, ships, industrial machinery) while preserving Chinese *culture and institutions* — Confucian values, the scholar-gentry bureaucracy, the traditional examination system.

> [!tip]
> Compare China's Self-Strengthening Movement to Japan's Meiji approach. Both aimed to "adopt Western technology while preserving culture." But Japan went much further — abolishing feudalism, reforming education top to bottom, creating a new constitution. China tried to change as little as possible. This difference in scope explains much of the difference in outcomes.

### Why It Failed
The strategy of grafting technology onto an unreformed system had limits:
- The civil service examination system still rewarded mastery of classical texts, not technical or scientific knowledge
- Corrupt officials and bureaucratic resistance slowed implementation
- Without fundamental institutional reform, the new military technology couldn't be effectively organized or sustained
- **The Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)** exposed the failure: Japan — a nation China had long considered culturally inferior — defeated China decisively

---

## The Hundred Days of Reform (1898)

The shock of defeat in the Sino-Japanese War convinced reformers that more radical change was needed.

### The Reformers
- **Emperor Guangxu**: The young Qing emperor who genuinely wanted change
- **Kang Youwei**: A brilliant civil servant and intellectual who had studied both Confucianism and Western political thought; became the reform movement's chief architect

### The Reforms Proposed
Between June and September 1898 — just **103 days** — Guangxu issued a sweeping series of edicts:

- **Abolish the outdated civil service examination** (which had been largely unchanged for over a thousand years)
- **Eliminate corruption** and the practice of buying government positions
- Establish **Western-style industrial, commercial, and medical systems**
- Reorganize the military on modern lines
- Create modern schools emphasizing practical subjects

These reforms, if implemented, would have been as transformative as the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]].

---

## [[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Empress Cixi]]'s Coup: Reform Crushed

[[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Empress Cixi]] — the conservative empress dowager, Emperor Guangxu's aunt and adoptive mother — refused to allow the reforms to proceed. In September 1898, she staged a **coup d'état**:

- Placed Emperor Guangxu under **house arrest** on a lake island in the Forbidden City (where he remained until his death in 1908)
- **Repealed virtually all reform edicts**
- Had several reform leaders executed; Kang Youwei fled into exile
- Blocked the extension of **railroads and telegraph lines** into China's interior, fearing they would bring foreign influence

### Why Did Cixi Resist?
Cixi and the conservative establishment (including many Manchu nobles and traditional scholars) feared that radical reform would:
- Undermine the Qing dynasty's political control
- Destroy the scholar-gentry class that staffed the bureaucracy and supported the regime
- Open China to even greater foreign cultural penetration

> [!exam-tip]
> [[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Empress Cixi]] is a key figure for the AP exam as a symbol of **conservative resistance to reform**. Compare her to other conservative rulers who resisted industrialization. Contrast her with Meiji reformers who embraced change despite cultural disruption.

---

## The Irony: Cixi's Late Concessions

In a bitter irony, [[Concept Notes/Empress Cixi|Empress Cixi]] herself later acknowledged that China's civil service system was broken:
- Wealthy families were buying government posts
- Bribery was rampant
- Unqualified officials held positions

In the early 1900s, even Cixi supported **abolishing the civil service examination** — abandoning **2,500 years of tradition** in the scholar-gentry bureaucratic system. But by then it was too late for systematic reform under the Qing.

---

## The End: Republic of China (1911)

In **1911**, the Qing dynasty collapsed. China became a **republic** — but a weak, fragmented one torn by warlordism, foreign pressure, and political instability. The traumatic 19th century had left the central government too weakened to mount the kind of systematic industrial transformation Japan had achieved.

> [!tip]
> **China vs. Japan comparison** (an AP exam classic):
> - Both faced Western pressure in the mid-1800s
> - Japan responded with systematic, top-down reform (Meiji Restoration)
> - China attempted reform but was blocked by conservatives (Empress Cixi) and undermined by colonial pressure
> - Japan became an industrial great power; China became a semi-colonial state
> - Key variable: **the strength and coherence of the central government** and the willingness of elites to embrace fundamental change

## U.S. Role in East Asian Stability

The United States tried to maintain a balance of power in Asia during this period:
- President **Theodore Roosevelt** mediated the **Treaty of Portsmouth (1905)** that ended the Russo-Japanese War
- The U.S. promoted the **Open Door Policy** in China — equal trading access for all powers rather than exclusive spheres of influence
