# Key Terms — Unit 5.6: Industrialization: Government's Role

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Laissez-faire** — The economic doctrine that government should not interfere in markets; Adam Smith's legacy; dominant ideology of British industrialization. (Justified minimal factory regulation, enclosure acts, and opposition to unions — while actually relying on government enforcement of property rights and contracts.)

2. **Protective tariff** — A tax on imported goods designed to shield domestic industries from foreign competition; used by Germany, USA, Russia, and Japan to build industries that could not yet compete with established British manufacturers. (The debate between free trade [Britain] and protectionism [late industrializers] is one of the most important economic policy debates of the 19th century.)

3. **Corn Laws (Britain, 1815–1846)** — British laws that imposed tariffs on imported grain, protecting landed interests at the expense of urban industrial workers who paid higher food prices. (Their repeal in 1846 — after campaigns by the Anti-Corn Law League — marked Britain's full commitment to free trade and the political victory of the industrial bourgeoisie over the landed aristocracy.)

4. **Free trade** — The absence of tariffs, quotas, and other restrictions on international commerce; championed by Britain after the repeal of the Corn Laws (1846). (Britain's free trade advocacy was partly principled [Adam Smith's influence] and partly self-interested: as the world's most efficient manufacturer, Britain benefited from open markets more than protected ones.)

5. **Land grants (USA)** — Federal government grants of public land to railroad corporations and state universities to stimulate development. (The railroads received ~170 million acres — more than the area of Texas — making the Transcontinental Railroad financially viable; illustrates that American "free market" industrialization depended heavily on government resource transfers.)

6. **Morrill Tariff (1861)** — American tariff legislation that significantly raised import duties on manufactured goods, protecting Northern industrial interests during the Civil War. (The tariff debate was a major sectional conflict: Northern manufacturers wanted protection; Southern cotton exporters wanted free trade to keep European markets open; the Confederacy explicitly adopted free trade.)

7. **National Banking Acts (USA, 1863–1864)** — Federal legislation creating a national banking system and national paper currency during the Civil War; provided financial infrastructure for industrial expansion. (Another example of US government's active (if indirect) role in industrialization — "laissez-faire" America created the financial system that private industrialists operated within.)

8. **State-owned enterprise** — A business owned and operated by government rather than private investors; Meiji Japan's industrial policy relied heavily on state enterprises that were later privatized. (Allowed industrialization to proceed even when private capital was insufficient; the model was later adopted by many developing nations in the 20th century.)

9. **Zaibatsu** — Large Japanese family-owned industrial and financial conglomerates that dominated the Japanese economy from the Meiji era onward; created when the government sold state enterprises to favored private interests. (Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo — these companies received state assets at favorable prices and became the backbone of Japanese industrial capitalism.)

10. **Bismarck's social welfare legislation (Germany, 1883–1889)** — A series of laws establishing health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884), and old-age/disability pensions (1889) — the first modern welfare state legislation. (Bismarck's motivation was explicitly anti-socialist: by meeting workers' basic needs, he hoped to undercut support for the Social Democratic Party; his strategy partially succeeded, demonstrating government's use of social policy as political control.)

11. **Witte System (Russia)** — Finance Minister Sergei Witte's strategy (1890s) of attracting French investment, building state railroads, and establishing protective tariffs to drive rapid Russian industrialization. (Created rapid industrial growth but also concentrated a volatile proletariat in Russian cities; Witte was dismissed in 1903 after his industrial policy created social unrest.)

12. **Import substitution** — An economic strategy of producing domestically what would otherwise be imported, using tariffs and state investment to protect infant industries. (Used by Germany, Japan, USA, and later 20th-century developing nations; the opposite of free trade theory.)

13. **Social Darwinism** — The application of Darwin's evolutionary concepts to human society, arguing that competition between individuals, corporations, and nations naturally selects the "fittest"; used to justify extreme wealth inequality and laissez-faire opposition to welfare. (Herbert Spencer coined "survival of the fittest" to describe social competition; used by American robber barons to justify opposing regulation and unions.)

> [!exam-tip]
> A critical AP insight: Britain preached **free trade** to the world while having built its industrial advantage through **mercantilism** and **colonial extraction**. The USA, Germany, and Japan industrialized behind **protective tariffs**, then advocated free trade once they were competitive. This pattern — "kicking away the ladder" after climbing it — is a key economic history concept.
