# Topic 5.6 — Japan & the Meiji Restoration

## Japan's Transformation: The Gold Standard

Japan's response to Western industrial power is the most dramatic and successful example of **state-sponsored industrialization** in 19th-century history. In roughly 40 years, Japan went from a feudal, isolated society to a world industrial and military power. (AMSCO pp. 319–321)

---

## Before the Meiji Era: Isolation and the Shogunate

Between **1600 and 1854**, Japan had almost no contact with the outside world. The **Tokugawa shogunate** enforced strict isolation (sakoku — "closed country") allowing only minimal, controlled trade with the Dutch at the port of Nagasaki.

This long isolation meant Japan:
- Preserved a feudal social structure (samurai warrior class, daimyo lords, emperor as symbolic figure)
- Had no knowledge of, or preparation for, modern industrial technology
- Was far behind Western nations in military capability

---

## The Shock: Commodore Perry (1853–1854)

In **1853**, U.S. Commodore **Matthew Perry** sailed a squadron of steam-powered warships ("black ships") into **Edo (Tokyo) Bay** and demanded that Japan open its ports to American trade. The display of American firepower was deliberate and overwhelming.

- Perry returned in **1854 with more ships**, and Japan signed the **Convention of Kanagawa**, opening two ports
- Japan subsequently yielded to similar demands from other Western powers (Britain, France, Russia, the Netherlands)

### Japan Watched China's Fate

Japanese reformers were acutely aware of what had happened to China after the [[Concept Notes/Opium War|Opium War]]:
- Foreign "spheres of influence" carved up Chinese territory
- Humiliating unequal treaties
- A once-great civilization reduced to semi-colonial dependency

The argument of Japanese reformers was compelling: **adopt enough Western technology to protect traditional Japanese culture** — before the West does to Japan what it did to China.

> [!exam-tip]
> Perry's arrival is the key **cause** of the Meiji Restoration. For the AP exam, be ready to explain the chain: Perry's "black ships" → fear of China's fate → political crisis and overthrow of the shogunate → Meiji Restoration → systematic modernization.

---

## The Meiji Restoration (1868)

In **1868**, a coalition of reform-minded samurai (mainly from the Satsuma and Choshu domains) overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate. They declared the restoration of direct imperial rule under **Emperor Meiji** ("Enlightened Rule").

The name "restoration" was somewhat misleading — the emperor had not actually ruled for centuries. In practice, real power was held by an oligarchy of reformers (many of whom became **genro** — elder statesmen). The key point is that the new government was **centralized, ambitious, and determined to modernize**.

The foundational document, the **Charter Oath (1868)**, established:
- Formal abolition of feudalism
- Equality before the law
- Abolition of cruel punishments
- Openness to knowledge from around the world

---

## The Meiji Reform Program

The Meiji government pursued modernization **systematically across every sector of society**.

### Political Reform
- **Constitutional monarchy** modeled on **Prussia** (not Britain): a strong emperor ruling through a subordinate political leader (prime minister)
- Centralized bureaucracy replaced decentralized feudal domains

### Military Reform
- Army reorganized on the **Prussian model**
- Modern navy built (eventually defeating China in 1895 and Russia in 1905)
- **Universal military conscription** replaced the samurai monopoly on warfare

> [!warning]
> The abolition of the samurai class was one of the most socially disruptive aspects of the Meiji Restoration. In **1871**, samurai were given a final payment and their special status (and right to carry swords) was dissolved. Some transitioned into the new government as **genro**. The domains of **Satsuma and Choshu** resisted; the last major battle between samurai and the emperor's conscript army was fought in the **1870s**. Samurai were arguably the **main domestic victims** of Japan's rapid modernization.

### Educational Reform
- New national school system established
- Emphasis on **technical and scientific fields**
- Students sent abroad to study Western systems
- Western teachers brought to Japan

### Infrastructure
- **Railroads and roads** built across the country
- Telegraph networks established
- Ports modernized

### Economic and Industrial Policy
- Government **subsidized key industries**: tea, silk, weaponry, shipbuilding, sake
- Financed primarily through a **high agricultural tax** (shifting the tax burden onto peasants, similar to Muhammad Ali's Egypt)
- Agricultural tax revenue flowed to Tokyo's central bureaucracy and funded industrialization

### Private Sector: The [[Concept Notes/zaibatsu|zaibatsu]]
Alongside government investment, the Meiji era saw the rise of **[[Concept Notes/zaibatsu|zaibatsu]]** — powerful family-owned business conglomerates that dominated Japanese industry, functioning somewhat like American monopolies or conglomerates.

**Example:** Toyoda Loom Works, founded in **1906** by a carpenter, manufactured textile machinery. Generations later, this company became **Toyota Motor Company** — one of the world's largest automakers.

## The Social Cost: Industrialization's Dark Side

The [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] created an industrial society — and like Britain before it, Japan replicated **industrial society's problems**:

- **Female mill workers** were exploited in ways strikingly similar to women in British textile mills
- Peasants bore the heaviest tax burden to finance industrialization
- The pace of change was socially disorienting for many Japanese

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves this kind of **continuity**: Japan's industrialization caused similar social disruptions (worker exploitation, displacement of traditional classes) as Britain's had decades earlier. This is a strong argument for a LEQ or DBQ about the **effects of industrialization across different societies**.

---

## Japan's Results: From Isolation to Great Power

By 1905, Japan had:
- **Defeated China** in the Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)
- **Defeated Russia** in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), mediated by U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt at the Treaty of Portsmouth
- Established itself as the **first non-Western industrial great power**
- Built an empire in East Asia (Taiwan, later Korea)

The speed and completeness of Japan's transformation remains one of the most remarkable stories in modern world history.
