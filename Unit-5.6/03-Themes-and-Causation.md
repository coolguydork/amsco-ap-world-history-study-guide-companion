# Themes and Causation — Unit 5.6: Industrialization: Government's Role

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## Governance

The central causation question for this topic is: what role does government play in creating industrial economies? The 19th century provides a natural experiment with dramatically different outcomes. Britain's minimal state approach worked when Britain was the only industrial nation and faced no foreign competition — private entrepreneurs had every incentive to invest in machines and factories. But late industrializers (Germany, USA, Japan) faced British competition that no private investor could overcome without tariff protection or state subsidies. This demonstrates that **government policy is not neutral** in economic development: laissez-faire is itself a policy choice that produces specific distributional outcomes (benefiting established industries over emerging ones, wealthy over poor, creditors over debtors). The choice between free trade and protection, between laissez-faire and state direction, was fundamentally a political struggle with economic consequences.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam often frames the "government role in industrialization" question as a comparison problem. Practice this: "Compare the role of government in the industrialization of Japan and Britain." Answer structure: (1) similarity — both provided legal infrastructure; (2) key difference — Japan built state enterprises, Britain did not; (3) reason for difference — Japan industrialized late under threat of imperialism with insufficient private capital; Britain industrialized first when there was no foreign competition to fear.

## Economic Systems

Government policy shaped not just *whether* nations industrialized but *what kind* of industrial economy they developed. Britain's laissez-faire approach produced an economy dominated by small and medium-sized firms, with relatively weak coordination between industries, and significant class conflict between capital and labor. Germany's state-directed approach produced large, vertically integrated industrial cartels, close bank-industry relationships, and a social welfare system that managed class conflict. Japan's state-led model produced zaibatsu (industrial conglomerates) with close ties to the government — a "developmental state" model. These different structures had lasting consequences: German and Japanese economies continued to be characterized by close government-business cooperation through the 20th century, while Britain retained a more arms-length relationship.

## Social Structures

Government industrial policy directly determined social outcomes. Bismarck's social welfare legislation (1883–1889) was explicitly designed to prevent socialist revolution by binding the working class to the state through economic benefits — demonstrating that welfare policy is both economic and political. Britain's laissez-faire refusal to regulate factory conditions produced the brutal working conditions documented by Dickens, Engels, and government commissions — eventually producing political pressure that forced the Factory Acts of the 1830s–1870s. In colonial contexts, British free trade policy was implemented through state power in India — the British colonial government imposed the very lack of tariff protection that deindustrialized Indian textiles. This illustrates a profound irony: the "free market" imposed on India was enforced by imperial state violence.

## Cultural Developments and Religion

The ideological debate over government's economic role was one of the defining intellectual conflicts of the 19th century. **Liberalism** (in the 19th-century sense) championed free markets, individual rights, and minimal government — the ideology of Manchester manufacturers and their economist advocates. **Conservatism** (Bismarck's variant) accepted state direction of the economy to preserve social order. **Socialism** demanded state ownership of the means of production to end exploitation. **Social Darwinism** provided a pseudo-scientific justification for laissez-faire by naturalizing inequality as the result of competition. These ideological contests were not abstract: they determined whether children worked in factories, whether workers could organize unions, and whether the poor received any state assistance.

## Technology and Environment

Government decisions about infrastructure investment had direct environmental consequences. State railroad subsidies (USA, Russia, Japan) opened previously inaccessible regions to resource extraction — accelerating deforestation, mining, and agricultural expansion in Siberia, the American West, and Hokkaido. Bismarck's infrastructure investment concentrated heavy industry in the Ruhr Valley, creating some of the most severe industrial air and water pollution in 19th-century Europe. By contrast, India's colonial government's free trade policy prevented domestic industrialization — with the paradoxical result that India had lower carbon emissions and less industrial pollution in the 19th century, but also lower economic development.
