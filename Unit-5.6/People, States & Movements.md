# People, States & Movements

**AMSCO pp. 317–324**

---

## Key People

**Muhammad Ali (c. 1769–1849)**
Albanian-born Ottoman military officer who became the de facto ruler of Egypt after the Ottoman–Mamluk conflict of 1801–1805. Despite nominally serving the Ottoman sultan, Ali operated as an independent modernizer: he rebuilt Egypt's military on a European model, established schools and newspapers, sent officers to study in France, and pursued aggressive state-sponsored industrialization — textile factories, armament plants, naval shipyards. He financed these reforms by taxing peasants so heavily they were forced to surrender their land to the state, giving the government control of lucrative cotton exports. He is called **the first great modern ruler of Egypt**. His model of top-down, state-owned industrialization anticipates the Meiji approach in Japan — though Egypt's modernization ultimately led to debt and British domination.

> [!exam-tip]
> Muhammad Ali is a strong piece of evidence for SAQ/LEQ prompts about **government-directed industrialization outside Europe**, or prompts comparing non-Western responses to industrialization. Key argument: state-directed reform can begin modernization but may lead to dependency if debt is not managed.

---

**Commodore Matthew Perry (1794–1858)**
US Navy officer sent by President Millard Fillmore to force Japan to open trade. In 1853, Perry sailed four warships (the "Black Ships") into Edo (Tokyo) Bay, presenting demands for trade access. When Japan stalled, he returned in 1854 with seven ships and forced the Convention of Kanagawa — Japan's first "unequal treaty." Perry never set foot in Japan with peaceful intent: his mission was explicitly coercive. His arrival ended over 200 years of Japanese isolation and directly triggered the political crisis that led to the Meiji Restoration.

> [!tip]
> Perry is the **external shock** that caused Japan's internal transformation. On the AP exam, he represents Western imperial pressure (like Opium Wars for China). The difference is what happened *after*: Japan reformed rapidly, China did not.

---

**Emperor Meiji / Mutsuhito (r. 1868–1912)**
The 122nd Emperor of Japan, who gave his name to the Meiji era ("enlightened rule"). As a teenager, Mutsuhito became the symbolic figurehead of the Restoration movement that overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate. In practice, the Meiji government was run by a group of capable samurai-class reformers (the Meiji oligarchs), but the emperor served as the unifying symbol of Japan's modernization drive. His 1871 letter to US President Grant (quoted in AMSCO) articulates the Meiji philosophy: adopt Western innovations selectively to achieve equality with Western powers, while preserving Japan's identity.

---

**Okubo Toshimichi (1830–1878)**
One of the principal architects of the Meiji state. As the AMSCO primary source demonstrates, Okubo argued that national strength depended on economic development, and that it was the government's duty to "guide and encourage" industry. His view — that private wealth and national power were inseparable, and both required state direction — was the intellectual foundation of Meiji industrialization policy. He was assassinated in 1878, but his economic framework shaped Japan for decades.

---

**Tsar Alexander II (r. 1855–1881)**
Russian tsar who abolished serfdom in 1861, partly to enable Russia to industrialize by creating a mobile labor force. Alexander's reforms represent a parallel case to the Meiji Restoration: a ruler using state authority to dismantle a feudal labor system and lay the groundwork for industrial development. Russia's industrialization was slower and less successful than Japan's, providing a useful AP comparison.

---

**Sultan Abdulhamid II (r. 1876–1909)**
Ottoman sultan who tried to resist Western influence while simultaneously modernizing the military. His contradictory approach — suppressing liberal constitutionalists while building railroads — exemplifies the Ottoman Empire's inability to pursue coherent reform. His reign ended with the Young Turk Revolution (1908), which attempted to revive Ottoman constitutionalism. By then, the empire was too far gone.

## States

**Ottoman Empire**
By the 19th century, the once-mighty Ottoman state was in severe decline: corruption, ethnic nationalist unrest (Greeks, Serbs, Bulgarians, Arabs all seeking independence), and an outdated economy. The Tanzimat reforms (c. 1839–1876) attempted legal and administrative modernization, but were incomplete and contested. Egypt, technically an Ottoman vassal, actually outpaced Constantinople in reforms under Muhammad Ali. The empire collapsed after World War I and was replaced by the Turkish Republic and a set of Arab successor states.

**Meiji Japan**
The most successful non-Western industrializer of the 19th century. Under Meiji governance, Japan transformed from a feudal, isolationist state into an industrial power with a modern military and constitutional government in less than 50 years. By 1895 Japan defeated China in war; by 1905 it defeated Russia — the first time a non-Western nation defeated a major European power in modern warfare. Japan's success made it the model and the anomaly among non-Western responses to Western imperialism.

## Movements

**Meiji Restoration (1868)**
The political movement that overthrew Japan's Tokugawa shogunate and restored imperial rule under Emperor Meiji. Driven by samurai-class reformers ("the Meiji oligarchs") who concluded Japan must westernize selectively to survive. The Restoration was simultaneously conservative (preserving the emperor as divine symbol) and radical (abolishing feudalism, conscripting a national army, building public schools). See [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] for cross-topic context.

**Tanzimat Reforms (c. 1839–1876)**
Ottoman modernization effort meaning "reorganization." Introduced equality before the law for all Ottoman subjects (including non-Muslims), a new legal code modeled on France, a reorganized military, and limited industrial investment. Failed to prevent decline because they were inconsistently implemented, opposed by conservative religious elites, and underfunded.
