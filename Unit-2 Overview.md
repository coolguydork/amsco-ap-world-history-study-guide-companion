# Unit 2 — Networks of Exchange (c. 1200–c. 1450)

> [!summary] Unit 2 Big Picture
> Between 1200 and 1450, existing trade routes didn't just survive — they **expanded dramatically** in volume, scope, and cultural reach. The Mongol Empire unified Eurasia under a single political umbrella. The Indian Ocean became the world's busiest maritime highway. Trans-Saharan routes carried gold and Islam deep into Africa. And everywhere merchants went, they brought disease, ideas, religions, and technologies with them.

## Why Unit 2 Matters for the AP Exam

Unit 2 is essentially the "connective tissue" of the entire AP World History course. The trade networks here explain:
- **How** Chinese technology (gunpowder, printing, compass) reached Europe
- **Why** the Black Death devastated Eurasia in the 14th century
- **How** Islam spread to Africa and Southeast Asia without armies
- **Why** West African kingdoms like Mali became fabulously wealthy
- **What** the Mongols destroyed — and what they accidentally built

Almost every LEQ, DBQ, or SAQ prompt about Period 1 will touch Unit 2. Know it cold.

## The Three Great Networks

### 1. The Silk Roads (Topics 2.1–2.2)
The ancient overland routes linking China to the Mediterranean. By 1200, the **Mongol Empire** had unified them under a single authority for the first time in centuries, creating the **Pax Mongolica** (c. 1250–c. 1350) — a period of relative peace and vigorous trade. Goods, ideas, and plague flowed freely across Eurasia.

### 2. The Indian Ocean Network (Topic 2.3)
The world's largest maritime trade system, linking East Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and China. Powered by **monsoon winds** and dominated by **Muslim merchants**, it spread Islam peacefully through trade and intermarriage. [[Zheng He]]'s voyages (1405–1433) demonstrated China's naval power at its height.

### 3. The Trans-Saharan Routes (Topic 2.4)
Camel caravans crossing the world's largest desert, connecting North Africa to the gold-rich kingdoms of West Africa ([[Mali Empire|Mali]], [[Songhai]]). Gold and salt were the key commodities. Islam traveled south with the merchants, transforming [[Timbuktu]] into one of the world's great centers of Islamic scholarship.

## The Unifying Themes of Unit 2

**1. Trade causes cultural diffusion** — wherever merchants go, religions, technologies, and ideas follow. Islam spread to West Africa, Southeast Asia, and East Africa primarily through trade, not conquest.

**2. Political stability enables trade** — the Mongols, the Mali Empire, and the Sultanate of Malacca all show how stable states grew wealthy by protecting and taxing trade.

**3. Trade has environmental consequences** — the most dramatic was the **[[Concept Notes/Black Death|Black Death]]**, which killed perhaps a third of Europe's population and tens of millions in Asia after traveling the same routes as silk and spices.

**4. Commercial innovation follows trade** — paper money, bills of exchange, banking houses, and credit systems all emerged to manage the increasing volume and complexity of trade.

## AP Themes Covered in Unit 2

- **Governance** — how empires (Mongol, Mali, Malacca) organized and taxed trade
- **Economic Systems** — commercial innovations, luxury goods demand, proto-industrialization
- **Cultural Developments** — spread of Islam, Buddhism, and Christianity via trade routes
- **Technology & Environment** — navigation tech (compass, lateen sails, astrolabe), Black Death
- **Human Migration** — diasporic merchant communities; spread of disease
