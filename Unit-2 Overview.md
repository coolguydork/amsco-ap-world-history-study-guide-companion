# Unit 2 Overview — Networks of Exchange
*c. 1200–c. 1450 | AP Period 1*

> *"I have indeed — it is well known — seen in my travelling things most wonderful and most strange. And I will tell our book all the truth of what I have seen and what I have not seen."*
> — Marco Polo, *The Travels of Marco Polo*, c. 1300

---

## What Is Unit 2 About?

Unit 1 showed you seven civilizations developing largely on their own terms. Unit 2 shows you what happened when they collided. Between c. 1200 and c. 1450, three great networks of exchange — the **Silk Roads**, the **Indian Ocean Basin**, and the **Trans-Saharan routes** — grew dramatically in volume, reach, and consequence. People, goods, religions, technologies, and diseases moved across hemispheres at a pace the world had never seen.

The AP exam's essential question for this unit is deceptively simple: **what were the causes and effects of the growth of networks of exchange after 1200?** The causes include geographical advantages, merchant enterprise, state support, and Mongol unification of Eurasia. The effects include cultural diffusion, technology transfer, the spread of Islam, and the most devastating pandemic in human history.

The unit's organizing insight is that **connectivity has costs as well as benefits**. Trade moved silk and spices — and it also moved the bubonic plague. Understanding both sides of that ledger is what the AP exam is testing.

## AP Themes in Unit 2

Unit 2 is the most theme-dense unit in the entire course. Six of the seven AP themes appear prominently.

**Economic Systems (ECN)** is the foundation of the unit. All three networks moved luxury goods — silk, spices, gold, ivory — but also bulk commodities like grain and salt. The critical economic concept is **specialization and comparative advantage**: regions traded what they produced best (West African gold, Chinese silk, Indian spices) for what they could not produce at home. State-sponsored infrastructure — caravanserais on the Silk Roads, port cities on the Indian Ocean — enabled this system to scale.

**Cultural Developments and Interactions (CDI)** explains the unit's biggest long-term consequences. Islam spread along every network simultaneously: Arab merchants carried it to Southeast Asia via the Indian Ocean, Berber traders carried it to West Africa via the trans-Saharan routes, and Persian scholars circulated it across Central Asia via the Silk Roads. Buddhism, Christianity, and Hinduism also moved along these routes, though with less reach than Islam in this period.

**Technology and Innovation (TEC)** runs as a thread through all three networks. The **lateen sail** allowed Indian Ocean dhows to tack against the wind. **Caravanserais** provided rest stops every 20–30 miles across Central Asian deserts. **The magnetic compass** and **astrolabe** improved navigation. Most consequentially, **paper money** from China and Islamic **bills of exchange** enabled high-volume commerce without hauling coins across continents.

**Governance (GOV)** enters through the Mongol Empire (Topic 2.2) and through state-building in Mali (Topic 2.4). The Mongols created the conditions for trade by unifying the Silk Roads under a single authority and establishing the **Yam postal system** — a relay network that moved messages and goods across Eurasia with unprecedented speed.

**Human Migration and Settlement (MIG)** shaped the Indian Ocean world especially. **Diasporic merchant communities** — Chinese merchants in Southeast Asia, Arab traders in East Africa, Indian merchants in Malacca — created permanent settlements along trade routes. These communities became cultural crossroads, translating goods, languages, and faiths between civilizations.

**Humans and the Environment (ENV)** takes center stage in Topic 2.6. The **Black Death** is the defining event: the plague (*Yersinia pestis*) traveled along the very trade routes that had built Unit 2's prosperity, killing roughly one-third of Europe's population by 1351. Agricultural diffusion (Champa rice, citrus, sugar) and the beginning of the **Little Ice Age** (c. 1300) also shaped the environmental story.

## The Three Networks: A Framework for Every Essay

The AP exam frequently asks you to compare, contrast, or connect the three major trade networks. Here is the framework you need:

| Feature | Silk Roads | Indian Ocean | Trans-Saharan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Type** | Overland caravan | Maritime | Overland caravan (desert) |
| **Primary technology** | Camel; caravanserais | Lateen-sail dhow; monsoon winds | Camel |
| **Primary goods** | Silk, porcelain, spices, paper | Spices, cotton, porcelain, gold, enslaved people | Gold, salt, enslaved people, ivory |
| **Dominant merchants** | Sogdians → Muslim merchants | Arab, Indian, and Chinese merchants | Berber and Arab merchants |
| **Key trading cities** | Chang'an, Samarkand, Aleppo | Malacca, Calicut, Kilwa | Timbuktu, Gao, Marrakesh |
| **Religion spread** | Buddhism, Islam, Christianity | Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism | Islam |
| **State that most shaped it** | Mongol Empire (Pax Mongolica) | Various; Ming China (Zheng He) | Mali Empire |
| **Peak years (c. 1200–1450)** | 1258–1368 (Pax Mongolica) | Continuous; peaks c. 1400 | c. 1200–1450 (Mali era) |

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 2.7 is the AP's synthesis topic for this unit — the exam will test your ability to identify **similarities** across the three networks, not just differences. The key similarities: all three moved luxury goods across long distances; all three depended on a "transport technology" suited to their environment (camel vs. dhow); all three facilitated the spread of Islam; all three were shaped by state power; and all three carried the Black Death simultaneously after 1347.

## The Mongol Factor

The [[Concept Notes/Mongols\|Mongol Empire]] (Topic 2.2) is the pivot of this entire unit. Without the Mongol unification of Eurasia, the Silk Roads' peak under the [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica\|Pax Mongolica]] would not have happened — and neither would the Black Death's westward journey.

The Mongols created a **security corridor** across Central Asia. Their **Yam postal system** connected China to Persia in a matter of weeks. Their religious **tolerance** allowed Christian, Buddhist, and Muslim merchants to operate freely across the empire. Marco Polo's famous journey (1271–1295) was only possible because a single empire controlled the entire route from Venice to Beijing.

At the same time, the Mongol conquests were catastrophic. The destruction of **Baghdad in 1258** — where Hulagu Khan killed hundreds of thousands and burned the House of Wisdom — ended the Abbasid Caliphate and sent shockwaves through the Islamic world. The depopulation of Persia and Central Asia from Mongol violence left some regions still recovering two centuries later.

> [!warning]
> Don't characterize the Mongols as simply "destroyers" or simply "trade facilitators." The AP exam expects nuance. The Mongols were **simultaneously** the greatest stimulus to long-distance trade in the medieval world **and** the perpetrators of some of the most catastrophic violence. The *Pax Mongolica* was real — and so was Baghdad 1258.

## Islam: The Thread That Runs Through Every Network

One of Unit 2's most important exam insights is that **Islam served as a common commercial and cultural language across all three networks simultaneously**. By c. 1200, Muslim merchants were already dominant in the Indian Ocean Basin. Arab and Berber traders controlled the trans-Saharan routes. And after the Mongol period opened the Silk Roads, Persian and Turkic Muslim merchants became the Silk Roads' dominant long-distance traders.

This gave Islam a structural advantage in spreading religion through commerce. When Muslim merchants settled permanently in Southeast Asian port cities, they built mosques, married local women, and raised children in the faith — a pattern of **peaceful commercial diffusion** rather than conquest. The result was the Islamization of the Malay Peninsula and coastal Java by c. 1400.

The Mali Empire (Topic 2.4) is the trans-Saharan version of this story. After Sundiata founded Mali in 1235, the empire's ruling class embraced Islam as a legitimizing ideology that also facilitated trade relationships with North African Muslim merchants. Mansa Musa's spectacular **hajj of 1324** — his pilgrimage to Mecca with a retinue of 60,000 and so much gold that he crashed Middle Eastern gold markets — is the unit's single most vivid primary-source moment.

## The Travelers: Primary Sources as Evidence

The AP exam expects you to use specific travelers as evidence. Each represents a different network and a different purpose.

**Marco Polo** (1271–1295) documented the Silk Roads at their Pax Mongolica peak. His account of Yuan China under Kublai Khan introduced Europeans to paper money, coal, and the Grand Canal. His reliability is debated, but his work is the primary European source on the Silk Roads and the AP exam treats it as evidence of the routes' cultural and commercial traffic.

**Ibn Battuta** (1325–1354) is the unit's most versatile traveler — he used all three networks. Born in Morocco, he traveled 75,000 miles over 29 years, visiting Dar al-Islam from West Africa to Southeast Asia. His accounts document the **social unity of the Islamic world**: wherever he went, he found qadis, madrasas, and mosques operating on the same institutional template. He is the best primary source for the cultural consequences of trade.

**Zheng He** (1405–1433) represents Chinese state power projected into the Indian Ocean. His seven voyages commanded the largest wooden ships ever built and reached East Africa — but his voyages were **tributary, not commercial**. China was not seeking trade profits; it was projecting imperial prestige. When the Ming court shut the voyages down after 1433, the decision reflected internal politics, not any failure of the Indian Ocean network itself.

## Connectivity's Dark Side: The Black Death

The [[Concept Notes/Black Death\|Black Death]] is the unit's most important environmental consequence — and its most powerful exam evidence for the **costs of connectivity**. The plague (*Yersinia pestis*) originated in Central Asian rodent populations and began spreading along Mongol trade routes in the 1330s. It reached China and the Middle East by the 1340s and arrived in Europe via Sicilian ports in 1347. By 1351, it had killed roughly 75–200 million people across Eurasia — approximately one-third of Europe's population.

For the AP exam, the Black Death matters because it illustrates three key patterns:

First, **trade routes transmitted disease as efficiently as goods**. The very infrastructure — caravanserais, port cities, ships — that accelerated commerce also accelerated contagion. Connectivity created a single epidemiological zone across Afro-Eurasia.

Second, the demographic collapse had **profound economic and social consequences**. Labor scarcity drove up wages for surviving workers, weakening feudal ties in Europe. Depopulation reduced demand for luxury goods, slowing the trade that had spread the disease in the first place.

Third, the Black Death demonstrates **causation and contingency**. The plague's westward spread was not inevitable — it was the direct consequence of Mongol political unification making Silk Roads travel faster and safer than ever before. The Pax Mongolica enabled the plague's continental journey.

## Agricultural Diffusion: Trade's Constructive Legacy

Not all environmental consequences were catastrophic. Trade routes also transmitted **new crops** that transformed regional agriculture. From Vietnam, **Champa rice** spread to Song China — a drought-resistant variety that yielded two harvests per year and drove a Chinese population boom from about 60 million to 120 million between 1000 and 1200. Indonesian seafarers introduced **bananas** to East Africa, enabling Bantu agricultural communities to settle in regions too wet for yams. Muslim merchants spread **sugar, cotton, and citrus** across the Mediterranean, transforming European diet and agriculture.

These agricultural exchanges show that the AP's concept of "environmental consequences" is not limited to catastrophe. New crops represent a positive feedback loop: higher food output → population growth → more demand for luxury goods → more trade → more agricultural exchange.

## Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

### What Changed, c. 1200–c. 1450?

The **volume and speed of long-distance trade** increased dramatically, driven by the Pax Mongolica (1206–1368) on the overland routes and by improvements in maritime technology on the Indian Ocean. Regions that had been loosely connected became densely networked.

**Islam's geographic reach** expanded dramatically in this period, especially into sub-Saharan West Africa (via trans-Saharan routes) and Southeast Asia (via the Indian Ocean). Both expansions were primarily commercial, not military.

The **Black Death** (1347–1351) was the period's most abrupt change: a demographic catastrophe that reduced population, disrupted trade, and weakened feudal institutions across Eurasia.

The **Mali Empire** (1235–1468) rose to become sub-Saharan Africa's most powerful state, replacing Ghana as the controller of the gold-salt trade. Timbuktu grew into one of the world's great Islamic intellectual centers.

### What Stayed the Same?

All three trade networks **continued to function** throughout the period, even surviving the Black Death's disruption. The structural advantages that had created them — monsoon winds, Saharan camel routes, overland Central Asian corridors — did not change.

**Luxury goods** remained the primary traded commodities: silk and porcelain from China, spices from Southeast Asia, gold and ivory from West Africa. Mass consumer goods would not become dominant until European commercial capitalism in Unit 4.

**Diasporic merchant communities** persisted as the organizational model for long-distance commerce. The pattern — merchants of one faith settling in foreign port cities, maintaining separate social institutions while integrating commercially — was as true in 1450 as it had been in 1200.

## How Unit 2 Connects Forward

Unit 2 sets up the next three units in critical ways. The exam will expect you to recall Unit 2 evidence when writing essays on later topics.

**To Unit 3 (Land-Based Empires):** The Mongol Empire's successor states — the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals — all drew on Mongol administrative models. Ottoman expansion eventually disrupted the Silk Roads (c. 1450), a key cause of European maritime exploration. The gunpowder technologies that the Mongols spread westward became the foundation for the "Gunpowder Empires" of Unit 3.

**To Unit 4 (Maritime Empires):** The decline of the Silk Roads pushed European merchants to seek oceanic alternatives. Zheng He's voyages (1405–1433) demonstrated that the Indian Ocean was reachable and profitable — though China chose not to exploit this. European sailors made the same journey sixty years later and chose differently. The trans-Saharan gold trade that enriched Mali was the same West African gold that Portuguese merchants sought when they began sailing down the African coast in the 1440s.

**To Unit 5 (Revolutions):** The Black Death's demographic aftermath in Europe — rising wages, weakening feudalism, new demands on the Church's authority — laid groundwork for the Renaissance, the Reformation, and early capitalism.

> [!exam-tip]
> Unit 2 is **the most important unit for cross-period LEQs**. The AP exam loves prompts that ask you to compare trade networks across periods (Unit 2 vs. Unit 4) or to evaluate the role of technology in trade (a thread from Unit 2 through Unit 6). Make sure you can deploy Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, and trans-Saharan evidence fluently — you will need it in essays well beyond Unit 2.

## Exam Strategy for Unit 2

**LEQ prompts commonly test:**

Causation LEQs asking what caused the growth of trade networks after 1200. Best answers address **multiple causes at multiple scales**: geographic (monsoon winds, Sahara as barrier/corridor), technological (caravanserais, lateen sail, compass), economic (comparative advantage, diasporic merchants), and political (Pax Mongolica, Mali state support).

Comparison LEQs asking you to compare two of the three trade networks. The framework table above gives you the structural comparison. Make sure to discuss **both similarities and differences** — the AP exam penalizes one-sided comparisons.

CCOT LEQs asking how trade networks changed and continued over the period c. 1200–c. 1450, or across a longer span. The Black Death (catastrophic disruption), the Mongol unification (enabling peak trade), and the Mali Empire's rise (sub-Saharan integration) are your three best change arguments.

**Best evidence pairings for essays:**

To argue **connectivity drove cultural change**: spread of Islam via Indian Ocean (Southeast Asia) + spread of Islam via trans-Saharan (Mali) + spread of papermaking via Silk Roads (Europe) = Islam and technology follow commerce.

To argue **state power shaped trade**: Pax Mongolica enables Silk Roads peak + Mali Empire controls trans-Saharan corridor + Zheng He's state-sponsored voyages project Ming power = states determine the scale of trade.

To argue **connectivity has costs**: Black Death spreads via Silk Roads → kills 1/3 of Europe; simultaneously spreads via trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean → demonstrates all three networks are a single system with shared vulnerabilities.

> [!exam-tip]
> Ibn Battuta is your single most versatile primary source for this unit. He traveled all three networks, documented Islamic cultural unity, and left accounts of Mali, the Indian Ocean, the Silk Roads, and Southeast Asia. If an SAQ or DBQ gives you an Ibn Battuta excerpt, use it to support arguments about cultural diffusion, the commercial spread of Islam, and the global reach of the trade networks.
