# Connections

Topic 6.8 is the synthesis capstone for Unit 6. Its connections radiate in two directions: backward into Unit 6 topics whose content it synthesizes, and forward into Unit 7 (the crises of the early 20th century that imperialism made possible).

---

## Within Unit 6: What 6.8 Synthesizes

**[[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview\|Rationales for Imperialism Overview]]**
[[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview|Topic 6.1]] establishes the ideological causes of imperialism (Social Darwinism, White Man's Burden, civilizing mission, nationalism). Topic 6.8 takes those ideological justifications and asks: what were the actual *effects*? The gap between the stated rationales and the lived consequences (dispossession, inequality, forced labor) is itself an AP argument about historical causation.

**[[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview\|State Expansion Overview]]**
[[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview|Topic 6.2]] covers the *mechanics* of how states expanded (military technology, treaties, settler colonialism). Topic 6.8 synthesizes the *consequences* of that expansion for colonized peoples — dependent economies, disrupted governance, migration.

**[[Unit-6.3/Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview\|Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview]]**
Topics 6.3 and 6.8 are deeply linked: 6.3 provides case studies of resistance; 6.8 asks you to evaluate the long-term significance of that resistance. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 and the Boxer Rebellion are examples in both topics.

**[[Unit-6.4/Global Economic Development Overview\|Global Economic Development Overview]]**
[[Unit-6.4/Global Economic Development Overview|Topic 6.4]] covers the economic transformation of the industrial era. Topic 6.8 traces how those transformations caused imperial expansion and global inequality — the dependent colonial economy model and the widening wealth gap.

**[[Unit-6.5/Economic Imperialism Overview\|Economic Imperialism Overview]]**
[[Unit-6.5/Economic Imperialism Overview|Topic 6.5]] covers informal empire — economic control without direct political rule (China, Ottoman Empire, Latin America). Topic 6.8 connects this to the broader pattern: both formal and informal imperialism created dependent economies and fueled resistance.

**[[Unit-6.6/Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World Overview\|Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World Overview]]**
[[Unit-6.6/Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World Overview|Topic 6.6]] identifies push and pull factors for migration. Topic 6.8 places migration in its causal context: imperialism and industrialization were the structural forces that set millions of people in motion.

**Effects of Migration [[Unit-6.7/Effects of Migration Overview|Effects of Migration Overview]]**
[[Unit-6.7/Effects of Migration Overview|Topic 6.7]] covers the social effects of migration (discrimination, enclaves, nativism). Topic 6.8 draws on those effects as one of the four major consequences of imperialism it asks students to evaluate in relative significance.

## Forward Connections to Unit 7

Topic 6.8 explicitly names several developments that will become Unit 7 content:

**Global rivalries → World War I:** The European political rivalries intensified by competition for colonies were a contributing cause of WWI. The alliance system, the arms race, and the imperial competition for Africa and Asia created the volatile geopolitical environment that ignited in 1914.

**Nascent independence movements → 20th-century decolonization:** The Indian Rebellion of 1857, Boxer Rebellion, and African resistance movements are the roots of the independence movements that will succeed in Unit 9 (1900–present). The key argument: imperialism inadvertently created the conditions for its own undoing by spreading nationalist ideology and creating educated indigenous elites.

**Indentured labor → global diaspora communities:** The migration patterns established in this period created lasting diaspora communities (Indian workers in Fiji, South Africa, and the Caribbean; Chinese communities in Southeast Asia and the Americas) that shape global demographics to this day.
