# Key Terms & Concepts

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 6.8 has no dedicated key terms list in AMSCO — it is a synthesis/causation topic. The terms below are drawn from the narrative (pp. 439–442) and are the conceptual vocabulary needed to write analytically about imperialism's causes and effects. Knowing these lets you write precise, exam-ready arguments.

**Industrial capitalism**
An economic system in which private owners use capital (machinery, factories) to produce goods for profit, with labor hired in exchange for wages. Industrial capitalism drove the overproduction crisis that pushed European states toward imperial expansion — more goods needed more markets and more raw materials.

**Real wages**
Wages adjusted for inflation, measuring actual purchasing power rather than nominal dollar amounts. Real wages roughly doubled in Britain between 1819 and 1852, suggesting rising standards of living for industrial workers — though this masked growing inequality and urban hardships.

**Overproduction**
A condition in which industrial output exceeds what domestic markets can absorb. Overproduction was a core cause of 19th-century imperialism: industrialized nations needed foreign markets for surplus goods and foreign territories for raw materials. It drove the shift from mercantilist to capitalist trade systems.

**Dependent colonial economy**
An economy structured to supply raw materials and agricultural products to an imperial power, rather than developing its own industrial capacity. Colonial economies were made *dependent* — they could not industrialize and were vulnerable to commodity price swings. This is a key AP concept for explaining global economic inequality.

**Cash crops**
Agricultural products grown for export and sale rather than local consumption (e.g., cotton, rubber, sugar, coffee). Imperial powers introduced cash crop monocultures in colonies, which increased vulnerability to natural disasters and market volatility and reinforced economic dependence.

**Settler colonialism**
A form of colonialism in which colonizers permanently settle in a territory, displacing or eliminating indigenous populations. Distinguished from extractive colonialism (exploiting resources with indigenous or imported labor) by its demographic transformation of the colonized region. Most significant in the Americas, Australia, and parts of Africa during this period.

**Indentured labor**
A system in which workers sign contracts to labor for a set period (typically 5–7 years) in exchange for transport to a new location. After completing their indenture, workers could remain or return home. Indentured laborers from China, Japan, South Asia, and Europe were recruited across the Americas, Southeast Asia, Africa, and Australia to fill labor shortages after the end of chattel slavery.

> [!warning]
> Indentured labor and chattel slavery are distinct. Indentured laborers had legal contracts and theoretically retained rights; enslaved people were property with no legal personhood. However, the conditions of indenture were often exploitative, and the AP exam may ask you to compare and contrast these labor systems.

**Enclaves**
Immigrant communities clustered together in a specific neighborhood or area, maintaining cultural practices, language, and social networks from their home countries. Enclaves provided mutual support but could also reinforce social segregation and fuel nativist resentment from the host population.

**Westernization**
The adoption of Western (primarily European) cultural, political, and economic practices by non-Western societies. In colonized regions, Westernization was often imposed by imperial powers through education, religion, and law. Local populations frequently resisted Westernization as a threat to cultural identity and political autonomy.

**Nascent independence movements**
Early, formative anti-colonial movements that had not yet achieved political independence. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 and the Boxer Rebellion in China were nascent independence movements — they failed militarily but demonstrated that imperial control was contested and planted the seeds for 20th-century nationalist independence movements.

**Sepoys**
Indian soldiers employed by the [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]]. Sepoys formed the backbone of British military power in India. Their rebellion in 1857 (the Sepoy Mutiny or Indian Rebellion) was a pivotal event that ended Company rule and transferred authority to the British Crown — beginning the period known as the British Raj.

**British Raj**
The period of direct Crown rule over India, from 1858 to 1947, following the dissolution of the [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] after the Rebellion of 1857. The Raj was the centerpiece of the British Empire and the most populous colonial territory of the 19th century.

**Boxer Rebellion**
A Chinese nationalist uprising (1899–1901) led by the Society of Righteous and Harmonious Fists (the "Boxers") against Western imperial influence, Christian missionaries, and foreign economic penetration of China. It was suppressed by a multinational military force, and China was forced to pay enormous reparations — further deepening its subjugation.

> [!tip]
> The Boxer Rebellion is often tested in comparison with the Indian Rebellion of 1857. Both were anti-Western responses to imperialism; both failed militarily; both accelerated rather than reversed imperial control in the short term; both contributed to the eventual collapse of old imperial regimes (Mughal/Qing).

**Causation (AP Historical Reasoning Skill)**
The skill of identifying and explaining causes and effects in history, and evaluating their *relative significance*. For Topic 6.8, causation means: (1) identifying what caused imperialism (industrialization, overproduction, competition), (2) identifying what imperialism caused (migration, rebellion, inequality), and (3) arguing *which* cause or effect mattered most, and why.

> [!exam-tip]
> Relative significance is not about listing the most effects — it's about making an argument. Use the four criteria: depth of change, number of people affected, duration, and importance of the change. State your claim, provide evidence, and explain why your chosen effect outweighs the others.
