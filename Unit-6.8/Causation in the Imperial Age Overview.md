# Causation in the Imperial Age

**AMSCO pp. 439–449 | AP Period 3 | Skill: Causation**

Topic 6.8 is the synthesis and causation topic for Unit 6. Rather than introducing new content, it asks students to evaluate the *relative significance* of the multiple effects of industrialization and imperialism from 1750 to 1900. The core question is not just *what* happened, but *how much did it matter* and *why did it happen*.

> [!exam-tip]
> This topic is explicitly tagged **Causation** — the AP exam loves to ask you to explain what caused imperialism AND what effects imperialism caused. Be ready to argue relative significance: which effect was most important, and why, using the criteria of depth of change, number of people affected, duration, and importance of the change.

## The Core Causal Chain

Industrial capitalism set off a chain reaction that reshaped the world between 1750 and 1900. Increased manufacturing capacity created **overproduction** — more goods than domestic markets could absorb. This pushed industrialized nations to seek foreign markets and raw materials, fueling imperial expansion. Competition among European powers for colonies and resources intensified geopolitical rivalries that would eventually explode in World War I.

## Effect 1: Changes in Standards of Living (AMSCO p. 440)

Industrialization produced more consumer goods — textiles, home furnishings, clothing, porcelain — and drove prices down. **Real wages** (wages adjusted for inflation) roughly doubled in Britain between 1819 and 1852. By these measures, standards of living rose for people across income groups in industrializing nations.

The picture was not uniformly positive, however. The **wealth gap** widened as income distribution became more unequal. Urbanization brought pollution and overcrowding. And for colonized peoples, industrialization meant deindustrialization — their economies were restructured to supply raw materials to Europe rather than develop their own manufacturing capacity. The gap between industrial and non-industrial societies grew dramatically across this period.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate rising living standards for industrial workers in Europe with rising living standards globally. For colonized regions, participation in the world economy often meant dependence, vulnerability to market shocks, and extraction of wealth — not development.

## Effect 2: Overseas Expansion (AMSCO p. 441)

As domestic markets became saturated, industrialized nations turned to empire-building. New territories supplied raw materials (coal, iron, tin, bauxite, rubber, copper) and absorbed surplus production. This was not entirely new — European colonialism had begun in the 1400s–1500s — but it dramatically accelerated in the 19th century.

Regions that had previously resisted European control were now overwhelmed. Most of Africa fell under direct European rule by 1900. Great Britain colonized large portions of South Asia; France dominated Southeast Asia. Even China, the largest and most powerful civilization of the pre-industrial era, was forced into unequal treaties and subjected to Western and Japanese imperial domination.

The key enabler was technology: advances in **military technology** (repeating rifles, machine guns), **shipbuilding** (steam-powered ironclads), and **medicine** (quinine for malaria) gave industrialized nations decisive military advantages over non-industrialized regions.

> [!tip]
> The phrase "the gap between industrial and non-industrial societies" is a crucial AP concept. You can use it to explain almost any comparative question about this era — why Europe dominated, why standards of living diverged, why independence movements emerged later.

## Effect 3: Seeds of Revolution and Rebellion (AMSCO p. 441)

Imperialism planted the seeds of resistance that would bloom into full independence movements in the 20th century. Two key examples:

**Indian Rebellion of 1857 (Sepoy Mutiny):** Indian soldiers (*sepoys*) employed by the [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] revolted against British rule. The rebellion was suppressed, but its consequence was the **dissolution of the Company** and the beginning of **direct Crown rule** — the British Raj. The rebellion also demonstrated that anti-colonial sentiment was widespread and could mobilize both Hindu and Muslim Indians.

**Boxer Rebellion (China, 1899–1901):** Chinese nationalists (the Boxers) attempted to expel Western and Japanese influence, particularly Christian missionaries. Though suppressed by a multinational force, the rebellion foreshadowed the collapse of the Qing Dynasty and the Chinese nationalist movements of the early 20th century.

Both rebellions failed militarily but succeeded in demonstrating the limits of imperial control and galvanizing nationalist consciousness. Earlier, in the Americas, former colonial territories had already achieved independence (U.S. in 1776, Latin American states in the early 1800s), providing a model for later movements.

## Effect 4: Migration and Discrimination (AMSCO p. 442)

Imperialism drove massive migration. People moved from rural to urban areas within industrializing countries, from less-developed to more-developed parts of Europe, and from colonial regions to industrial economies seeking labor. **Indentured laborers** from China, Japan, South Asia, and Europe traveled to the Americas, Southeast Asia, Africa, and Australia — signing contracts to work for set periods, often in exploitative conditions.

Immigrant communities formed **enclaves** — ethnic neighborhoods providing community but reinforcing segregation. Immigrants regularly faced discrimination based on race, religion, or national origin. Working-class people in industrial economies viewed immigrant labor as competition, fueling racial and ethnic hostility. The wealth accumulation of the middle and upper classes contrasted sharply with the precarious position of working-class and immigrant populations.

## Relative Significance: How to Argue It

The AP exam will ask you to evaluate which effect was *most significant*. Use these criteria:

| Criterion | What to assess |
|-----------|---------------|
| Depth of change | How fundamentally did it alter people's lives? |
| Number of people | How many people were affected globally? |
| Duration | How long did the effect last? |
| Importance of change | Did it set the stage for further major changes? |

A strong argument might prioritize **overseas expansion and global inequality** because it affected the largest number of people (hundreds of millions across Asia, Africa, and Latin America), lasted well into the 21st century in the form of persistent economic inequalities, and directly caused the political rivalries that led to World War I. Alternatively, you could argue for **migration and demographic change** as most significant because it permanently reshaped the populations of entire continents.

> [!exam-tip]
> For SAQs and LEQs, don't just list effects — rank them and defend your ranking. The AP rubric rewards analytical arguments, not descriptive lists.
