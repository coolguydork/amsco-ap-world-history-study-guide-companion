# Cold War Overview

**Unit 8 — Cold War and Decolonization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 554–561**

**Essential Question:** What were the causes and effects of the ideological struggle of the Cold War?

## The Big Picture

After World War II, the United States and the Soviet Union emerged as the world's two superpowers — and they were ideologically incompatible. The US championed capitalism and democracy; the USSR championed communism and authoritarian single-party rule. This fundamental divide shaped global politics for roughly forty-five years in what became known as the Cold War: a sustained rivalry characterized by ideological competition, proxy conflicts, arms races, and a persistent threat of nuclear annihilation — but no direct military confrontation between the superpowers themselves.

The Cold War was not inevitable. In 1945, the wartime allies cooperated to establish the **[[Concept Notes/United Nations|United Nations]]** as a successor to the failed League of Nations. But within two years, the cooperative spirit had collapsed. Soviet expansion into Eastern Europe, the US response via the **Truman Doctrine** and **Marshall Plan**, and the intensifying nuclear arms race locked the two powers into a global competition that neither could easily exit.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the **ideological differences** between the US and USSR and the specific policies each used to advance their interests (Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, COMECON). Know these as responses to specific events, not just abstract concepts.

## Origins of the Cold War

The roots of Cold War tension lay in the **Potsdam Conference** (1945), where Truman and Stalin first clashed over the postwar order. The wartime partnership dissolved quickly as the Soviets moved to consolidate control over Eastern Europe. Winston Churchill's March 1946 speech captured the emerging divide with the phrase **Iron Curtain** — a metaphor for the wall descending across Europe separating Soviet-controlled East from democratic West.

The two systems differed fundamentally in economics and governance. In capitalist countries, private ownership drove economic decisions. In communist countries, state ownership and centralized planning organized the economy around collective goals. Politically, the US held free elections and a free press; the USSR held no meaningful elections and controlled information through state media.

> [!warning]
> The Cold War was **ideological** as much as strategic. Don't reduce it to a simple power struggle — both sides genuinely believed their system was superior and worth spreading globally.

## US Containment Policy

The US response to Soviet expansion was shaped by diplomat **George Kennan**, who argued that the Soviets would keep expanding their influence if unchecked. His solution — **containment** — became the cornerstone of US Cold War strategy: prevent communism from spreading beyond its existing foothold, but don't necessarily roll it back.

President Truman translated containment into action through two major initiatives:

- The **Truman Doctrine** (1947): pledged US economic and military support to countries resisting communist takeover, beginning with Greece and Turkey.
- The **Marshall Plan** (1948): provided $13 billion in economic aid to rebuild Western Europe, based on the logic that economic instability breeds communist revolution.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Marshall Plan is a classic AP example of **economic statecraft** — using financial aid as a tool of foreign policy. The US wasn't being purely altruistic; rebuilding Europe served American strategic interests by preventing communist revolutions in economically devastated countries.

## Soviet Response

The USSR refused Marshall Plan aid and created its own response — the **Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (COMECON)** in 1947 — to coordinate trade and economic policy among its **satellite countries** in Eastern Europe (Bulgaria, East Germany, Hungary, Poland, Romania). These nations were directed to follow Soviet five-year plans, banned non-communist parties, and were forced to trade exclusively within the Soviet bloc.

The Soviets justified their control over Eastern Europe partly through the concept of **world revolution**: Marxist ideology held that communist revolution was historically inevitable and that the capitalist world posed a permanent threat to socialist societies.

## The Arms Race and Space Race

The Cold War rivalry extended into technology and military capability. In 1957, the Soviets launched **Sputnik**, the world's first artificial satellite, inaugurating the Space Race. The US launched its first satellite in 1958. Both nations competed to be first to orbit astronauts, then to land on the moon.

More consequential was the arms race. By 1957, both nations had tested intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) capable of delivering nuclear warheads across continents. This created the doctrine of **Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD)**: since any nuclear attack would guarantee retaliation that obliterated both sides, neither had an incentive to strike first. The balance of terror became the Cold War's de facto peace mechanism.

> [!warning]
> MAD is not simply "they couldn't fight because they'd both die." It's a strategic doctrine — the deliberate maintenance of enough second-strike capability to deter a first strike. The logic only works if both sides believe the other will actually retaliate.

## The Non-Aligned Movement

Many newly independent African and Asian nations refused to join either Cold War camp. In 1955, Indonesia hosted the **Bandung Conference**, where representatives of 29 nations — more than half the world's population — condemned colonialism and sought an alternative to superpower domination. This impulse led to the formal creation of the **Non-Aligned Movement** in 1961.

Leaders like India's **Jawaharlal Nehru**, Ghana's **Kwame Nkrumah**, Egypt's **Gamal Abdel Nasser**, and Indonesia's **Sukarno** advocated for independent foreign policies. In practice, non-alignment proved difficult — member states often tilted toward one superpower or the other when their national interests demanded it.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Non-Aligned Movement is crucial for AP essays about **decolonization and Cold War**: newly independent nations weren't passive players — they actively tried to leverage Cold War competition for their own benefit, playing superpowers against each other for aid.
