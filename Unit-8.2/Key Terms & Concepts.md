# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 8.2 — The Cold War | AMSCO pp. 554–561**

---

**[[Concept Notes/United Nations|United Nations]] (UN)**
International organization established in 1945 to replace the failed League of Nations. Unlike the League, it included the world's major powers (US, USSR, UK, China) and was designed to act quickly to prevent small conflicts from escalating. Despite its founding amid Cold War tensions, it served as a key forum for international diplomacy — though the US-Soviet rivalry often paralyzed its Security Council.

**Iron Curtain**
Churchill's 1946 metaphor for the political and ideological division between Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe and the democratic West. The phrase captured the abrupt closing off of Eastern European nations from Western influence — physically realized later by the Berlin Wall (1961). On the AP exam, it signals the symbolic start of the Cold War era.

**satellite countries**
Small states economically and politically subordinate to a larger power — in Cold War usage, the Eastern European nations (Bulgaria, East Germany, Hungary, Poland, Romania) dominated by the USSR after WWII. They were required to adopt Soviet-style economic planning, ban non-communist parties, and trade exclusively within the Soviet bloc. The relationship mirrored imperialism in practice even as the Soviets condemned Western imperialism in rhetoric.

**world revolution**
Marxist-Leninist belief that communist revolution was historically inevitable and that organized workers would eventually overthrow capitalism in all countries. This ideological framework made the USSR inherently expansionist in Western eyes — the Soviets supported communist uprisings in Germany, Hungary, and elsewhere in the early 1920s, then again after WWII. It fed Western fears that gave rise to the Truman Doctrine.

**containment**
US foreign policy strategy articulated by diplomat George Kennan (1946) that aimed to prevent the spread of Soviet communism beyond its existing boundaries — without necessarily trying to roll it back where it already existed. Containment became the dominant framework for US Cold War strategy and justified intervention in Korea, Vietnam, and dozens of other proxy conflicts over four decades.

**Truman Doctrine**
1947 policy declaration by President Harry Truman committing the United States to support any nation threatened by communist takeover. Specifically triggered by crises in Greece (communist insurgency) and Turkey (Soviet pressure over the Dardanelles strait), the Doctrine marked the US's formal rejection of postwar isolationism. It gave containment its official presidential endorsement and set the precedent for decades of US intervention abroad.

**Marshall Plan**
US economic aid program (1948) that provided $13 billion to rebuild war-devastated Western European economies. Secretary of State George Marshall proposed the plan based on the logic that economic instability enabled communist revolution — so the best way to contain communism was to rebuild prosperity. It worked: Western Europe's output was 35% higher by 1951 than in 1938. The USSR refused to participate and barred its satellites from doing so.

**Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (COMECON)**
Soviet economic organization (1947) created as a response to the Marshall Plan. It coordinated trade and economic policy among the USSR and its Eastern European satellite states. Unlike the Marshall Plan, COMECON was primarily focused on integrating satellite economies into the Soviet system rather than spurring independent growth — and its economic impact was far more modest.

**Sputnik**
The world's first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union in 1957. Sputnik shocked Americans by demonstrating Soviet technological capability and triggered the Space Race. It also had military implications: if the Soviets could launch a satellite into orbit, they could deliver nuclear warheads anywhere on Earth. Congress responded by massively increasing science education funding (National Defense Education Act) and creating NASA (1958).

**mutual assured destruction (MAD)**
Strategic nuclear doctrine holding that any first strike by either superpower would guarantee a devastating retaliatory strike — meaning both sides would be destroyed in any nuclear war. MAD served as the Cold War's paradoxical peace mechanism: the very certainty of mutual annihilation deterred either side from starting a nuclear conflict. It required both sides to continuously maintain and modernize their nuclear arsenals to guarantee credible second-strike capability.

**Non-Aligned Movement**
Formal organization (established 1961) of nations that refused to align with either the US or USSR during the Cold War. Its roots lay in the 1955 Bandung Conference hosted by Indonesia. Key leaders included Nehru (India), Nasser (Egypt), Nkrumah (Ghana), and Sukarno (Indonesia). In practice, non-alignment was difficult to maintain — member states often accepted aid from one superpower or tilted toward one side when conflicts demanded it.

**Bandung Conference**
1955 conference in Bandung, Indonesia, bringing together 29 African and Asian nations representing over half the world's population. Delegates passed resolutions condemning colonialism and seeking an alternative to Cold War superpower domination. It was the precursor to the Non-Aligned Movement and a landmark assertion that newly decolonized nations would chart their own path.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam often pairs the **Truman Doctrine + Marshall Plan** as two sides of the same coin: military/political containment and economic containment. They're best understood as a package, not separate policies.

> [!tip]
> **COMECON vs. Marshall Plan:** Marshall Plan → rebuilds independent capitalist economies; COMECON → integrates satellites into Soviet command economy. The structural difference explains why Marshall Plan worked and COMECON didn't produce comparable growth.
