# People, States & Movements

## Allied Leaders

**Winston Churchill** (1874–1965) — British Prime Minister
Churchill took power in May 1940, just as Germany overran France. He rallied the British public through the Battle of Britain with defiant oratory ("We shall never surrender") while forging the crucial Anglo-American alliance with Roosevelt. His strategic priority was keeping Britain alive until the U.S. entered the war. Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin formed the "Big Three" Allied leadership; tensions over post-war Europe foreshadowed the Cold War. His correspondence with Roosevelt produced the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Charter|Atlantic Charter]] (1941), which outlined post-war [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]] and freedom principles.

**Franklin D. Roosevelt** (1882–1945) — U.S. President
FDR led the United States from isolationism to full wartime mobilization. Before Pearl Harbor he worked around congressional restraints through the Destroyers-for-Bases Agreement and Lend-Lease Act. After December 7, 1941, he directed the largest military-industrial buildup in history. He died in April 1945, leaving Harry Truman to make the decision to use atomic bombs. Roosevelt's "Europe First" strategy — prioritizing defeat of Germany before Japan — shaped Allied operations.

**Joseph Stalin** (1878–1953) — Soviet Premier
Stalin bore the greatest human burden of WWII: approximately 27 million Soviets died. His forces endured and ultimately reversed the German invasion, grinding down the Wehrmacht through sheer industrial output and sacrifice. Victories at Stalingrad and Kursk were primarily Soviet achievements. Stalin pushed relentlessly for a second front (D-Day) to relieve Eastern Front pressure and at Yalta (1945) secured Soviet influence over Eastern Europe — seeds of the Cold War.

**Dwight D. Eisenhower** (1890–1969) — Supreme Allied Commander, Europe
"Ike" commanded the Allied invasion of North Africa (1942–43), Sicily, and Italy before being named Supreme Allied Commander for the D-Day invasion. His greatest challenge was coordinating multinational forces and managing difficult personalities (especially Montgomery and Patton). D-Day's success is largely credited to his meticulous planning. He later became U.S. President (1953–61).

**Douglas MacArthur** (1880–1964) — Supreme Allied Commander, Pacific
MacArthur commanded Allied forces in the Southwest Pacific after escaping the Japanese conquest of the Philippines ("I shall return"). His island-hopping strategy advanced Allied forces toward Japan while minimizing casualties by bypassing fortified positions. He presided over Japan's formal surrender ceremony on the USS Missouri (September 2, 1945). His subsequent role governing occupied Japan guided its democratic reconstruction.

**Harry S. Truman** (1884–1972) — U.S. President
Truman assumed the presidency in April 1945 with the war nearly won in Europe and the Pacific campaign ongoing. He made the most consequential decision of the war — to use atomic bombs against Japan — to avoid an estimated million-casualty invasion of the Japanese home islands. The decision remains historically debated (see Themes & Historical Reasoning).

## Axis Leaders

**Adolf Hitler** (1889–1945) — German Führer
Hitler directed German strategy personally, often overriding professional generals. His strategic gambles — Blitzkrieg, Operation Barbarossa, declaring war on the United States — succeeded initially but proved catastrophically overextended. He refused to allow tactical retreats (costing Germany its Sixth Army at Stalingrad) and spent his final days in a Berlin bunker, committing suicide April 30, 1945. See [[Unit-7.6/Causes of World War II Overview\|Causes of World War II Overview]] for his rise.

**Erwin Rommel** (1891–1944) — German Field Marshal
The "Desert Fox" commanded the Afrika Korps in North Africa (1941–1943), threatening British control of Egypt and the Suez Canal. He was respected by both sides for tactical brilliance and relative humane conduct. Defeated at El Alamein (1942), he was later implicated in the July 1944 assassination plot against Hitler and forced to commit suicide. His reputation has made him an atypical subject of WWII historiography — often discussed separately from Nazi ideology.

**Hideki Tojo** (1884–1948) — Japanese Prime Minister and War Minister
Tojo was the driving force behind Japan's decision to attack Pearl Harbor and expand into Southeast Asia. As both Prime Minister and War Minister, he exercised near-total control over Japanese war policy. He was convicted and executed as a war criminal after Japan's defeat.

## States in WWII

**The Allied Powers**
The core Allied alliance consisted of Britain (from 1939), the USSR (from June 1941), and the United States (from December 1941), plus China and numerous colonial armies. Despite profound ideological differences, they united around unconditional Axis surrender. The alliance was held together by shared strategic interests, the Lend-Lease supply network, and personal diplomacy among the Big Three.

**The Axis Powers**
Germany, Italy, and Japan formed the Axis alliance through the Rome-Berlin Axis (1936) and Tripartite Pact (1940). Their coordination was looser than the Allies'; each pursued separate imperial goals. Italy collapsed in 1943; Germany in 1945; Japan in 1945. See [[Unit-7.6/Causes of World War II Overview\|Causes of World War II Overview]] for Axis formation.

**Vichy France**
After Germany's conquest of France (1940), the collaborationist Vichy regime under Marshal Pétain governed southern France and the French overseas empire. Vichy cooperated with German racial policies, including deporting French Jews. The Free French resistance under Charles de Gaulle, backed by Britain, maintained a rival claim to French sovereignty. Vichy represents the range of occupied-nation responses: from collaboration to resistance.

**Colonial Armies**
Colonies from across the British, French, and Dutch empires contributed soldiers to Allied forces. India's army grew from 200,000 to 2.5 million — the largest volunteer army in history — fighting in North Africa and Southeast Asia. Colonial participation in WWII strengthened demands for independence after the war, accelerating decolonization.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP essays frequently reward noting that colonial subjects fought for empires that denied them rights — their wartime service became leverage for postwar independence movements. This connects WWII conduct ([[Unit-7.7/Conducting World War II Overview|7.7]]) directly to decolonization (Unit 8).

> [!tip]
> **"Big Three" comparison:** Churchill prioritized Europe; Stalin demanded a second front; Roosevelt balanced both. Their agreements at Yalta (1945) divided post-war Europe and planted the seeds of the Cold War.
