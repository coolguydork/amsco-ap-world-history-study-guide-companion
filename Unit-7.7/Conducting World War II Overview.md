# Conducting World War II

**AP Period 4 | Unit 7 | Printed pp. 511–520**
**AP Themes:** GOV, TEC, SIO | **AP Skill:** Causation

**Essential Question:** How was WWII fought, and what were its consequences for the international order?

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about **WWII turning points** (Stalingrad, Midway, D-Day), **total war mobilization** compared to WWI, and **the decision to use atomic bombs** as a historiographical debate. Also watch for comparisons of how different governments (US, Germany, Japan) mobilized their home fronts.

## Overview

World War II was the deadliest conflict in human history, killing approximately 75 million people — two-thirds of them civilians. Governments mobilized entire societies through propaganda, industrial production, and ideological appeals in what historians call **total war**. New military technologies — tanks, aircraft carriers, strategic bombing, and ultimately nuclear weapons — transformed the scale and lethality of warfare under a framework historians call [[Concept Notes/Total War|Total War]].

## Japan's Imperial Expansion

Japan's military government sought to build a "New Order in East Asia," controlling Southeast Asia's oil and raw materials to sustain its war in China. When the German-Soviet [[Concept Notes/Nonaggression Pact|Nonaggression Pact]] (1939) blocked Japanese expansion into Siberia, Japan pivoted south. U.S. economic sanctions over China threatened to strangle Japan's economy, prompting Japan to strike the U.S. Pacific Fleet at **Pearl Harbor** on December 7, 1941 — a gamble that brought the United States into the war and made WWII truly global. Japan rapidly seized the Philippines, Dutch East Indies, Malaya, and Burma, threatening Australia.

## Germany's Early Victories

Hitler deployed **blitzkrieg** (lightning war) — fast tank columns with air support — to conquer Poland (4 weeks), Denmark, Norway, and France (6 weeks). France's fall produced the collaborationist **Vichy** regime in southern France. Britain alone held out in Western Europe. The **Battle of Britain** (1940) saw the Luftwaffe bombard British cities and bases for months; Britain's RAF and superior radar system prevented invasion, marking Germany's first strategic defeat.

In June 1941 Hitler launched **Operation Barbarossa**, attacking the USSR. Initial successes gave way to the brutal **Siege of Leningrad** (900 days, ~1 million deaths) and eventual German overextension. The United States supported Britain through the **Destroyers-for-Bases Agreement** (1940) and the **Lend-Lease Act** (1941), abandoning any pretense of neutrality before Pearl Harbor.

## Turning Points: 1942

The year 1942 was decisive in both theaters:

- **European/North Africa:** General Rommel threatened Egypt but was defeated at the **Battle of El Alamein**. A Soviet counteroffensive destroyed the German Sixth Army at **Stalingrad** — Germany's first catastrophic defeat, turning the tide in Europe.
- **Pacific:** The **Battle of the Coral Sea** stopped Japan's drive toward Australia. The **Battle of Midway Island** destroyed four Japanese carriers and handed the U.S. Navy permanent strategic superiority. The **Guadalcanal** campaign (1942–43) was the first major Allied ground offensive.

## Allied Victory

Allied strategy in the Pacific was **island-hopping** — seizing weakly defended islands and bypassing fortified ones, cutting supply lines and advancing toward Japan. **Aircraft carriers** proved decisive, extending naval air power across vast ocean distances.

In Europe, after liberating North Africa and Italy, the Allies launched the massive **D-Day** amphibious assault at Normandy (June 6, 1944) under Eisenhower's command. Germany's final offensive — the **Battle of the Bulge** (winter 1944) — failed, exhausting its reserves. On the Eastern Front, the **Battle of Kursk** (1943) confirmed Soviet dominance. Hitler committed suicide on April 30, 1945; Germany surrendered May 8 (**V-E Day**).

In the Pacific, after fire-bombing Tokyo and capturing Okinawa and Iwo Jima, the U.S. dropped atomic bombs on **Hiroshima** (August 6) and **Nagasaki** (August 9), 1945. Japan surrendered unconditionally on **V-J Day** (September 2, 1945).

## Home Fronts and Total War

The U.S. converted industrial capacity to war production with minimal government interference, using propaganda like "Rosie the Riveter" to draw women into factories. The USSR accepted staggering sacrifices. Germany relied on **forced labor** — 20% of its wartime workforce — with predictably low productivity. Japan's government prioritized morale over mobilization and refused to deploy women.

Colonial armies played important roles: India's volunteer army grew from 200,000 to 2.5 million — the largest in history — fighting in North Africa and Southeast Asia.

## Consequences

WWII's aftermath redrew the world order: it discredited European colonialism (colonies that fought expected independence), launched the Cold War between the U.S. and USSR, birthed the nuclear age, and created the international institutions (UN, World Bank, IMF) designed to prevent future catastrophic wars.
