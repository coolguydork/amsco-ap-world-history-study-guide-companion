# Chapter 6: Developments in Europe (c. 1200–1450)

#apwh #unit-1 #period-1

## Chapter Summary

Medieval Europe (c. 1200–1450) was a fragmented region organized around feudalism, manorialism, and the authority of the Catholic Church. While less commercially and institutionally powerful than the Islamic world or Song China of the same era, Europe was not stagnant: the period 1000–1300 saw real population growth, urban revival, the rise of Italian merchant banking, and a flourishing of cathedral building and university culture. The Crusades created sustained contact with the Islamic and Byzantine worlds, universities and scholasticism advanced intellectual life, and the catastrophic [[Black Death]] reshaped demographics, labor relations, and social hierarchies. By 1450, Europe stood on the threshold of dramatic transformation — weakened feudal structures, rising monarchies, and intensified trade set the stage for the Renaissance and eventual global expansion.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests how the **Black Death** caused labor shortages that weakened feudalism and shifted power toward peasants and urban workers. Know this cause-and-effect chain cold.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain how and why states, empires, and other political entities developed and competed for power in medieval Europe
- Describe the role of the Catholic Church in shaping European culture, politics, and society
- Analyze the causes and effects of the Crusades on Europe and the wider world
- Explain how the Black Death disrupted and transformed European social and economic structures
- Describe the development of trade networks and early capitalism in medieval Europe

## Key Themes at a Glance

- **Governance**: Feudal decentralization vs. Church authority; rise of representative institutions (Magna Carta, Parliament)
- **Culture**: Catholic Church dominance; universities and scholasticism; the Crusades as cultural encounter
- **Economy**: Manorial system; revival of trade and towns; early banking and merchant class
- **Society**: Rigid three-estates hierarchy disrupted by plague; emergence of new social mobility
- **Technology & Environment**: The Black Death as environmental catastrophe; agricultural innovations (three-field system)
