# Themes and Causation — Chapter 6: Developments in Europe (c. 1200–1450)

#apwh #unit-1 #period-1

> [!tip]
> The AP exam rewards **cause-and-effect reasoning**. For each theme, identify what *caused* the development and what it *led to*.

---

## 🏛️ Governance

Medieval Europe was politically fragmented: no single ruler controlled the continent. Instead, power was distributed among the Catholic Church (which claimed spiritual authority over all Christians), feudal lords (who controlled land and local military power), and monarchs (who varied greatly in strength). The signing of the Magna Carta in 1215 established a crucial precedent — that kings were subject to law — and the gradual development of Parliament in England showed that representative institutions could check royal power. The [[Hundred Years' War]] accelerated centralization in France and England, as both monarchies needed to mobilize national resources, weakening local feudal lords in the process. Meanwhile, the [[Holy Roman Empire]] failed to centralize, leaving Germany politically fragmented for centuries.

> [!exam-tip]
> Compare European feudal decentralization with the centralized bureaucratic states of Song China or Abbasid Caliphate — a common AP comparison question.

## 💰 Economic Systems

The medieval European economy was dominated by manorialism — peasants worked the land of lords in exchange for protection, with little market exchange. However, from roughly 1000–1300, a commercial revival transformed Europe: towns grew, trade routes expanded (especially through Italian city-states like Venice and Genoa), and a merchant class emerged. The [[Hanseatic League]] organized northern European trade, while Italian bankers developed early financial instruments like letters of credit. The [[Black Death]] catastrophically disrupted this growth, killing so many laborers that surviving peasants could demand wages, accelerating the decline of serfdom. The plague paradoxically stimulated commercial capitalism by increasing wages and reducing the relative power of landowners.

## 🎨 Cultural Developments

The [[Catholic Church]] was the dominant cultural force in medieval Europe — it built the great Gothic cathedrals, funded universities, and controlled literacy through monasteries. The rise of [[Scholasticism]], championed by Thomas Aquinas, represented the Church's attempt to incorporate Greek rational philosophy (especially Aristotle) into Christian theology. The [[Crusades]] exposed Europeans to Islamic science, mathematics, and philosophy, contributing to the intellectual revival of the 12th century. At the same time, the growing use of vernacular languages (Dante wrote in Italian, Chaucer in English) reflected a cultural shift as literacy spread beyond the clergy. By 1400, the Western Schism and the devastation of the [[Black Death]] had deeply eroded confidence in Church authority.

---

## 👥 Social Structures

Medieval European society was organized around the Three Estates: the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and commoners (Third Estate). This hierarchy was theoretically fixed by birth and divine order. However, the [[Black Death]] upended social structures by killing roughly one-third of the population, creating labor shortages that gave surviving peasants unprecedented bargaining power. Peasant revolts (like the English Peasants' Revolt of 1381) reflected new class tensions. The growth of towns and a merchant class also challenged the binary world of lord-and-peasant, creating a proto-middle class that would eventually undermine the feudal order entirely.

> [!warning]
> Don't oversimplify: the Black Death did NOT immediately end feudalism. It weakened it gradually over decades, and lords initially tried to reassert control through legislation (like England's Statute of Laborers, 1351).

## 🌿 Technology and Environment

The three-field system, horse collar, and iron plow improved agricultural productivity in Europe before 1300, supporting population growth to roughly 75 million. This demographic expansion made Europe uniquely vulnerable to the [[Black Death]], which arrived via [[Silk Roads|trade routes]] in 1347 and killed 25–30 million people within five years. The plague was not just a biological event but an environmental catastrophe that restructured European society. In terms of technology, the period saw the development of mechanical clocks and early navigational tools — laying the groundwork for European maritime expansion after 1450.

---

## 🚶 Human Migration and Cultural Interaction

The [[Crusades]] (1095–1291) sent hundreds of thousands of Europeans into the Middle East, creating sustained contact between Latin Christian, Eastern Orthodox, and Islamic civilizations. This interaction transferred knowledge (Arabic numerals, advances in medicine and mathematics), goods (spices, textiles), and ideas back to Europe. Jewish communities in Europe faced persecution and expulsion as scapegoating intensified during the Crusades and the Black Death. The fall of the [[Byzantine Empire]] in 1453 triggered a westward migration of Greek scholars into Italy, directly fueling the Italian Renaissance.

---

## 🌐 Cross-Cutting Causation Chain

**Population growth** (three-field system, stable climate) → **Commercial revival & town growth** → **Church wealth & corruption** → **Crusades & cultural contact** → **Black Death arrives via trade routes** → **Labor shortages & peasant empowerment** → **Weakened feudalism & Church authority** → **Rise of centralized monarchies & merchant class** → **European expansion (1450s onward)**
