# Key Terms — Chapter 6: Developments in Europe (c. 1200–1450)

#apwh #unit-1 #period-1

**Feudalism** — A political and social system in which lords granted land (fiefs) to vassals in exchange for military service and loyalty. (Significance: organized medieval European society in the absence of strong centralized states; weakened after the [[Black Death]])

**Manorialism** — The economic system of medieval Europe in which peasants (serfs) worked the lord's land in exchange for protection. (Significance: the agricultural backbone of feudalism; declined as towns and trade revived)

**[[Black Death]]** — The bubonic plague epidemic that swept Europe from 1347–1353, killing roughly one-third of the population. (Significance: destabilized feudalism, labor systems, and Church authority; accelerated social change)

**Serfdom** — The condition of peasants legally bound to the land of a lord, unable to leave without permission. (Significance: declined after the Black Death as labor became scarce and peasants gained bargaining power)

**Three-Field System** — An agricultural innovation dividing farmland into three rotating sections to improve yields. (Significance: increased food production and supported population growth before the Black Death)

**[[Catholic Church]]** — The dominant religious institution of medieval Europe, led by the Pope, controlling spiritual life, education, and significant political power. (Significance: the most powerful unifying force in a politically fragmented Europe)

**Pope** — The head of the Roman Catholic Church, whose authority rivaled and sometimes exceeded that of monarchs. (Significance: the Investiture Controversy showed the Church could challenge kings; the Great Schism weakened papal power)

**[[Crusades]]** — A series of religiously motivated military campaigns (1095–1291) launched by European Christians to capture the Holy Land from Muslim rulers. (Significance: increased trade and cultural exchange with the Islamic world; fueled antisemitism and violence in Europe)

**Investiture Controversy** — The conflict between Pope Gregory VII and Holy Roman Emperor Henry IV over who had the right to appoint Church officials. (Significance: demonstrated the Church's political power; temporarily humiliated secular rulers)

**[[Holy Roman Empire]]** — A loose confederation of Germanic states in Central Europe nominally ruled by an emperor; frequently in conflict with the papacy. (Significance: political fragmentation prevented centralized rule in Germany, unlike France or England)

**Magna Carta** — A 1215 charter signed by King John of England limiting royal power and establishing that even the king was subject to law. (Significance: foundational document for representative government and rule of law)

**Parliament** — The representative assembly in England that gradually gained power to advise and check the monarch. (Significance: early example of representative institutions that would shape modern democracy)

**Scholasticism** — A method of intellectual inquiry used in medieval universities that sought to reconcile Christian theology with classical Greek philosophy (especially Aristotle). (Significance: laid groundwork for rational, evidence-based thinking in Europe)

**Thomas Aquinas** — Dominican friar and scholastic theologian who argued that faith and reason were compatible in his *Summa Theologica*. (Significance: most influential thinker of medieval Europe; his work shaped Catholic doctrine for centuries)

**[[Universities]]** — Formal institutions of higher learning that emerged in Europe c. 1100–1200 (Bologna, Paris, Oxford). (Significance: preserved and transmitted classical knowledge; trained clergy and government officials)

**Great Schism (1054)** — The split between the Roman Catholic Church (West) and the Eastern Orthodox Church (East). (Significance: permanently divided Christianity and weakened European religious unity)

**Western Schism (1378–1417)** — A period when multiple men simultaneously claimed to be the legitimate Pope. (Significance: severely damaged the Church's credibility and authority)

**[[Byzantine Empire]]** — The eastern continuation of the Roman Empire, centered in Constantinople, lasting until 1453. (Significance: preserved Greek and Roman knowledge; influenced Eastern Europe and provided a Christian bulwark against Islamic expansion)

**Reconquista** — The centuries-long Christian military campaign to reclaim the Iberian Peninsula (Spain/Portugal) from Muslim rulers, completed in 1492. (Significance: religious motivation for expansion; established patterns of forced conversion and religious intolerance)

**Hanseatic League** — A commercial and defensive confederation of merchant guilds and towns in northern Europe (c. 1241–1600). (Significance: example of early economic cooperation and the growing power of merchants)

**Guild** — An association of craftsmen or merchants who regulated trade, set standards, and protected members' interests. (Significance: organized urban economic life; restricted free market but ensured quality and trained workers)

**Vernacular** — Everyday local languages (French, English, Italian, German) as opposed to Latin. (Significance: use of vernacular in literature (Dante, Chaucer) showed cultural shift away from Church monopoly on written expression)

**[[Hundred Years' War]]** (1337–1453) — A prolonged conflict between England and France over territory and succession. (Significance: fueled French and English national identity; Joan of Arc became a symbol of French nationalism)

**Joan of Arc** — A French peasant girl who led French forces to key victories in the Hundred Years' War, claiming divine guidance. (Significance: symbol of religious nationalism; her trial and execution by the English exposed tensions between popular religion and Church authority)

**Three Estates** — The social hierarchy of medieval Europe: First Estate (clergy), Second Estate (nobility), Third Estate (commoners). (Significance: rigid system challenged by the Black Death and growing merchant class; collapsed during the French Revolution)
