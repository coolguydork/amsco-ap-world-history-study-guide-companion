# Mass Atrocities

**Unit 7 — Global Conflict | AP Period 4 | Printed pp. 521–530**

**AP Themes:** GOV, CDI, SIO
**AP Skill:** Causation
**Essential Question:** What conditions enabled mass atrocities in the 20th century, and how did these events shape human rights movements?

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 7.8 is Causation-focused. The AP exam will ask you to explain the **causes** of mass atrocities (war, economic crisis, nationalism, ideology) and their **effects** (human rights law, ICC, UN reform). Know at least two case studies well enough to compare them.

## The Big Picture

The two World Wars killed tens of millions of soldiers — but civilian deaths through deliberate genocide, ethnic violence, and strategic bombing often exceeded military casualties. The 20th century produced a grim catalogue of state-organized mass killing, beginning with the Armenian [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] (1915) and continuing through the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] (1941–1945), the Khmer Rouge killings in Cambodia, and post-Cold War genocides in Bosnia (1992–1995), Rwanda (1994), and Darfur (2003–ongoing). Each atrocity shared common conditions: war or political crisis, nationalist or ideological extremism, dehumanization of a target group, and the failure of outside powers to intervene.

The phrase "Never again," adopted after the Holocaust, shaped international institutions — the Nuremberg Trials, the UN Genocide Convention (1948), and eventually the [[Concept Notes/International Criminal Court|International Criminal Court]] (2002). Yet genocides continued, exposing the gap between international norms and enforcement.

## World War I and Its Atrocities (1914–1918)

WWI produced staggering casualties: 8–9 million soldiers dead, more than 21 million wounded. Civilian deaths ranged from 6–13 million — one of the first modern wars where civilians were considered legitimate targets. The war's psychological toll was equally devastating; the [[Concept Notes/Lost Generation|Lost Generation]] emerged as a cultural expression of disillusionment.

**The Armenian Genocide (1915–1916)** was the 20th century's first genocide. The Ottoman "Young Turk" government accused the Christian Armenian minority of collaborating with Russia (an Ottoman enemy) and deported between 600,000 and 1.5 million Armenians to camps in Syria and Iraq. Most died from execution, starvation, disease, or exposure. The Turkish government has disputed the genocide label, calling the deaths a consequence of war and ethnic conflict. Adolf Hitler later cited the Armenian case as proof that the world would not intervene: *"Who, after all, speaks today of the annihilation of the Armenians?"*

**The 1918–1919 Influenza Pandemic** extended the war's death toll past Armistice Day (November 11, 1918). Millions of returning soldiers spread the flu globally, killing 20 million in Europe and the U.S. and perhaps 7 million in India alone. Total worldwide deaths are estimated at 20–50 million. The pandemic showed that modern transportation could transform a regional disease into a global catastrophe.

## WWII Atrocities: The Nazis (1939–1945)

WWII produced 30–50 million total deaths, with civilian casualties likely exceeding military ones. The Soviet Union lost perhaps half of all deaths.

**The Holocaust** was the Nazi regime's systematic genocide of approximately 6 million Jews — around two-thirds of European Jewry — along with 5 million others (Roma, disabled, political prisoners, LGBTQ individuals, Soviet POWs). Heinrich Himmler's SS implemented the "Final Solution" beginning in 1942: mobile killing units first shot Jews in mass graves; later, Jews were deported to death camps (Auschwitz, Treblinka, Dachau) and gassed. The Nuremberg Laws (1935) had earlier stripped Jews of citizenship and forced them into ghettos. The Holocaust's scale and industrial efficiency — trains, gas chambers, crematoria — made it the paradigmatic genocide of the modern era.

## WWII Atrocities: Japan and the Allies

**Japan** did not pursue a Holocaust-style genocide but caused millions of civilian deaths. The Rape of Nanking (1937) killed at least 100,000 Chinese soldiers and civilians. Under "Asia for Asiatics," Japan forced conquered peoples into brutal labor programs and compelled women into sexual slavery as "comfort women." Over a million civilians died in Vietnam alone under Japanese occupation.

**Allied firebombing** showed that democracies also targeted civilians. The Allies firebombed Hamburg (1943, ~50,000 dead) and Dresden (1945, ~25,000 dead). The U.S. firebombed Tokyo and then dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945), killing 100,000–200,000 and introducing the nuclear age. These attacks raised enduring moral questions about total war and civilian immunity.

## Post-WWII Genocides: "Never Again" — Repeatedly

Despite the Holocaust's lessons and the creation of international human rights frameworks, genocides continued.

**Bosnia (1992–1995):** Yugoslavia's violent collapse after the Cold War produced ethnic cleansing by Serb forces led by Slobodan Milošević. Serbs committed mass murder and rape against Bosnian Muslims (Bosniaks) and Albanians in Kosovo. Over 300,000 died in Yugoslavia's balkanization. Milošević was later indicted by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia.

**Rwanda (1994):** Colonial-era Belgian favoritism toward the minority Tutsis over the majority Hutus created lasting resentment. After independence (1962), Hutu-led governments discriminated against Tutsis. In 1994, after Rwanda's Hutu president was killed, Hutu extremists organized genocide: 500,000–1 million Tutsis and moderate Hutus were killed in roughly 100 days. The UN and individual nations (including the U.S.) refused to intervene or evacuate Rwandans.

**Darfur, Sudan (2003–ongoing):** Non-Arab Muslim rebels in Darfur rose against the Arab-controlled Sudanese government. President Omar al-Bashir unleashed the Janjaweed militias, who destroyed hundreds of villages and killed 200,000+, displacing over a million people. The ICC charged al-Bashir with war crimes — the first sitting head of state so indicted — but enforcement remained limited.

## The International Response and Human Rights

The Holocaust's aftermath created a new global human rights architecture. The Nuremberg Trials (1945–1946) established that leaders could be held criminally accountable for crimes against humanity — a revolutionary legal principle. The UN Genocide Convention (1948) obligated states to prevent and punish genocide. The Universal Declaration of [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|Human Rights]] (1948) articulated universal rights. Eventually, the [[Concept Notes/International Criminal Court|International Criminal Court]] (est. 2002) gave these norms an enforcement mechanism.

Yet Bosnia and Rwanda exposed the gap between principle and action. The "responsibility to protect" (R2P) doctrine, adopted by the UN in 2005, attempted to codify when the international community could intervene — but Darfur showed it remained more aspiration than guarantee.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate **genocide** (deliberate destruction of a group) with **ethnic cleansing** (forced removal of a group) — they overlap but are legally distinct. On the AP, you may need to explain the difference. Both appear in this topic.

> [!tip]
> Hitler's quote about the Armenians is a famous AP primary source. It shows **continuity** across 20th-century genocides: perpetrators learned from earlier atrocities that the world would not stop them.
