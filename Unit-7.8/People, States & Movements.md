# People, States & Movements

**Unit 7 — Global Conflict | AP Period 4 | Printed pp. 521–530**

## People

**Adolf Hitler**
German Führer (1933–1945) who ordered the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] and pursued Lebensraum through aggressive war. His quote — *"Who, after all, speaks today of the annihilation of the Armenians?"* — explicitly cited the Armenian [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] as precedent for the Holocaust, demonstrating that earlier atrocities emboldened later perpetrators. His rise to power through democratic elections, followed by the dismantling of democratic institutions, is the AP's primary example of how totalitarianism enables genocide.

**Heinrich Himmler**
Head of the Nazi SS (Schutzstaffel) and chief architect of the Holocaust. Oversaw the mobile killing units (Einsatzgruppen) that shot Jews and buried them in mass graves, then coordinated the shift to death camps as the Final Solution was industrialized. Also directed forced labor of conquered peoples and the removal of Slavic populations. Represents the bureaucratic, systematic nature of state-organized genocide — mass murder as an administrative project.

**Raphael Lemkin**
Polish-Jewish lawyer who coined the term "genocide" in 1944 and lobbied successfully for the UN Genocide Convention (1948). Lemkin's personal history — most of his family died in the Holocaust — drove his legal activism. The Genocide Convention obligated signatory states to prevent and punish genocide, creating the international legal framework the AP associates with post-WWII human rights norms.

**Elie Wiesel**
Holocaust survivor, author of *Night* (a memoir of his experience in Auschwitz and Buchenwald), and Nobel Peace Prize laureate. Became one of the 20th century's most prominent advocates for remembrance and human rights. His famous phrase "Never shall I forget" encapsulates the survivor generation's insistence on historical memory as a moral obligation.

**Gerda Weissmann Klein**
Polish-American Holocaust survivor whose memoir *All But My Life* (1957) is excerpted in the AMSCO Think As a Historian section. Her question — "Why did we walk like meek sheep to the slaughterhouse?" — reflects the historical debate about victim response and perpetrator conditions. Her answer (faith in humanity) speaks to the difficulty of comprehending genocide before it fully unfolds.

**Slobodan Milošević**
Serbian nationalist president who directed ethnic cleansing and genocide in Bosnia and Kosovo during Yugoslavia's violent breakup in the 1990s. Indicted by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia for war crimes. Died in custody in 2006 before a verdict. The Milošević case established that post-WWII international justice mechanisms could pursue sitting heads of state — but also showed their limits.

**Omar al-Bashir**
President of Sudan (1989–2019) who directed the Darfur genocide beginning in 2003. First sitting head of state charged by the [[Concept Notes/International Criminal Court\|International Criminal Court]] (2009) for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide. Remained in power despite ICC charges for years, exposing the limits of international enforcement when geopolitical interests (China, Arab League) protected the perpetrator.

**Pol Pot**
Khmer Rouge leader who directed the Cambodian genocide (1975–1979), killing 1.6–1.8 million Cambodians in an attempt to "purify" society along racial, social, and political lines. Referenced in AMSCO Topic 7.8 as evidence that genocide continued after WWII; covered in detail in Topic 8.6. Represents post-WWII communist totalitarian genocide alongside Stalin's famines.

## States

**Ottoman Empire (Young Turks)**
Declining empire ruled by a nationalist military clique ("Young Turks") during WWI. Accused the Christian Armenian minority of collaborating with Russia and organized their deportation and mass killing (1915–1916). The Armenian Genocide — the 20th century's first — established the pattern: wartime emergency used to justify targeting an "internal enemy." The Ottoman Empire's dissolution at WWI's end reshaped the Middle East and created many of the region's borders and conflicts.

**Nazi Germany**
The totalitarian state that implemented the Holocaust. Key stages of escalation: legal exclusion via Nuremberg Laws (1935) → forced segregation in ghettos → deportation to death camps and the Final Solution (1942–1945). Nazi Germany shows how a modern bureaucratic state with advanced technology (railways, industrial killing facilities) could systematize genocide. The Holocaust's scale — 6 million Jews, 11 million total — remains the paradigmatic case in international law and human rights education.

**Yugoslavia (and its successor states)**
Multi-ethnic communist federation held together by Marshal Tito (d. 1980). After the Soviet Union collapsed, Yugoslavia disintegrated violently along ethnic and religious lines. Slobodan Milošević's Serbia pursued ethnic purity through genocide and ethnic cleansing in Bosnia-Herzegovina and Kosovo. The term "balkanization" derives from this breakup. Yugoslavia's collapse is a key AP example of how Cold War-era authoritarianism suppressed ethnic conflicts that erupted catastrophically once the lid was removed.

**Rwanda**
Small East African country whose colonial history — Belgian favoritism toward the Tutsi minority over the Hutu majority — created the ethnic hierarchy that exploded into genocide in 1994. After independence (1962), Hutu-led governments used discrimination and periodic violence against Tutsis. The 1994 genocide (500,000–1 million dead in 100 days) was organized by the Hutu-led state using radio propaganda and government-distributed weapons. The international community's failure to intervene became a defining lesson in the limits of post-Holocaust human rights norms.

**Sudan**
Arab-led East African state whose government used the Janjaweed militias to commit genocide in the western Darfur region beginning in 2003 against non-Arab Muslim rebels and civilians. President Omar al-Bashir was ICC-indicted but remained in power for years — illustrating how geopolitical interests (oil, regional alliances) could shield perpetrators from international justice.

## Movements & Institutions

**SS (Schutzstaffel)**
Nazi Germany's special police force headed by Heinrich Himmler; the primary instrument of the Holocaust. Ran the death camp system, conducted mass shootings via mobile Einsatzgruppen units, managed forced labor, and oversaw deportation logistics. The SS embodied what historian Hannah Arendt called the "banality of evil" — genocide executed through bureaucratic routine by ordinary people following orders.

**Young Turks (Committee of Union and Progress)**
Ottoman nationalist faction that controlled the empire during WWI; directed the Armenian Genocide. Motivated by pan-Turkish nationalism and fears of the empire's collapse. Their use of wartime emergency to target a minority group became a template that Hitler explicitly acknowledged. The Young Turks' leaders escaped prosecution after WWI — a fact Lemkin cited when arguing for a Genocide Convention.

**International Criminal Court (ICC)**
Permanent international tribunal established by the Rome Statute (2002); prosecutes genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. Built on the Nuremberg precedent that individuals can be held accountable for atrocities. The ICC's indictment of Omar al-Bashir was historically significant but practically limited — its enforcement depends on state cooperation, which powerful actors (U.S., China, Russia) have often withheld.

**[[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]] Peacekeeping**
The UN's capacity to deploy peacekeeping forces was directly tested and found wanting in Rwanda (1994), where peacekeepers were ordered not to use force and were too few in number. The failure catalyzed reform debates about the UN's role in internal conflicts and eventually the "responsibility to protect" (R2P) doctrine (2005), which asserted the international community's obligation to intervene when a state commits atrocities against its own people.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves asking about **international responses to genocide** — and the answer is almost always "insufficient." Know: (1) UN peacekeepers in Rwanda were ordered not to use force; (2) individual nations evacuated their own personnel but not Rwandans; (3) the ICC charged Bashir but couldn't enforce the warrant. These are three ways the "Never again" commitment failed in practice.
