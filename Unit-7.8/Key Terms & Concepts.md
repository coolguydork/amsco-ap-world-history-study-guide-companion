# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 7 — Global Conflict | AP Period 4 | Printed pp. 521–530**

## Government: War & Strategy

**Armistice Day**
November 11, 1918 — the day Germany surrendered and WWI ended; Allied advances had become decisive by summer 1918 once U.S. forces were deployed in Europe. Now observed as Veterans Day (U.S.) and Remembrance Day (Commonwealth nations). On the AP, it marks the endpoint of WWI and the beginning of the unstable interwar period.

**firebombing**
Air raid tactic using incendiary bombs to create massive urban fires and kill civilians. The Allies firebombed Hamburg (~50,000 dead, 1943) and Dresden (~25,000 dead, 1945) in Germany and Tokyo (1945) in Japan. WWII firebombing exemplifies total war — the deliberate targeting of civilian populations and infrastructure as a war strategy. Preceded and accompanied the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

**Hamburg**
Major German port city firebombed by the Allies in 1943 (Operation Gomorrah); approximately 50,000 civilians killed. Demonstrated Allied capacity to destroy entire cities with conventional incendiary weapons; one of the deadliest conventional air raids of WWII.

**Dresden**
Historic German city firebombed by Allied forces in February 1945; approximately 25,000 deaths and 15 square miles of the historic city center destroyed. Controversial because the war was nearly over; exemplifies the Allied use of strategic bombing against civilian populations.

> [!warning]
> Students often think only the Axis powers committed atrocities. Allied firebombing of Hamburg and Dresden — and U.S. atomic bombings — show that total war blurred the line between military and civilian targets for all belligerents.

**Tokyo**
Japanese capital firebombed by the United States in March 1945; one of the most destructive conventional air raids in history. Followed by atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Together these attacks killed hundreds of thousands of civilians and compelled Japan's surrender.

## Government: Genocidal Policies

**genocide**
Deliberate and systematic destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Coined by Polish-Jewish lawyer Raphael Lemkin; codified in the UN [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] Convention (1948). AP case studies: Armenian Genocide (1915), [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] (1941–1945), Cambodian Genocide (1975–1979, Topic 8.6), Rwanda (1994), Darfur (2003). Key AP skill: compare the causes and conditions that enabled each.

**Final Solution**
Nazi Germany's plan for the systematic murder of all Jews in Europe, formally coordinated at the Wannsee Conference (January 1942); implemented by Heinrich Himmler's SS through mobile killing units and death camps (Auschwitz, Treblinka, Dachau). Killed approximately 6 million Jews — the Holocaust. The ultimate expression of Nazi racial ideology.

**Holocaust**
Nazi Germany's systematic genocide of approximately 6 million Jews and 5 million others (Roma, disabled, political prisoners, gay people, Soviet POWs) during WWII (1941–1945). Implemented via ghettos, deportation, and death camps using industrial technology (trains, gas chambers, crematoria). Led directly to the Nuremberg Trials, the UN Genocide Convention, and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

**Asia for Asiatics**
Japanese imperial slogan used to frame conquest of East and Southeast Asia as liberation from Western colonialism. In practice, a cover for brutal exploitation: conquered peoples were forced into military labor, public works, and farms; women in Korea, China, and elsewhere were forced into sexual slavery as "comfort women." Over a million civilians died in Vietnam alone under Japanese occupation.

**ethnic cleansing**
The forced removal, expulsion, or killing of an ethnic group from a territory to achieve ethnic homogeneity; practiced by Serb forces in Bosnia and Kosovo in the 1990s against Muslims. Overlaps with but is legally distinct from genocide — ethnic cleansing aims at removal; genocide aims at destruction. Both are crimes against humanity under international law.

**balkanization**
The fragmentation of a region or state into smaller, often hostile ethnically defined units; term coined from Yugoslavia's violent dissolution in the 1990s. Yugoslavia's balkanization produced the Bosnian genocide and ethnic cleansing in Kosovo, with 300,000+ deaths. On the AP, it illustrates how the end of the Cold War unleashed suppressed nationalist and ethnic conflicts.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP may ask you to compare genocide and ethnic cleansing. Genocide = deliberate destruction of a group. Ethnic cleansing = forced removal of a group from a territory. Ethnic cleansing can escalate into genocide (as in Bosnia) but is legally distinct.

## Government: Organizations & Places

**[[Concept Notes/International Criminal Court\|International Criminal Court]] (ICC)**
Permanent international tribunal established by the Rome Statute (2002) to prosecute war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide when national courts cannot or will not act. Charged Sudan's President Omar al-Bashir with war crimes — the first sitting head of state so indicted. Represents the post-Holocaust global consensus that individuals, including leaders, can be held criminally accountable for atrocities.

**Bosnia**
Former Yugoslav republic where Serb forces committed genocide and ethnic cleansing against Bosnian Muslims (Bosniaks) and Croats in 1992–1995; directed by Slobodan Milošević. Included the Srebrenica massacre (1995), where 8,000 Bosniak men and boys were killed in a UN-designated "safe zone." Over 100,000 killed total; part of Yugoslavia's violent balkanization after the Cold War ended.

**Rwanda**
Small East African country where the Hutu majority government directed genocide against the Tutsi minority in 1994; 500,000–1 million killed in approximately 100 days. Root cause: Belgian colonial favoritism toward Tutsis over Hutus created lasting resentment. International community (UN, U.S.) refused to intervene or even evacuate Rwandans. Became the defining case of international failure on genocide prevention.

**Darfur**
Region in western Sudan where the Sudanese government and Arab Janjaweed militias carried out genocide beginning in 2003 against non-Arab Muslim Africans; 200,000+ killed, 1+ million displaced into Chad. ICC charged President Omar al-Bashir — but genocide continued. Demonstrates the ongoing gap between international human rights norms and enforcement capacity.

## Government: Leaders

**Heinrich Himmler**
Head of the Nazi SS and chief architect of the Holocaust; oversaw deportation of Jews to death camps, forced labor, mass shootings, and the concentration camp system. Second most powerful Nazi after Hitler; embodied the bureaucratic, systematic nature of genocide under totalitarian regimes. Committed suicide after capture in 1945.

**Slobodan Milošević**
Serbian nationalist president (1989–1997) who directed ethnic cleansing and genocide in Bosnia and Kosovo during Yugoslavia's breakup. Indicted by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia for war crimes and crimes against humanity. Died in custody (2006) before a verdict — an important AP example of international justice's limits.

**Omar al-Bashir**
Sudanese president (1989–2019) who directed the Darfur genocide. First sitting head of state indicted by the ICC (2009) for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide. His continued rule despite ICC charges highlighted limits of international enforcement — sovereignty and geopolitical interests often blocked action.

## Culture: Groups & Movements

**Armenians**
Christian minority within the Ottoman Empire; victims of the first genocide of the 20th century (1915–1916). The Ottoman "Young Turk" government accused Armenians of collaborating with Russia and deported them to camps in Syria/Iraq; 600,000–1.5 million died. Turkey disputes the genocide label. Hitler cited the Armenian case as precedent, arguing the world would not stop a determined perpetrator.

**Tutsis**
Minority ethnic group in Rwanda favored by Belgian colonizers; targeted for genocide by the Hutu majority government in 1994. ~500,000–1 million Tutsis killed in 100 days. The RPF (Tutsi rebel army in exile) ultimately ended the genocide and took power, but the country bore deep scars. The Rwanda case shows how colonial legacies created the ethnic hierarchies that later enabled genocide.

**Hutus**
Majority ethnic group in Rwanda; resented Belgian-era favoritism toward Tutsis. After independence (1962), Hutu-led governments discriminated against Tutsis for decades. In 1994, Hutu extremists organized mass slaughter using government radio to coordinate killing. The genocide was not spontaneous but deliberately organized by the state — a classic AP example of top-down genocide.

**[[Concept Notes/Lost Generation\|Lost Generation]]**
Term originally describing American expatriate writers (Hemingway, Fitzgerald) living in Paris after WWI; broadened to describe the generation traumatized by WWI's mass death. Reflected widespread disillusionment with nationalism, progress, and traditional values after the war's senseless destruction. A cultural consequence of total war and a CDI theme (how war reshapes culture and identity).

## Technology & Environment

**influenza epidemic (1918–1919)**
The "Spanish flu" pandemic spread globally when WWI soldiers returned home, killing 20 million in Europe/U.S. and ~7 million in India; total worldwide deaths estimated at 20–50 million — more than WWI combat itself. Demonstrated how modern transportation could transform a regional disease into a global catastrophe. An early example of the unintended consequences of globalization.

**pandemic**
A disease epidemic affecting a very wide geographic area or the entire world. The 1918–1919 influenza pandemic killed more people than WWI. The term appears in this topic to emphasize that the war's death toll extended well past the Armistice through disease. Modern examples: AIDS (1980s–present), COVID-19 (2020).

## Society: Discrimination

**Nuremberg Laws**
1935 German racial laws that stripped Jews of citizenship, forbade marriage between Jews and non-Jews, and excluded Jews from professional and public life. The legal foundation of Nazi persecution; transformed Jews from citizens to stateless subjects. A key AP example of how law can institutionalize racism and pave the way for genocide.

**ghetto**
In Nazi Germany, walled or segregated sections of cities where Jews were forced to live under deliberately lethal overcrowded conditions — starvation, disease — before deportation to death camps. The Warsaw Ghetto (Poland) was the largest. Ghettos represent a stage in escalating Nazi persecution: legal exclusion (Nuremberg Laws) → segregation (ghettos) → deportation and mass murder (Final Solution).

**Janjaweed**
Arab militias ("evil men on horseback") unleashed by the Sudanese government against non-Arab Muslim Africans in Darfur beginning in 2003. Attacked and destroyed hundreds of villages, killing 200,000+ and displacing 1+ million. Worked alongside Sudanese military forces. A post-Cold War example of state-sponsored genocide using proxy militias to maintain deniability.

> [!tip]
> The AP loves comparing genocides across time. Key comparison axis: **How did the perpetrators dehumanize the target group?** Nazis used pseudo-scientific racism; Ottoman Turks used wartime treason accusations; Hutu extremists used radio propaganda; Sudan used ethnic/religious difference. Different methods, same logic.
