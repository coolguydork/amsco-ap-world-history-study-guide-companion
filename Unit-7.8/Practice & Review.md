## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: Holocaust Encyclopedia, United States Holocaust Memorial Museum*

> "The Holocaust was the systematic, bureaucratic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million Jews by the Nazi regime and its collaborators. . . . The Nazis, who came to power in Germany in January 1933, believed that Germans were 'racially superior' and that the Jews, deemed 'inferior,' were an alien threat to the so-called German racial community. . . .
>
> To concentrate and monitor the Jewish population as well as to facilitate later deportation of the Jews, the Germans and their collaborators created ghettos, transit camps, and forced-labor camps for Jews during the war years. . . .
>
> Between 1941 and 1945, Nazi German authorities deported millions of Jews from Germany, from occupied territories, and from the countries of many of its Axis allies to ghettos and to killing centers, often called extermination camps, where they were murdered in specially developed gassing facilities."

**1. Which best helps explain the passage in its broader historical context?**

A. Several European countries reached an agreement in Munich, in 1938, that allowed Germany to seize the Sudetenland.
B. World War II included the first use of atomic weapons in human history.
C. Political conflicts before and during World War II led to acts of genocide and other large-scale ethnic violence.
D. During Kristallnacht in 1938, Jews throughout Germany and Austria were beaten and killed.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The passage describes systematic, state-sponsored genocide — a product of the political extremism and ethnic nationalist violence that defined the WWII era. C is the only option that places the Holocaust in its broader historical context (political conflict → genocide). A (Munich Agreement) is context for appeasement, not genocide. B (atomic weapons) is unrelated to the Holocaust. D (Kristallnacht) is a specific event during the Holocaust escalation, not broader context.

**2. The policy of the Japanese that most closely paralleled the conditions described in the passage was:**

A. forcing conquered peoples into deadly forced labor programs
B. engaging in surprise military attacks, such as the one on Pearl Harbor
C. attempting to liberate the people of Asia from Western imperialism
D. invading Manchuria after an attack on a Japanese railway station there

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage describes how Nazis used ghettos, transit camps, and forced labor to control and ultimately kill Jews. Japan's "Asia for Asiatics" program forced conquered peoples into brutal labor, causing over a million civilian deaths in Vietnam alone. Japanese forced labor parallels the Nazi use of concentration and labor camps. B (Pearl Harbor) is a military attack strategy, not a policy toward civilians. C (liberation) was Japan's rhetoric, not its actual policy. D (Manchuria) is an invasion, not a civilian exploitation policy.

**3. The campaign of genocide represented in the passage is most similar to which of the following examples?**

A. the killing of 26 Republicans between August and September 1936 by fascists belonging to Franco's regime
B. the massacre of a band of Lakota people on their way to the Pine Ridge Reservation for shelter from the winter by the U.S. 7th Cavalry in December 1890
C. the deportation and execution of 1.5 million Armenians by the Ottoman Empire
D. the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The passage describes systematic, state-sponsored, bureaucratic genocide targeting an ethnic/religious group. The Armenian Genocide (1915–1916) matches all these features: the Ottoman state organized deportations and mass killings of Armenians based on ethnicity and religion. A (Spain, 1936) was targeted political killing, not ethnic genocide. B (Wounded Knee) was a massacre, but not a systematic state policy of ethnic extermination. D (Franz Ferdinand) was a political assassination, not genocide.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Rape of Nanking (Source: Iris Chang, *The Rape of Nanking*, 1997)

> "Japan used a highly developed military machine and a master-race mentality to set about establishing its right to rule its neighbors. . . . If one event can be held up as an example of the unmitigated evil lying just below the surface of unbridled military adventurism, that moment is the Rape of Nanking. . . . When the city fell on December 13, 1937, Japanese soldiers began an orgy of cruelty seldom if ever matched in world history. . . . Years later experts at the International Military Tribunal of the Far East (IMTFE) estimated that more than 260,000 noncombatants died at the hands of Japanese soldiers in Nanking in late 1937 and early 1938, though some experts have placed the figure at well over 350,000."

**(A) Identify ONE historical development of the mid-20th century that might explain why Japan acted as described in the passage.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Japanese imperial expansion and a "master-race mentality" (as Chang notes) drove Japan's aggression. Japanese militarism — fueled by nationalist ideology and the need for raw materials and markets after the Great Depression — led Japan to invade China in the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937). Japanese leaders promoted the belief that Japanese civilization was superior to other Asian peoples, dehumanizing Chinese civilians and making atrocities like the Rape of Nanking possible. This ideology parallels Nazi racial supremacy and Ottoman nationalist contempt for Armenians.

**(B) Explain ONE way in which the actions of Japan described in the passage are similar to the actions of Nazi Germany.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Both Japan and Nazi Germany used a "master-race mentality" (Chang's term) to justify the mass killing of civilian populations they viewed as racially or culturally inferior. Just as Nazi ideology called Jews a biological threat requiring extermination, Japanese imperial ideology portrayed Chinese civilians as subhuman targets during the Rape of Nanking. In both cases, dehumanization by the state preceded and enabled mass atrocity — demonstrating that racial ideology is a key enabling condition for genocide and mass violence.

**(C) Explain what the title of Chang's book indicates about her point of view about events she describes.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Sourcing / Point of View
> Chang's subtitle — "The Forgotten Holocaust of World War II" — reveals her point of view that the Rape of Nanking has been unjustly neglected in global historical memory compared to the Holocaust. By calling it "forgotten," she implies that the international community failed to adequately reckon with Japanese wartime atrocities, just as it initially failed to recognize the Armenian Genocide. Her framing positions the Rape of Nanking as morally equivalent to the Holocaust, challenging readers and historians to apply consistent standards of remembrance and accountability across different national and ethnic groups.

### SAQ 2 — Causes and Effects of Mass Atrocities

**(A) Identify ONE historical development that contributed to the context for the rise of extremist groups prior to World War II.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The Great Depression (beginning 1929) created economic crisis across Europe and beyond, fueling resentment and making radical political movements attractive to desperate populations. In Germany, mass unemployment and the humiliation of the Versailles settlement created fertile ground for Nazi propaganda that blamed Jews for Germany's economic collapse. Economic scapegoating lowered psychological resistance to violence against minority groups, directly contributing to the conditions that enabled the Holocaust.

**(B) Explain ONE way in which the mass destruction of specific populations impacted societies after World War II.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation (Effects)
> The Holocaust directly led to the creation of new international legal and institutional frameworks to prevent future genocides. The Nuremberg Trials (1945–1946) established the precedent that leaders could be held criminally accountable for crimes against humanity — a revolution in international law that sovereignty did not protect mass murderers. This precedent eventually led to the UN Genocide Convention (1948), the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), and the International Criminal Court (2002), fundamentally reshaping how the international community understood state accountability and human rights.

**(C) Explain the difference between genocide and ethnic violence.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization
> Genocide is the deliberate and systematic destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, carried out with the intent to eliminate that group. Ethnic violence refers to violence between ethnic groups but without the deliberate, organized intent to destroy the group entirely. Ethnic violence can be spontaneous or mob-driven; genocide requires state organization, legal frameworks, and a sustained campaign of extermination. For example, the Holocaust was genocide — the Nazi state deliberately organized the murder of all European Jews. Ethnic violence might refer to intercommunal riots that lack centralized state direction. The legal distinction matters because the UN Genocide Convention creates specific obligations to prevent and punish genocide, obligations that do not apply to all ethnic violence.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

*In one to three paragraphs, explain the various causes and consequences of mass atrocities in the period from 1900 to the present.*

> [!tip] Model Response — Extended Causation
> Mass atrocities in the 20th and early 21st centuries shared common enabling conditions: state authority, ideological dehumanization, war or crisis, and international inaction. The Armenian Genocide (1915) occurred under WWI emergency conditions when the Ottoman government used wartime necessity to justify deporting and killing 1.5 million Christian Armenians. The Holocaust (1941–1945) combined Nazi racial ideology, state bureaucratic organization, and the cover of WWII to murder 6 million Jews and 5 million others. In both cases, the state was the perpetrator, dehumanization preceded mass killing, and outside powers failed to intervene.
>
> Post-WWII genocides in Cambodia, Bosnia, Rwanda, and Darfur demonstrated that the "Never again" commitment was aspirational rather than guaranteed. The Rwandan Genocide (1994) killed 500,000–1 million people in 100 days while UN peacekeepers watched under orders not to use force. The international community's repeated failure to enforce its own norms exposed the limits of sovereignty-based international law: states powerful enough to avoid ICC prosecution, or protected by great-power allies, faced few real consequences.
>
> The consequences of mass atrocities transformed international law and institutions. The Nuremberg Trials established individual criminal accountability for crimes against humanity. The UN Genocide Convention and Universal Declaration of Human Rights (both 1948) codified global norms. The ICC (2002) created a permanent enforcement mechanism, however imperfect. These developments represent a genuine shift in international norms — the idea that sovereignty does not protect leaders who commit atrocities. Yet the ongoing gap between norms and enforcement remains the central challenge of the post-WWII human rights order.
