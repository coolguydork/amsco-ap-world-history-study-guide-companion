# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Unit 7 — Global Conflict | AP Period 4 | Printed pp. 521–530**

**AP Skill:** Causation
**AP Themes:** GOV (Governance), CDI (Cultural Developments & Interactions), SIO (Social Interactions & Organization)

## Causation: What Conditions Enable Genocide?

Topic 7.8's AP skill is **Causation** — the exam will ask you to explain the causes of mass atrocities and their consequences. The AMSCO Think As a Historian section identifies three historical conditions that historians link to genocide:

**War** creates a psychological environment where killing members of an "outgroup" perceived as a threat becomes normalized and even state-sanctioned. Both the Armenian [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] and the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] occurred during major wars; wartime emergency gave perpetrators cover ("military necessity") and suppressed outside scrutiny.

**Economic crisis** fosters scapegoating — blaming a minority group for broader economic problems. Nazi propaganda blamed Jews for Germany's post-WWI humiliation and economic collapse; the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression|Great Depression]] deepened resentment and made radicalism attractive to desperate populations.

**Enlightenment thinking (perverted)** — the idea that society can be "perfected" by removing "biological threats." Jews were called a "virus"; Armenians were called disease-carrying "microbes." This pseudo-scientific racism gave genocide an ideological veneer of rationality and inevitability, making perpetrators feel they were performing a necessary, even noble act.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP may give you a document about genocide and ask you to identify a cause. Look for: (1) war/crisis language justifying emergency measures; (2) dehumanization of the target group; (3) state authority organizing the violence; (4) failure of outside powers to intervene. All four conditions appear in every AP case study.

## GOV — Governance: The State as Perpetrator

The 20th century's mass atrocities share a crucial feature: **the state is the perpetrator, not just a bystander**. Genocide is not mob violence — it requires state organization, legal frameworks, logistics, and ideology. This distinguishes it from random ethnic violence and makes it a GOV theme.

The Nuremberg Laws (1935) show how legal exclusion precedes physical violence. By stripping Jews of citizenship, the Nazi state made them legally vulnerable to any further measure. The progression — legal exclusion → segregation in ghettos → deportation → mass murder — follows a consistent escalation pattern visible across multiple case studies.

Post-WWII, the international community created new governance institutions to prevent recurrence: the UN Genocide Convention (1948), the Universal Declaration of [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|Human Rights]] (1948), the Nuremberg Tribunal precedent for individual criminal accountability, and eventually the ICC (2002). These represent a fundamental shift in international law: the idea that sovereignty does not protect leaders who commit crimes against humanity.

## CDI — Cultural Developments & Interactions: Dehumanization and Memory

Genocide requires a prior cultural process: **dehumanization**. Before mass killing can occur, perpetrators must convince themselves (and often bystanders) that the target group is subhuman, dangerous, or alien. This is a CDI theme because it involves how cultures construct "the other."

Nazis used pseudo-scientific racial theory (calling Jews a biological "virus"). Ottoman nationalists framed Armenians as treasonous collaborators with the enemy. Hutu Power extremists used radio broadcasts to call Tutsis "cockroaches" (*inyenzi*) — a dehumanizing label that lowered the psychological barrier to killing. The Janjaweed in Darfur framed non-Arab Africans as lesser Muslims undeserving of protection.

Cultural **memory** is also a CDI theme. The Holocaust produced a massive literature of survivor testimony (Elie Wiesel, Primo Levi, Gerda Weissmann Klein) that shaped post-war global culture and kept the atrocity in public consciousness. Rwanda, by contrast, initially received little attention — one AP implication being that whose suffering gets remembered is itself a cultural and political question.

## SIO — Social Interactions & Organization: Colonial Legacies and Ethnic Hierarchy

Several 20th-century genocides trace their roots to colonial-era social engineering. Belgian colonizers in Rwanda artificially hardened the distinction between Hutus and Tutsis — issuing identity cards, creating different legal statuses, and educating Tutsis preferentially. When colonialism ended, this imposed hierarchy became the fault line that exploded into genocide.

This is a SIO theme: how social hierarchies created (or intensified) by outside powers become sources of internal conflict long after the colonizers leave. The same dynamic appears in the former Yugoslavia, where ethnic and religious differences were managed — but not resolved — under communism, then erupted violently once the authoritarian lid was removed.

## Historiography: Explaining Perpetrator Behavior

The Think As a Historian excerpt from Gerda Weissmann Klein's memoir poses a key historiographical question: *Why did victims not resist more?* Her answer — faith in humanity — reflects a deeper historical problem: genocides are nearly impossible to recognize from the inside until they are already underway. Perpetrators exploit this disbelief, presenting each escalation as a limited emergency measure.

Historians have debated whether genocide requires special kinds of perpetrators (Hannah Arendt's "banality of evil" argues ordinary people can commit atrocities under the right conditions) or whether ordinary people are transformed by war, ideology, and social pressure. The AP expects you to understand these debates as evidence of **how historians construct interpretations** from different frameworks.

> [!tip]
> AMSCO's Think As a Historian asks you to draw a relationship between genocide and imperialism. The link: imperialism created racial hierarchies (Hutus/Tutsis in Rwanda), destabilized traditional societies, and spread Social Darwinist ideas that portrayed some peoples as superior and others as inferior — all preconditions for 20th-century genocide.

## Causation Synthesis: Effects of Mass Atrocities

| Effect | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| Nuremberg Trials (1945–46) | Established that war crimes and crimes against humanity can be prosecuted; created precedent for individual criminal accountability |
| UN Genocide Convention (1948) | First international treaty obligating states to prevent and punish genocide |
| Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) | Codified universal rights for all people regardless of state citizenship; humanitarian counterweight to sovereignty |
| State of Israel (1948) | Holocaust's refugee crisis and moral case for a Jewish homeland accelerated Israeli independence |
| International Criminal Court (2002) | Permanent body to prosecute atrocities; built on Nuremberg precedent |
| R2P Doctrine (2005) | UN asserted international community's responsibility to intervene when states commit atrocities against their own people |
| Ongoing failures | Bosnia, Rwanda, Darfur showed gap between norms and enforcement remains wide |
