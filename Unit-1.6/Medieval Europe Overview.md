# Topic 1.6 — Developments in Europe (c. 1200–1450)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves to test the **causes and consequences of political decentralization** in Europe and the **role of the Roman Catholic Church** as a unifying force. Know how feudalism, the Crusades, and the Black Death connect to each other and to long-term change.

## Context: From Roman Collapse to the High Middle Ages

When the Western Roman Empire fell in the 5th–6th centuries, Europe fragmented into small, competing kingdoms. Trade collapsed, literacy declined, and local strongmen replaced centralized authority. The one institution that survived and kept some unity was the **Roman Catholic Church**.

By 1000 CE, however, things began to shift. The era from roughly 1000 to 1450 is called the **High Middle Ages** — a period of agricultural revival, population growth, renewed trade, and intellectual creativity. Topic 1.6 traces how three forces — religion, agriculture, and political decentralization — shaped European society across these centuries. (AMSCO pp. 53–61)

## Feudalism and the Manorial System

European political life was built on **feudalism** — a decentralized system of mutual obligations exchanging land for loyalty and military service. The hierarchy ran: monarch → lords → knights → peasants. Lords granted **fiefs** (tracts of land) to vassals who pledged service in return. Because wealth was measured in land rather than cash, the entire system rested on agriculture.

The economic counterpart to feudalism was the **manorial system**. Large estates (manors) were self-sufficient units producing nearly everything residents needed. **Serfs** — peasants tied to the land — could not travel or marry without the lord's permission. They paid tribute in crops or labor in exchange for protection. Many serfs lived and died on a single manor, largely unaware of the wider world.

> [!tip]
> Compare with Japan (Topic 1.1): both had a warrior class (knights/samurai), codes of honor (chivalry/bushido), and a lord-vassal hierarchy. Key difference — Japan's emperor was a figurehead while Europe's monarchs were genuinely competing for power with the Church.

Agricultural improvements gradually eroded the manor's isolation. The **three-field system** rotated crops across three fields — one in wheat/rye, one in nitrogen-fixing legumes, one left fallow — dramatically raising yields. New plow designs (heavier wheeled plows for northern soils, lighter ones for the south) and windmills further boosted productivity. More food → population growth → more towns.

## The Roman Catholic Church as a Unifying Force

In a Europe divided into hundreds of small states, the Church was the only truly pan-European institution. Its power was cultural, political, and economic all at once.

**Culturally**, the Church dominated education, literacy, and art. It founded Europe's first universities. Clergy were often the only literate people in a community. All major art focused on religious themes, helping illiterate serfs visualize scripture. Even as **vernacular languages** (French, English, Italian) replaced Latin in daily life, Latin remained the Church's common tongue, connecting scholars across borders.

**Politically**, the Church leveraged enormous influence over secular rulers. A lord who displeased a bishop might find religious services canceled for his serfs — a powerful threat in a deeply religious society. The struggle over **lay investiture** (whether secular rulers could appoint bishops) culminated in the Concordat of Worms (1122), which granted the Church autonomy from secular authority — a major Church victory.

**Scholasticism** — the attempt to reconcile classical Greek philosophy (especially Aristotle) with Christian theology — produced thinkers like Thomas Aquinas. Peter Abelard captured the spirit of the age: studying Aristotle rigorously yet insisting faith came first.

The **Great Schism of 1054** split Christianity into the Roman Catholic Church (dominating western Europe) and the Eastern Orthodox Church (dominant from Greece to Russia). This division shaped very different cultural zones — Catholic Europe vs. Orthodox Byzantium/Kievan Rus.

## Political Trends: Centralizing Monarchies

Across the later Middle Ages, monarchies grew stronger at the expense of feudal lords. Kings built bureaucracies and professional militaries loyal directly to the crown.

**France**: Philip II (r. 1180–1223) built the first real French bureaucracy. Philip IV (r. 1285–1314) convened the first **Estates-General** — an advisory body of clergy, nobility, and commoners — though it had limited power since the upper two estates paid no taxes.

**Holy Roman Empire**: Otto I was crowned Holy Roman Emperor in 962, reviving Charlemagne's legacy. After a prolonged struggle with the papacy over lay investiture, the empire remained fragmented — a loose confederation of German princes rather than a unified state. The empire lasted (with diminishing authority) until Napoleon dissolved it in 1806.

**England**: After William the Conqueror's Norman invasion of 1066, England developed a strong feudal administration. Pressure from nobles produced two landmark documents: the **Magna Carta** (1215), which required the king to respect certain rights (including trial by jury), and the first **English Parliament** (1265), dividing into the House of Lords (nobles and Church hierarchy) and the House of Commons (elected wealthy townspeople). England's Parliament grew stronger than similar bodies on the continent.

The **Hundred Years' War** (1337–1453) between England and France both exhausted the two kingdoms and deepened national identities — soldiers fighting under a monarch began to think of themselves as "English" or "French" rather than as regional subjects.

## The Crusades: Religion, Commerce, and Contact

The **Crusades** (1095–late 1200s) were a series of European military campaigns to reclaim the Holy Land (Palestine) from Muslim control. Their causes were religious, social, and economic: the desire to protect Christian pilgrims, **primogeniture** (eldest-son inheritance) that left younger sons landless and restless, merchant desire for open trade routes, and Church ambition to project power.

Only the **First Crusade** was a clear military success — Jerusalem was taken in 1099 but lost again to Saladin in 1187. The **Fourth Crusade** (1202–1204) never reached the Holy Land — Venice redirected the Crusaders to sack Zara and then Constantinople (a major Venetian trade rival) instead.

Despite military failure, the Crusades dramatically increased European contact with the wealthier, more sophisticated Middle East. European demand for Middle Eastern goods surged, stimulating long-distance trade.

## Social and Economic Transformation

**Long-distance trade** gradually replaced manorial self-sufficiency. Marco Polo's visit to Kublai Khan's court in Dadu (Beijing) in the late 13th century excited European curiosity about Asia, fueling interest in cartography and new trade routes.

A new social class emerged between the nobility and the peasantry — the **bourgeoisie** (burghers): merchants, shopkeepers, and craftspeople whose wealth came from commerce rather than land or religion.

**Urban growth** accelerated alongside the agricultural surplus, but was interrupted by two catastrophes: the **Black Death** (bubonic plague, arriving 1347), which killed roughly one-third of Europe's population, and the **Little Ice Age** (a multi-century cooling trend from roughly 1300), which reduced agricultural yields, slowed city growth, and fueled social unrest. The population loss from plague paradoxically gave surviving serfs more bargaining power — lords desperately needed labor.

**Jews and Muslims** in Europe faced widespread discrimination, scapegoating, and expulsion. Jews, barred from most professions, filled the role of moneylenders (Christians were prohibited by the Church from charging interest). Their financial expertise contributed to economic growth even as antisemitism intensified, leading to expulsions from England (1290), France (1394), and Spain (1492).

## The Renaissance (late 14th–15th century)

The growth of trade, agricultural surplus, and a literate middle class eventually sparked the **Renaissance** — a revival of interest in classical Greek and Roman art, literature, and civic virtue. Key features: the spread of **humanism** (focus on individual human potential), growth in **vernacular literature** (Dante's *Divine Comedy* in Italian; Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* in Middle English), and Gutenberg's **movable-type printing press** (c. 1450), which made books affordable and ideas spread rapidly. The Renaissance began in Italy (supported by Church and wealthy patrons like the Medici) and spread northward by 1500.

## Russia: A Separate Path

In Eastern Europe, Kievan Rus — centered in modern-day Kyiv — built a trade network connecting Scandinavia to the Mediterranean, adopting Orthodox Christianity and maintaining closer ties to Byzantium than to Catholic Europe. Mongol conquest in the 13th century isolated it further. Under Ivan the Great in the late 15th century, Moscow-based princes threw off Mongol rule, marking the foundation of the modern Russian state.
