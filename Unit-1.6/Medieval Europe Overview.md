# Topic 1.6 — Developments in Europe

> [!summary] Essential Question
> How did **beliefs and practices** of religion, **agricultural practices**, and **political decentralization** affect European society from c. 1200 to c. 1450?

## Context: Europe Lags Behind

In 1200, Europe was the least developed major civilization in Afro-Eurasia. Trade had declined since the fall of Rome. Intellectual life had contracted. The unified Roman state was replaced by hundreds of competing small kingdoms. Only the Roman Catholic Church retained power across the continent.

But between 1000 and 1450, learning and trade **began to revive** — the "High Middle Ages." The story of Topic 1.6 is how Europe slowly moved from feudal fragmentation toward more centralized states, driven by trade, religious institutions, contact with the more advanced Islamic world, and eventually the Renaissance.

> [!tip]
> Europe in 1200 looks weak compared to Song China, the Islamic world, or even West African kingdoms. That's the point. By 1450, the acceleration of state-building in Europe sets up the dramatic transformation in Units 3–4 (land-based empires, European expansion). Understanding where Europe starts helps you understand how far it traveled.

## Feudalism: Political and Social Systems

### The Core Logic

European civilization in the Middle Ages was built on **feudalism** — a decentralized system of land-for-loyalty exchanges arising from the need for defense in the absence of a strong central government. The basic structure:

1. **Monarch** → grants **fiefs** (tracts of land) to **lords** → lords become king's **vassals** (owed service)
2. **Lords** → grant land to **knights** → knights become lords' vassals, pledge military service
3. **Lords** → grant land and protection to **peasants** → peasants farm the lord's land and pay tribute in crops, labor, or occasionally coins

Feudalism provided some security for everyone in exchange for loyalty and service. Wealth was measured in **land, not cash**.

### The Code of Chivalry

The feudal system incorporated **chivalry** — an unwritten code of honor, courtesy, and bravery. In theory, knights protected women (placing them on a pedestal without actually giving them rights). In practice, women had very few rights under feudalism.

Compare to **bushido** in Japan (Topic 1.1) — both are warrior codes emphasizing honor, loyalty, and bravery. Key difference: bushido emphasized frugality and martial arts; chivalry emphasized courtesy, duty to God, and courtly love.

### The Manorial System

Large fiefs were called **manors** — economically self-sufficient estates that produced everything residents needed. Key features:

- **Serfs** were not slaves but were **bound to the land** — they could not travel or marry without their lord's permission; their children became serfs
- Manors limited the need for trade or contact with the outside world
- Many serfs spent their entire lives on a single manor, unaware of events elsewhere in Europe

Agricultural improvements came slowly:
- **Three-field system**: One field grew wheat/rye (food); one grew legumes (soil fertilization); one lay fallow (rest). Increased overall productivity.
- **Windmills and heavier plows**: Heavier plows worked well in dense northern European soil; lighter plows suited southern Europe
- These improvements promoted population growth

## Political Trends in the Later Middle Ages

### Growing Monarchies

In the later Middle Ages (1200–1450), monarchies began growing stronger at the expense of feudal lords by building their own bureaucracies and militaries. The process varied by country:

**France**: King Philip I (r. 1180–1223) developed the first real bureaucracy. The **Estates-General** — a body representing the clergy, nobility, and commoners — was convened, but the king exempted clergy and nobles from taxes. Without financial skin in the game, the Estates-General had little power (a problem that would erupt in the French Revolution of 1789).

**Holy Roman Empire**: German king Otto I was crowned Emperor in 962. The Empire survived the **lay investiture controversy** (whether popes or kings could appoint bishops) — resolved in the Concordat of Worms (1122), when the Church gained autonomy from secular leaders. The Empire weakened significantly in the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648).

**Norman England**: William the Conqueror invaded England in **1066** from Normandy (France). His tightly organized feudal system was run by royal sheriffs. In **1215**, English nobles forced King John to sign the **Magna Carta** — establishing that the king must respect certain rights (jury trial before imprisoning a noble; consultation on taxes). In **1265**, the first English **Parliament** formed (House of Lords = nobles + Church; House of Commons = wealthy townspeople). These developments strengthened noble rights but not the general population's.

**Hundred Years' War (1337–1453)**: England and France fought for over a century. Results: (1) national identities solidified — soldiers fought as "English" or "French," not just regional subjects; (2) gunpowder weapons (spread by Mongols from China) proved their military significance.

## The Roman Catholic Church

### Power and Reach

The Roman Catholic Church was the **most powerful institution in Europe** during the Middle Ages. In a continent of hundreds of small political states, the Church was the only truly pan-European institution. Often, Church staff were the only literate people in a community. Almost all education, art, and philosophy of the period was religious.

Key structures:
- **Pope** (supreme bishop in Rome) → **bishops** (regional leaders) → **local priests**
- If a lord displeased the Church, the bishop could cancel religious services — angering serfs and pressuring the lord

The Church held **great power in the feudal system**: it owned land, educated people, provided social services, and could threaten lords with spiritual consequences.

### Monasticism

Although some clergy withdrew to monasteries to pray and meditate, monasteries remained part of the broader economy — farming, producing goods, and providing protection. Women could become **nuns**, finding in convents one of the few places where they could exercise administrative leadership and learning.

### The Great Schism (1054)

The Christian Church in Europe divided into two branches:
- **Roman Catholic Church** — dominated Western Europe until the 16th century
- **Orthodox Church** — dominant in Greece, Russia, and Eastern Europe

### Reform and Corruption

Over time, the Church's wealth and political influence led to corruption (13th–14th centuries). Eventually, Martin Luther and other reformers would break with Rome in the 16th century — but that's Unit 3.

## The Crusades (1095–1200s)

### Why They Happened

Multiple pressures combined:
- Religious: Seljuk Turks restricted Christian pilgrim access to Jerusalem
- Social: Rules of **primogeniture** (eldest son inherits everything) left younger nobles with no wealth and land — a military campaign was a way out
- Economic: Merchants wanted unfettered access to Middle Eastern trade routes

The Church used spiritual authority to recruit fighters (granting relief from penance; promising heaven to those who died on Crusade). The Orthodox patriarch at Constantinople also appealed to Pope Urban II for help.

### Key Crusades

**First Crusade**: The only clear Christian victory. Conquered Jerusalem in July **1099**. Muslim forces under **Saladin** retook it in **1187**.

**Fourth Crusade (1202–1204)**: The most disastrous — never reached the Holy Land. Venice (contracted to transport Crusaders) diverted the campaign to sack **Zara** (an Italian city) and then **Constantinople** (a Venetian trade competitor). The Fourth Crusade permanently weakened the Byzantine Empire.

### Effects of the Crusades

- Promoted **cultural exchange** between Europe and the Middle East
- The Middle East had a higher standard of living — European Crusaders increased demand for Middle Eastern luxury goods, stimulating trade
- Strengthened European monarchies' power as kings organized and led campaigns
- Deepened European contact with Islamic scholarship and trade goods

## Economic and Social Change

### Marco Polo

In the late 13th century, Italian merchant **Marco Polo** visited the court of **Kublai Khan** in Dadu (Beijing), writing descriptions of Mongol customs and Chinese civilization that captivated Europeans. His accounts stimulated European interest in Asia and cartography (mapmaking). Curiosity about what lay beyond Europe — stimulated by Polo's accounts — helped motivate the later Age of Exploration.

### The Rise of the Middle Class (Bourgeoisie)

Economic growth created a new social class — the **bourgeoisie** (also called burghers): shopkeepers, merchants, craftspeople, and small landholders. Economic success began to rival religious vocation or military service as a path to status. This class would become central to European capitalism and the Renaissance.

### Urban Growth and the Black Death

The three-field system and agricultural improvements produced population growth and urban expansion. Markets could operate more frequently. Cities grew.

However:
- After **1300**, the **Little Ice Age** (a five-century climate cooling) reduced agricultural productivity, slowing urban growth
- The **Black Death** (bubonic plague, 14th century) killed as many as **one-third of Europe's population**
- This demographic catastrophe increased serfs' bargaining power with lords — if lords were harsh, serfs could leave (there was now more labor demand than supply)
- The climate and plague also triggered scapegoating of Jews and other minorities

### Jews in Medieval Europe

Jewish communities lived in Muslim areas of Spain and the Mediterranean, then gradually moved northward as Christians reconquered those areas. Many northern European Jews became **moneylenders** because the Church forbade Christians from charging interest on loans to other Christians — Jews were exempt from this restriction. Their financial expertise contributed to European economic growth. However, antisemitism was widespread: Jews were expelled from England (1290), France (1394), Spain (1492), and Portugal (1497).

## The Renaissance

The expansion of trade, agricultural surplus, and a growing middle class created the conditions for the **Renaissance** — a revival of interest in classical Greek and Roman literature, art, and culture. It began in Italy (~1300s) and spread northward by 1400.

Key features:
- **Humanism**: Focus on individual human achievement and potential rather than exclusively on God
- **Vernacular literature**: Writers like Dante (Italian) and Chaucer (Middle English) wrote in their spoken languages rather than Latin
- **Gutenberg's printing press (1439)**: Movable-type press enabled mass production of manuscripts → spread of literacy → rapid spread of ideas
- **Secular literature**: Writers began exploring human concerns alongside religious themes

### Knowledge Transfer and the Renaissance

Islamic scholarship laid the groundwork: paper-making (Chinese technology transmitted via Islam), Indian mathematics (transmitted via al-Andalus), Aristotle's works (preserved and transmitted by Islamic scholars) — all flowed into Europe in the centuries before the Renaissance. The Renaissance was not purely European; it was built on a global knowledge transfer chain.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the Renaissance as a product of cross-cultural exchange — not as a purely European achievement. The chain: India/China → Islamic scholars → al-Andalus → Europe is the key. Gutenberg's press is also often tested as a **technology transfer** story (paper from China → Islamic world → Europe → printing revolution).

## Origins of Russia

Kievan Rus (based in modern Kiev, Ukraine) was a city-state at the center of fur, fish, and grain trade connecting Scandinavia to Central Asia. It adopted **Orthodox Christianity**, maintaining closer cultural ties to Byzantium than to Catholic Europe. The Mongols overtook it in the 13th century.

The Mongols required local nobles to collect taxes. As these nobles grew wealthy, they began resisting Mongol rule. In the late 15th century, under **Ivan the Great** (Moscow), the region became independent of the Mongols — the beginning of modern Russia.
