# Connections — Topic 1.6: Developments in Europe

## Builds On (Prerequisites)

**Classical Rome and Early Medieval Collapse**
Feudalism is incomprehensible without Rome's fall. The Western Empire's 5th–6th century collapse removed centralized governance, trade networks, and military protection — creating the vacuum that feudalism filled. The Catholic Church's survival as a pan-European institution is equally a product of Rome: it inherited Roman administrative structures, used Latin, and claimed Rome as its seat of authority.

**Byzantine Empire (background context)**
The Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire preserved classical Greek learning and Orthodox Christianity throughout the Middle Ages. Its capital Constantinople was the wealthiest city in the Christian world until the Fourth Crusade (1204). Byzantine scholars fleeing the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople (1453) brought Greek manuscripts to Italy, directly fueling the Renaissance. See [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Mongol Empire Overview]] for how the Mongols reshaped Byzantium's neighbors.

## Leads To (Consequences)

**Topic 4.1 — European Exploration**
The Crusades reoriented European ambitions outward. Marco Polo's accounts of Asian wealth fueled desire for direct trade routes. When the Ottoman Empire (after 1453) controlled overland routes to Asia, European powers — especially Portugal and Spain — sought sea routes around Africa and across the Atlantic. Without the Crusades and the commercial contacts they created, the Age of Exploration is hard to explain.

**Topic 4.6 — [[Concept Notes/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]]**
The Church corruption that built up during the High Middle Ages — wealthy monasteries, politically ambitious clergy, the sale of indulgences — fed the Reformation. Martin Luther (1517) attacked exactly the practices that AMSCO flags as problems already visible in the 13th–14th centuries. The printing press (c. 1450) made Luther's ideas spread with unprecedented speed.

**Topic 5.1 — Renaissance and Enlightenment**
The Renaissance (already beginning in Topic 1.6) continues directly into Unit 5. The recovery of classical texts, the growth of humanism, and the printing press all flow forward into the Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment. The weakening of Church intellectual authority in the Renaissance is a precondition for secular scientific inquiry.

## Compare With (Lateral Connections)

**Topic 1.1 — East Asia (Song China and Japan)**
Two important comparisons live here. Song China was far more commercially sophisticated than feudal Europe — it had paper money, gunpowder, and printing centuries earlier. Yet both saw a growing merchant class challenge aristocratic hierarchy. Japan's feudalism (shogun, daimyo, samurai) parallels European feudalism (king, lord, knight) structurally — land for loyalty, warrior codes of honor — but Japan had no equivalent to the Church as a pan-regional authority, and Japan's emperor was a figurehead rather than a real political actor. See [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|East Asia Overview]].

**Topic 1.2 — Dar al-Islam**
In 1200, Islamic civilization was more urban, literate, commercially active, and philosophically sophisticated than Europe. The Crusades forced Europeans to confront this gap — and to desire Islamic goods and scholarship. Much of the Aristotle that European scholastics (Aquinas, Abelard) worked with came via Islamic translations and commentaries (especially Averroes/Ibn Rushd). The contrast between medieval Europe's relative poverty and isolation versus the prosperous Dar al-Islam is a core AP comparison. See [[Unit-1.2/Dar al-Islam Overview|Dar al-Islam Overview]].

**Topic 1.4 — The Americas**
Both medieval Europe and the Americas were politically decentralized relative to classical empires. But the Americas lacked connection to Afro-Eurasian trade networks, so they did not experience the commercial revolution that gradually transformed Europe. The Black Death (which reached Europe via Afro-Eurasian trade routes) did not affect the Americas — a comparison that becomes critical when European contact introduces disease to the Americas after 1492. See [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|The Americas Overview]].

**Topic 1.5 — Africa**
African kingdoms (Mali, Great Zimbabwe) in this period were integrated into trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade networks, often more commercially connected than feudal Europe. The gold and salt trade of West Africa would attract European interest in the coming centuries. Like Europe, parts of sub-Saharan Africa were converting to a world religion (Islam) that served as a cultural unifier across political boundaries.
