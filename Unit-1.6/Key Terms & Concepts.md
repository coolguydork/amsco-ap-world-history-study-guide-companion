# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.6: Developments in Europe

## Political & Social Organization

**[[Concept Notes/Feudalism\|Feudalism]]**
A decentralized political and social system in medieval Europe built on mutual obligations: monarchs granted land (fiefs) to lords, lords to knights, in exchange for military service and loyalty. Wealth was measured in land, not cash. Feudalism emerged from the power vacuum left by Rome's collapse. (AMSCO p. 53)

**Fief**
A tract of land granted by a lord to a vassal in exchange for military service or other obligations. The fief was the economic foundation of the feudal system.

**Vassal**
A person who received land from a lord and owed that lord service — usually military. Knights were vassals of lords; lords were vassals of kings. The relationship was sealed by an oath of fealty.

**Serf**
A peasant tied to the land in the manorial system. Unlike enslaved people, serfs were not property, but they could not leave the manor, travel, or marry without the lord's permission. They paid tribute in crops, labor, or (rarely) coins. Serfdom was hereditary. (AMSCO p. 54)

**Manorial System**
The economic backbone of feudal Europe. Manors were large, self-sufficient estates that produced everything residents needed — food, tools, and protection — minimizing contact with the outside world. Each manor typically had a church, mill, blacksmith, and wine press. (AMSCO p. 54)

**Code of Chivalry**
An unwritten code of conduct for knights emphasizing honor, courtesy, and bravery. It placed women on a symbolic pedestal while providing them few actual rights. Used to regulate disputes within the feudal warrior class.

**Primogeniture**
The practice of passing an entire inheritance to the eldest son. This left younger noble sons landless and restless, creating social pressure that contributed to the [[Concept Notes/Crusades\|Crusades]] — restless younger sons sought wealth through military conquest. (AMSCO p. 57)

**[[Concept Notes/Bourgeoisie\|Bourgeoisie]] (Burghers)**
The emerging middle class of merchants, shopkeepers, craftspeople, and small landholders in late medieval Europe. Their wealth came from commerce rather than land or Church rank. Their rise signaled a gradual shift away from the purely agricultural feudal economy. (AMSCO p. 59)

**Guilds**
Associations of craftspeople and merchants that regulated trade, set quality standards, and trained apprentices. Some women participated as artisans, though not all had property rights.

## The Church

**Great Schism (1054)**
The split of the Christian Church into the Roman Catholic Church (western Europe, led by the Pope in Rome) and the Eastern Orthodox Church (eastern Europe, led by the Patriarch of Constantinople). Each branch developed distinct theology, liturgy, and cultural influence. (AMSCO p. 56)

**Scholasticism**
The dominant intellectual tradition of medieval European universities — the attempt to reconcile classical Greek philosophy (especially Aristotle) with Christian theology. Thomas Aquinas was its greatest practitioner. Peter Abelard was an early, sometimes controversial, voice.

**Lay Investiture Controversy**
The dispute over whether secular rulers (kings and emperors) or the Church could appoint bishops and other Church officials. Resolved by the Concordat of Worms (1122), which established Church autonomy in appointing its own bishops. A major Church victory over secular power. (AMSCO p. 55)

**Monasticism**
The tradition of Christian monks and nuns withdrawing from secular society to pray and meditate in monasteries. Despite their withdrawal, monasteries were economically active — they farmed, provided charity, and sometimes accumulated great wealth. Women could enter religious orders as nuns, gaining unusual opportunities for leadership and administration.

## Politics

**Magna Carta (1215)**
A document forced on King John of England by rebellious nobles, establishing that the king must respect certain rights — including the right to a jury trial before a noble could be imprisoned. It also required royal consultation on certain taxes. A foundational document for the rule of law and limits on royal power. (AMSCO p. 56)

**English Parliament (first full meeting, 1265)**
A legislative body with two chambers: the House of Lords (representing nobles and Church hierarchy) and the House of Commons (elected representatives of wealthy townspeople). Over time, it became stronger than similar advisory bodies on the European continent. (AMSCO p. 56)

**Estates-General**
The French advisory body first convened under Philip IV in 1285–1314, representing three estates: clergy, nobility, and commoners. Because the top two estates paid no taxes, they had little incentive to defend royal government, limiting its power. It played a role leading up to the French Revolution (1789). (AMSCO p. 55)

**Hundred Years' War (1337–1453)**
A series of battles between England and France over territorial claims in France. The war helped forge national identities on both sides and demonstrated the spread of gunpowder weapons (invented in China, diffused westward via [[Concept Notes/Mongols\|Mongols]]). England ultimately retained only the port of Calais in France. (AMSCO p. 56)

**Reconquista**
The centuries-long Christian effort to reclaim the Iberian Peninsula (modern Spain and Portugal) from Muslim rulers. Completed in 1492. It also involved the Norman conquest of Muslim-held Sicily in southern Italy. (AMSCO p. 56)

## Religion and Society

**Crusades (1095–late 1200s)**
A series of European military campaigns aimed at reclaiming the Holy Land (Palestine) from Muslim control. Causes were religious (protect pilgrim access), social (primogeniture left younger sons landless), and economic (merchants wanted open trade routes). Only the First Crusade succeeded militarily. The Crusades dramatically increased European contact with and demand for Middle Eastern goods. (AMSCO pp. 57–58)

**Antisemitism**
Hostility and discrimination toward Jewish people. In medieval Europe, Jews faced expulsions from England (1290), France (1394), and Spain (1492), among others. Jews were blamed for economic hardships (especially during the Little Ice Age and [[Concept Notes/Black Death\|Black Death]]) and restricted to occupations like moneylending, which then reinforced negative stereotypes. (AMSCO p. 59)

## Agriculture & Environment

**Three-Field System**
A crop rotation system that divided farmland into three parts: one planted with wheat or rye (food), one with legumes (to restore soil nitrogen), and one left fallow (to recover). This system dramatically increased agricultural productivity, supporting population growth in the High Middle Ages. (AMSCO p. 54)

**Little Ice Age**
A multi-century period of cooling that began around 1300 and reduced agricultural productivity across Europe. Lower yields meant less trade, slower urban growth, increased disease, and social unrest. Marginalized groups — especially Jews — became scapegoats for the resulting hardships. (AMSCO p. 59)

**Black Death**
The catastrophic outbreak of bubonic plague that swept through Eurasia in the mid-14th century (arriving in Europe by 1347). It killed roughly one-third of Europe's population. The massive labor shortage gave surviving serfs more bargaining power with lords, weakening the manorial system and accelerating the decline of serfdom. (AMSCO p. 59)

## Culture & Ideas

**Renaissance**
A European cultural movement (beginning in 14th-century Italy, spreading north by 1500) characterized by a revival of classical Greek and Roman art, literature, and civic virtue. It produced humanism, vernacular literature, and new artistic techniques. Enabled by trade wealth and middle-class patronage. (AMSCO p. 60)

**Humanism**
The Renaissance intellectual emphasis on individual human potential, secular concerns, and classical learning — in contrast to medieval theology's focus on God and the afterlife. Humanists wrote secular literature, studied ancient texts, and promoted civic education.

**Vernacular**
The everyday language of ordinary people in a region (e.g., Italian, French, Middle English), as opposed to Latin. The rise of vernacular literature — Dante's *Divine Comedy*, Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* — reflected and reinforced regional identities and eventually contributed to the rise of nationalism.

**Printing Press (c. 1450)**
Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type printing press revolutionized the spread of ideas by making books affordable and literacy more accessible. It enabled the rapid spread of Renaissance and, later, Reformation ideas across Europe. (AMSCO p. 60)
