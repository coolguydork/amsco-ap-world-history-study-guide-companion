# People, States & Movements — Topic 1.6: Developments in Europe

## Key People

**Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)**
The greatest scholastic theologian of the Middle Ages. Aquinas synthesized Aristotle's philosophy with Christian doctrine, arguing that reason and faith were complementary rather than contradictory. His *Summa Theologica* became the cornerstone of Catholic intellectual tradition and shaped European university education for centuries.

**Peter Abelard (1079–1142)**
An early scholastic philosopher and theologian who studied classical thinkers — especially Aristotle — and sometimes challenged Church teachings. His famous line, "I should not wish to be Aristotle if this were to separate me from Christ," captures the medieval tension between reason and faith. He remained devout despite controversy, embodying the scholastic spirit of the High Middle Ages. (AMSCO p. 53)

**Pope Innocent III (r. 1198–1216)**
The most powerful pope of the Middle Ages. He launched the Fourth Crusade (1202–1204), declared wars against heretics, and wielded enormous political authority over European monarchs — including forcing King John of England to submit. His papacy represents the peak of medieval Church power.

**William the Conqueror (r. 1066–1087)**
A Norman duke who invaded and conquered England in 1066, fusing Norman and Anglo-Saxon cultures to create the modern English people. He imposed a tightly organized feudal system on England, using royal sheriffs as administrative officials. His conquest established the model of strong, centralized feudal monarchy in England. (AMSCO p. 56)

**King John of England (r. 1199–1216)**
The English king forced by rebellious nobles to sign the Magna Carta in 1215. The document limited royal authority and established that even the king was subject to the law — a foundational principle of English constitutional government.

**Philip II of France (r. 1180–1223)**
The first French king to develop a real royal bureaucracy, beginning the long process of centralizing power in France. He significantly expanded the territory under direct royal control. (AMSCO p. 55)

**Philip IV of France (r. 1285–1314)**
Convened the first Estates-General in France, an advisory body of clergy, nobility, and commoners. Because the top two estates paid no taxes, the Estates-General had little real power — a structural weakness that persisted until the French Revolution. (AMSCO p. 55)

**Eleanor of Aquitaine (c. 1122–1204)**
One of the most powerful women of the Middle Ages. Queen consort of both France and England, she was a major political figure and cultural patron who supported troubadour poetry and courtly literature. Her life illustrates how elite women could exercise significant influence within the constraints of medieval patriarchy.

**Saladin (r. 1174–1193)**
The Kurdish Muslim sultan who united Muslim forces and recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187, ending the gains of the First Crusade. His chivalrous conduct toward defeated enemies was noted even by his opponents, and he became a legendary figure in both Muslim and European tradition.

**Marco Polo (1254–1324)**
A Venetian merchant who traveled to the court of Kublai Khan in Dadu (modern Beijing) in the late 13th century. His written account of Mongol customs — multiple marriages, use of paper money, coal for heating, frequent bathing — astonished Europeans and sparked intense curiosity about Asia. His travels stimulated cartography and European interest in eastern trade routes. (AMSCO p. 58)

**Dante Alighieri (c. 1265–1321)**
Italian poet and father of the Italian literary language. His *Divine Comedy* — mapping hell, purgatory, and heaven — used a religious framework but fearlessly criticized corrupt Church officials. Writing in Italian vernacular rather than Latin, Dante helped legitimize vernacular literature and shaped the Renaissance spirit. (AMSCO p. 60)

**Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343–1400)**
English poet who wrote *The Canterbury Tales* in Middle English vernacular, portraying a cross-section of medieval English society — including satirical portraits of monks and nuns — with wit and realism. Like Dante, his choice of vernacular over Latin reflected growing confidence in regional languages and cultures. (AMSCO p. 61)

**Johannes Gutenberg (c. 1400–1468)**
German inventor of the movable-type printing press (c. 1450). His invention allowed manuscripts to be mass-produced at affordable cost, rapidly spreading literacy and ideas across Europe. The printing press was a precondition for both the Renaissance and the [[Concept Notes/Protestant Reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. (AMSCO p. 60)

**Ivan the Great (r. 1462–1505)**
Moscow-based Russian ruler who threw off Mongol rule in the late 15th century and established the foundations of the modern Russian state. His reign marks the transition from Mongol-dominated Kievan Rus to an independent, centralized Russian principality. (AMSCO p. 61)

## Key States & Empires

**Kingdom of England**
Transformed by the Norman Conquest (1066) into a tightly organized feudal monarchy. Key developments: Magna Carta (1215), first Parliament (1265), and the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453). England developed the strongest constitutional limits on royal power in Europe. (AMSCO p. 56)

**Kingdom of France**
Gradually centralized under kings like Philip II and Philip IV, but the Estates-General's weakness meant French monarchy grew more absolutist than England's. The Hundred Years' War fostered French national identity. (AMSCO p. 55)

**Holy Roman Empire (962–1806)**
A loose confederation of German and central European territories, crowned by Otto I in 962. Never truly unified — princes and bishops retained significant autonomy. Lost the lay investiture controversy (Concordat of Worms, 1122) to the papacy. Devastated by the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648); formally dissolved by Napoleon in 1806. (AMSCO p. 55)

**Papal States / Roman Catholic Church**
Not a conventional kingdom but arguably the most powerful institution in medieval Europe. The Church owned land, ran courts, educated the literate, and could excommunicate kings. The papacy's power peaked under Innocent III and declined in the 14th–15th centuries amid corruption and the Great Schism. (AMSCO pp. 56–57)

**Byzantine Empire (330–1453)**
The eastern continuation of the Roman Empire, centered in Constantinople. Preserved classical Greek learning and Orthodox Christianity. Sacked by the Fourth Crusade in 1204 (an act that deepened the Catholic-Orthodox rift). Fell to the Ottoman Turks in 1453. (AMSCO p. 56)

**Kievan Rus (c. 882–1240)**
The dominant city-state of early Russia, centered in modern Kyiv. Built a trade network linking Scandinavia to Byzantium and Central Asia. Adopted Orthodox Christianity, placing it culturally closer to Byzantium than to Catholic Europe. Overrun by [[Concept Notes/Mongols\|Mongols]] in the 13th century. (AMSCO p. 61)

**Venetian Republic**
A powerful Italian city-state and commercial empire. Venice dominated Mediterranean trade and redirected the Fourth Crusade to sack its trade rival, Constantinople, in 1204. Marco Polo was Venetian. Venice exemplifies the emerging merchant-capitalist class of late medieval Europe. (AMSCO p. 58)

## Key Movements

**The [[Concept Notes/Crusades\|Crusades]] (1095–late 1200s)**
A series of papally-sponsored military campaigns to reclaim the Holy Land. Caused by religious zeal, primogeniture, merchant ambition, and Church political goals. The First Crusade (1095–1099) succeeded; the Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) infamously sacked Constantinople instead. Long-term effect: dramatically increased European exposure to Islamic scholarship, goods, and trade routes. (AMSCO pp. 57–58)

**The Reconquista**
The centuries-long Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rulers, completed in 1492. Alongside the Norman conquest of Sicily, it demonstrates the intersection of religious and political motivations in medieval Christian expansion. (AMSCO p. 56)

**The Renaissance**
A cultural revival beginning in 14th-century Italy and spreading north by 1500, reviving classical learning, producing humanism and vernacular literature, and laying the groundwork for the Scientific Revolution and Reformation. Enabled by trade wealth, a growing middle class, and Church and merchant patronage. (AMSCO pp. 60–61)
