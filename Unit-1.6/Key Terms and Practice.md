# Topic 1.6 — Key Terms, Concepts & Practice

## Core Vocabulary

**Feudalism**
A decentralized political system based on land-for-loyalty exchanges. Monarchs granted fiefs to lords; lords granted land to knights; lords provided land and protection to peasants/serfs. Wealth measured in land, not cash. The dominant European political structure in the Middle Ages.

**Fief**
A tract of land granted by a monarch to a lord in exchange for loyalty and military service.

**Vassal**
A person who owed service and loyalty to another person of higher status (e.g., a lord was a vassal of the king; a knight was a vassal of the lord).

**Serfs**
Peasants bound to the land under feudalism. Not slaves, but could not travel, marry, or leave without the lord's permission. Children of serfs became serfs. Owed the lord crops, labor, and obedience.

**Manorial system**
The economic organization of feudalism. Large self-sufficient estates (manors) produced everything residents needed, limiting the need for trade. Many serfs never left their manor.

**Three-field system**
Agricultural rotation system with one food crop, one legume (to enrich soil), and one fallow field. Increased overall productivity and supported population growth.

**Chivalry**
The unwritten code of conduct for European knights: honor, courtesy, bravery, duty to God, protection of women. Compare to Japanese bushido — both are warrior codes, but with different emphases.

**Magna Carta (1215)**
Document forced on King John by English nobles, requiring the king to respect certain rights (jury trial, consultation on taxation). First major limitation on English royal power. Important step toward constitutional governance — though it protected nobles, not commoners.

**Parliament (England, 1265)**
First English Parliament: House of Lords (nobles + Church hierarchy) and House of Commons (wealthy townspeople). Gradually became more powerful than similar bodies in continental Europe.

**Estates-General (France)**
Advisory body to the French king representing the three estates (clergy, nobility, commoners). Had little power because the upper two estates were exempt from taxes — reducing their stake in government.

**Great Schism (1054)**
Split of the Christian Church into Roman Catholic (Western Europe) and Orthodox (Eastern Europe, Russia, Greece) branches.

**Lay investiture controversy**
The dispute over whether secular (non-religious) rulers could appoint bishops. Resolved in the Concordat of Worms (1122), when the Church won autonomy from secular leaders.

**Crusades (1095–1200s)**
European Christian military campaigns to retake the Holy Land from Muslim control. The First Crusade captured Jerusalem (1099); Saladin retook it (1187). The Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople instead of reaching the Holy Land. Overall, the Crusades promoted cultural exchange between Europe and the Islamic world.

**Reconquista**
The centuries-long effort by Spanish Christians to retake the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rulers. Finally completed in 1492.

**Bourgeoisie**
The growing European middle class of the late Middle Ages: shopkeepers, merchants, craftspeople, small landholders. Economic success began to rival religious vocation or military service as a path to social status.

**Black Death (14th century)**
Bubonic plague that killed up to one-third of Europe's population. Increased bargaining power of surviving serfs; contributed to the decline of the manorial system; triggered antisemitism and scapegoating.

**Little Ice Age (c. 1300–c. 1800)**
A period of climate cooling that reduced agricultural productivity, slowed urban growth, and increased disease and unemployment in Europe. An early example of climate shaping historical development.

**Renaissance**
A European cultural movement (beginning ~1300s in Italy) characterized by a revival of interest in classical Greek and Roman art, literature, and philosophy. Key features: humanism, vernacular literature, secular themes, the printing press.

**Humanism**
A Renaissance intellectual movement emphasizing individual human potential, achievement, and reason over exclusively religious concerns.

**Gutenberg's printing press (1439)**
Movable-type printing press developed by Johannes Gutenberg. Enabled mass production of texts at affordable costs; promoted literacy; rapidly spread ideas across Europe. Technology made possible by paper (Chinese invention, transmitted via the Islamic world).

**Antisemitism**
Prejudice and discrimination against Jewish people. Widespread in medieval Europe; Jews were expelled from England (1290), France (1394), Spain (1492), and Portugal (1497).

**Primogeniture**
The rule that the eldest son inherits the entire estate. Left younger nobles without wealth or land — one factor motivating younger men to join the Crusades.

## Key People

**William the Conqueror**
Norman king who invaded England in 1066. Created a tightly organized feudal system with royal sheriffs as administrative officials. The fusion of Normans and Anglo-Saxons created the modern English people.

**Peter Abelard (1079–1142)**
Medieval Christian scholar who studied Aristotle and sometimes criticized the Church, while remaining a committed Christian. Represents the intellectual tensions of the High Middle Ages.

**King John of England**
Forced by nobles to sign the Magna Carta in 1215 — the first formal limit on English royal power.

**Marco Polo (late 13th century)**
Italian merchant from Venice who visited the court of Kublai Khan. His descriptions of Mongol customs and Chinese civilization stimulated European interest in Asia and cartography.

**Dante Alighieri (1265–1321)**
Italian writer of *The Divine Comedy* (featuring hell, purgatory, and heaven). Used Italian vernacular (not Latin) and criticized corrupt Church officials. A key figure of the Southern Renaissance.

**Geoffrey Chaucer (late 1300s)**
English author of *The Canterbury Tales*, which portrayed a cross-section of English medieval society in Middle English vernacular. A key figure of the Northern Renaissance.

**Johannes Gutenberg (1439)**
Developer of movable-type printing press. Revolutionized the production and spread of ideas in Europe.

**Ivan the Great (late 15th century)**
Moscow-based ruler who led Russia to independence from Mongol rule. Beginning of the modern Russian state.

## AP Multiple Choice Practice

**Question 1.** Which of the following best explains why the Roman Catholic Church held such great power in medieval European society?

A) The Church used military force to compel obedience from European monarchs  
B) The Church was the only pan-European institution providing literacy, education, social services, and shared identity across fragmented political states  
C) The Church controlled all trade routes in the Mediterranean  
D) The Church was supported financially by the Byzantine Empire

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Church's power stemmed from being the only continental-scale institution in a fragmented Europe: literacy, education, identity, and even local governance (via manor churches and bishops). A overstates military power. C and D are wrong.

---

**Question 2.** Gutenberg's printing press (1439) is best understood in global context as an example of:

A) European technological innovation independent of outside influence  
B) Technology transfer: paper from China → Islamic world → Europe enabled the printing revolution  
C) The decline of oral tradition in medieval Europe  
D) The Church's support for the spread of literacy across Europe

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Paper was invented in China, transmitted to Europe via the Islamic world (who learned paper-making from China and taught it to Europeans through al-Andalus). Without paper, a printing press is useless. A ignores the global context. D is wrong — the Church was initially suspicious of the uncontrolled spread of printed ideas.

---

**Question 3.** The Magna Carta (1215) and the formation of the first English Parliament (1265) are best understood as evidence of:

A) The beginning of full democracy in England  
B) Gradual constraints on royal power that primarily benefited the nobility and wealthy townspeople  
C) The decline of feudalism and the rise of peasant power  
D) The Church's control over English political life

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Magna Carta protected noble rights (jury trial, consultation on taxation) — not commoners'. Parliament initially represented nobles, Church officials, and wealthy townspeople — not serfs. Progress toward constitutional governance, but slow and limited to elites.

## SAQ: Europe's Position in the World c. 1200

**Prompt:** Explain why Europe was less economically and politically developed than other regions of the world around 1200 CE.

**Model Response Points:**
- **Feudalism and political fragmentation**: Europe had no powerful central government in most regions. Hundreds of small, competing kingdoms with no shared bureaucracy, no commercial law, no common currency.
- **Manorial self-sufficiency**: The manor system was designed to be self-contained, reducing trade and contact with the outside world. This kept European technology and culture relatively isolated from the more advanced Islamic world and China.
- **Comparison**: Song China had a sophisticated commercial economy, proto-industrialization, and the world's largest urban population. The Islamic world had universities, the House of Wisdom, and commercial networks spanning three continents. West Africa had gold-rich empires and trans-Saharan trade. Europe lagged behind all of these.
- **Beginning of change**: After 1000 CE, the Crusades (even in failure) exposed Europeans to higher standards of living in the Middle East; contact with Islamic scholars transmitted Greek, Indian, and Chinese knowledge; trade began to revive and cities began to grow.

> [!exam-tip]
> This "Europe as starting from behind" framing is essential context for Units 3–4. The acceleration of European state-building and eventual global dominance after 1450 is *more impressive* if you understand how far behind Europe started in 1200.
