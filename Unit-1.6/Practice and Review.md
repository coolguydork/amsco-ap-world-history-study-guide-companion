# Topic 1.6 — Practice and Review

## Multiple Choice Practice

**Question 1.** Which of the following best explains why the Roman Catholic Church held such great power in medieval European society?

A) The Church used military force to compel obedience from European monarchs
B) The Church was the only pan-European institution providing literacy, education, social services, and shared identity across fragmented political states
C) The Church controlled all trade routes in the Mediterranean
D) The Church was supported financially by the Byzantine Empire

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Church's power stemmed from being the only continental-scale institution in a fragmented Europe: literacy, education, identity, and even local governance (via manor churches and bishops). A overstates military power. C and D are wrong.

---

**Question 2.** Gutenberg's printing press (1439) is best understood in global context as an example of:

A) European technological innovation independent of outside influence
B) Technology transfer: paper from China → Islamic world → Europe enabled the printing revolution
C) The decline of oral tradition in medieval Europe
D) The Church's support for the spread of literacy across Europe

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Paper was invented in China, transmitted to Europe via the Islamic world (who learned paper-making from China and taught it to Europeans through al-Andalus). Without paper, a printing press is useless. A ignores the global context. D is wrong — the Church was initially suspicious of the uncontrolled spread of printed ideas.

---

**Question 3.** The Magna Carta (1215) and the formation of the first English Parliament (1265) are best understood as evidence of:

A) The beginning of full democracy in England
B) Gradual constraints on royal power that primarily benefited the nobility and wealthy townspeople
C) The decline of feudalism and the rise of peasant power
D) The Church's control over English political life

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Magna Carta protected noble rights (jury trial, consultation on taxation) — not commoners'. Parliament initially represented nobles, Church officials, and wealthy townspeople — not serfs. Progress toward constitutional governance, but slow and limited to elites.

## Short Answer Question (SAQ) Practice

**Prompt:** Explain why Europe was less economically and politically developed than other regions of the world around 1200 CE.

> [!tip] Model Response Points
> - **Feudalism and political fragmentation:** Europe had no powerful central government in most regions. Hundreds of small, competing kingdoms with no shared bureaucracy, no commercial law, no common currency.
> - **Manorial self-sufficiency:** The manor system was designed to be self-contained, reducing trade and contact with the outside world. This kept European technology and culture relatively isolated from the more advanced Islamic world and China.
> - **Comparison:** Song China had a sophisticated commercial economy, proto-industrialization, and the world's largest urban population. The Islamic world had universities, the House of Wisdom, and commercial networks spanning three continents. West Africa had gold-rich empires and trans-Saharan trade. Europe lagged behind all of these.
> - **Beginning of change:** After 1000 CE, the Crusades (even in failure) exposed Europeans to higher standards of living in the Middle East; contact with Islamic scholars transmitted Greek, Indian, and Chinese knowledge; trade began to revive and cities began to grow.

> [!exam-tip]
> This "Europe as starting from behind" framing is essential context for Units 3–4. The acceleration of European state-building and eventual global dominance after 1450 is *more impressive* if you understand how far behind Europe started in 1200.

## LEQ/DBQ Thinking: Comparison Prompt

**Prompt:** Compare European feudalism with Japanese feudalism in the period c. 1200–c. 1450.

**Brainstorm outline:**

- **Thesis angle:** European and Japanese feudalism were structurally similar systems of decentralized political authority based on land-for-loyalty exchange, but they differed in the relative power of the warrior class versus the monarchy and in the ideological codes that governed warrior behavior.
- **Similarities:** Both had a hierarchy of lords and vassals; both depended on agricultural labor (serfs/peasants); both produced a warrior noble class (knights/samurai); both had warrior codes (chivalry/bushido) that emphasized loyalty, honor, and martial skill.
- **Differences:** In Japan, the emperor was a figurehead and real power lay with the shogun and regional daimyo — military power exceeded royal power. In Europe, monarchs gradually reasserted authority (Magna Carta shows nobles limiting kings, but kings remained central). Chivalry emphasized Christian piety and protection of women; bushido emphasized loyalty unto death and ritual suicide (seppuku).
- **Complexity:** European feudalism declined faster after the Black Death (1347–1353) weakened the manorial system and increased peasant bargaining power; Japanese feudalism persisted much longer.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Europe–Japan feudalism comparison is one of the most commonly tested comparisons in AP World History. Know both the structural similarities AND the key differences. The AP exam loves to ask: "In what ways were these systems similar, and in what specific way did they differ?"
