# Practice & Review — Topic 1.6: Developments in Europe

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the graph: "Average Air Temperatures in Eastern Europe, 850–1950," showing the Little Ice Age cooling trend from approximately 1300 onward. (AMSCO p. 62)*

**Question 1**
Which trend followed the temperature pattern shown in the graph?

(A) The centralization of political power
(B) The rate of growth in the urban population
(C) The use of systems of coerced labor
(D) The spread of Christianity in Europe

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Little Ice Age (beginning ~1300) reduced agricultural yields, which slowed urban population growth — cities depended on agricultural surpluses for food and trade. (A) is wrong because political centralization was a longer-term trend not directly driven by temperature. (C) Coerced labor (serfdom) was already established before the Little Ice Age and was actually weakened — not strengthened — by the labor scarcity following the Black Death. (D) Christianity's spread preceded the Little Ice Age by centuries.

**Question 2**
Which statement accurately reflects an effect of the temperature pattern shown in the graph?

(A) The Little Ice Age caused shortages of food and reduced the amount of agricultural surplus available for trade.
(B) One result of the Little Ice Age was that Vikings from Scandinavia began raiding coasts along England and France.
(C) As climates in Spain cooled, Ferdinand and Isabella financed the voyages of Christopher Columbus in search of land for cash crops.
(D) As a result of the pollution from the growth of industry in Western Europe, temperatures began to rise as the ozone became depleted.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> This is the direct, well-supported effect: lower temperatures → reduced crop yields → less agricultural surplus → less trade. (B) is wrong chronologically — Viking raids preceded the Little Ice Age by centuries (9th–11th c.). (C) Columbus's voyages (1492) were motivated by trade route access, not temperature-driven agricultural needs. (D) Industrial pollution is an anachronism — industrialization began in the 18th–19th centuries, long after the period in question.

**Question 3**
A historian would most likely use the chart to research which of the following developments in the period 1300–1500?

(A) The decline of Norse colonies in Greenland
(B) Native American transfer from dependence on agriculture to hunting
(C) European civil unrest and weakened economies
(D) A surge in witch trials on both sides of the Atlantic

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The Little Ice Age → reduced yields → food shortages → economic strain → social unrest. This is the most directly supported connection. (A) Norse Greenland colonies did decline in this period, partly due to climate, but this is a peripheral detail not central to the AP curriculum. (B) Native American agricultural patterns were shaped by their own environmental contexts, not directly by eastern European temperatures. (D) While witch trials did occur, connecting them to this specific temperature chart is too indirect for AP evidence standards.

## Short-Answer Questions

*SAQ 1 — Based on the Marco Polo passage (AMSCO p. 63):*

> "With these pieces of paper they can buy anything and pay for anything. And I can tell you that the papers that reckon as ten bezants do not weigh one. . . . There is no one who does not visit a bath-house at least three times a week and take a bath — in winter every day, if he can manage it. . . . Here too the inhabitants worship Mahomet [Muhammad] and are subject to the Great Khan."
> — Marco Polo, *The Travels of Marco Polo*, c. 1300

**(A) Identify ONE way in which the writings of Marco Polo affected European relationships with other regions.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Marco Polo's vivid descriptions of the wealth and sophistication of Mongol-ruled China and Central Asia dramatically increased European curiosity about Asia. His accounts of paper money, advanced trade networks, and abundant goods stimulated European interest in cartography and in finding direct trade routes to East Asia — a long-term cause of the Age of Exploration in the 15th–16th centuries.

**(B) Explain ONE way in which Marco Polo's point of view fits into the context of intercultural interactions in the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization
> Marco Polo wrote during a period when the Mongol Pax Mongolica (the peace imposed by Mongol rule across Eurasia) temporarily opened overland trade and travel routes from Europe to East Asia. As a Venetian merchant, Polo approached Asia through a commercial lens — noting what goods existed, how trade operated, and how wealthy the region was. His perspective reflects the growing European commercial interest in cross-cultural exchange that characterized the late 13th century.

**(C) Explain how the passage by Marco Polo illustrates the limitations of intercultural knowledge and understanding.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation / Limitations
> Polo's account, while detailed, reflects a European observer's outsider perspective — he describes customs (multiple marriages, frequent bathing, paper money) as exotic curiosities rather than as practices with their own internal logic. His framing reveals that European understanding of Asia remained superficial and filtered through European assumptions. The fact that Europeans found frequent bathing remarkable shows how different daily life standards were, and suggests how easily cultural misunderstanding could arise even from direct contact.

## SAQ 2 — Think As a Historian (AMSCO p. 64)

**(A) Identify ONE way in which Russia was linked to Europe in the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity
> Kievan Rus maintained trade networks that connected it to both Scandinavia and the Byzantine Empire — and through Byzantium, to the broader Mediterranean commercial world. It had adopted Orthodox Christianity from Byzantium, linking it culturally to the broader Christian world even if it was religiously distinct from Catholic western Europe.

**(B) Explain ONE historical situation in the period 1200–1450 that influenced the development of the Christian Crusades.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The rule of primogeniture — which gave entire estates to eldest sons — left younger noble sons with no inheritance and restless ambitions. The papacy channeled these landless warriors into Crusading campaigns, promising both spiritual rewards (remission of penance) and the possibility of territorial conquest in the Levant. Without this pool of militarily trained, landless nobles, the Crusades could not have been sustained as military campaigns.

**(C) Explain ONE way in which technological developments affected European manorial systems in the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The adoption of the three-field crop rotation system dramatically increased agricultural yields on European manors. By rotating wheat, nitrogen-fixing legumes, and fallow land across three fields, manors produced significantly more food per unit of land than under older two-field systems. This agricultural surplus supported population growth, which over time created more demand for labor and goods — gradually drawing manors into trade networks and eroding their self-sufficiency, ultimately weakening the manorial system itself.

## Think As a Historian: Identify Historical Developments

*For each aspect of European life, identify a development that contributed to change between c. 1200 and c. 1450. (AMSCO p. 64)*

**1. Agriculture**
The adoption of the three-field system and improved plow technology increased crop yields and supported population growth throughout the High Middle Ages, though the Little Ice Age and Black Death reversed these gains after c. 1300.

**2. Growth of towns and cities**
Agricultural surpluses freed labor for non-agricultural work; growing commercial trade created markets that operated more frequently than seasonal fairs; the bourgeoisie emerged as urban craftspeople and merchants accumulated wealth. The Black Death temporarily reversed urban growth but ultimately increased urban wages.

**3. Contact with other parts of the world**
The Crusades (1095–late 1200s) brought Europeans into sustained military and commercial contact with the Islamic world. Marco Polo's late 13th-century travels connected European imagination to East Asia. Both increased European demand for goods from outside Europe and stimulated cartographic interest.

**4. Social structures**
Feudal hierarchy remained dominant through most of the period, but the emergence of the bourgeoisie created a new middle rank between nobles and peasants. The Black Death's labor shortage gave serfs greater bargaining power and weakened the manorial system. Jewish and Muslim minorities faced increasing legal restrictions and expulsion.

**5. Shift from decentralized toward central governments**
Feudal fragmentation gave way to royal bureaucracies (France under Philip II), constitutional frameworks (English Magna Carta, Parliament), and advisory bodies (French Estates-General). Monarchies increasingly employed their own officials rather than relying on feudal lords, though the pace and degree of centralization varied significantly by region.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

*In one to three paragraphs, explain how the beliefs and practices of the predominant religions, agricultural practices, and political decentralization affected European society from c. 1200 to c. 1450.*

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> The Roman Catholic Church was the most powerful unifying force in a politically fragmented Europe. In a world of hundreds of small feudal states, the Church provided a common identity, a common language (Latin), and a common set of values. It ran schools, produced the only literate class, and could discipline even kings. The Crusades — launched by papal authority — show both the Church's enormous mobilizing power and its limits: only the First Crusade achieved its military goal, and the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople revealed how secular and commercial interests could hijack religious movements entirely.
>
> Agricultural practices shaped European society at its foundation. The manorial system made self-sufficiency the norm and restricted serfs' mobility and autonomy. The three-field system's gradual spread increased food production and supported population growth — but this progress was catastrophically reversed by the Little Ice Age (c. 1300) and the Black Death (1347). Paradoxically, the plague that killed roughly one-third of Europe's population gave surviving serfs more leverage over lords desperate for labor, accelerating the eventual decline of serfdom. Agricultural change thus both reinforced and ultimately undermined the feudal social order.
>
> Political decentralization — feudalism's defining feature — shaped everything from governance to identity. In the absence of strong central states, local lords held real power, the Church filled political vacuums, and people identified with their manor or lord rather than with a nation. By 1450, however, centralization was well underway: English Parliament, French bureaucracy, and growing royal authority were all limiting feudal fragmentation. The Hundred Years' War accelerated this by forcing English and French soldiers to identify with their monarchs rather than their regions. By the end of the period, the outlines of modern European nation-states were becoming visible.
