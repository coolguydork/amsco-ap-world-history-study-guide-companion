# Chapter 2: Developments in Dar al-Islam (1200–1450)

**AMSCO Unit:** Unit 1 — The Global Tapestry
**AP Exam Period:** Period 1 (1200–1450)
**Key Region:** Middle East, North Africa, Central Asia, South Asia, Spain

## Summary

By 1200, Islam had spread far beyond its Arabian origins to encompass a vast "Dar al-Islam" (House of Islam) stretching from Spain to Southeast Asia. Though politically fragmented — with the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad serving more as a symbolic center than a true government — the Islamic world was unified by shared religious practices, the Arabic language of scholarship, legal traditions (sharia), and trade networks. The catastrophic Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 ended the Abbasid Caliphate but did not destroy Islamic civilization; instead, new Islamic states emerged in the Mamluks of Egypt, the Delhi Sultanate in India, and eventually the Ottoman Empire. Islamic scholars, scientists, and merchants continued to transmit and expand human knowledge, while Sufi missionaries spread Islam to new regions including sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain how and why Islamic states developed, expanded, and declined during 1200–1450
- Explain the role of trade and cultural exchange in spreading Islam across regions
- Analyze how Sufism contributed to the spread of Islam
- Evaluate the impact of the Mongol conquests on Islamic civilization

## AP World History Key Concepts

- **KC-1.2.I** — Islam's expansion reshaped political authority and cultural identity across Afro-Eurasia
- **KC-1.3.II** — Interregional trade networks (Indian Ocean, Silk Roads, trans-Saharan) integrated the Islamic world
- **KC-2.1.II** — Mongol conquests disrupted and then reconnected Eurasian trade and political networks
- **KC-1.2.III** — Sufism contributed to the spread of Islam through missionary activity and cultural adaptation
