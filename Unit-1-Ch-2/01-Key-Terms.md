# Chapter 2: Key Terms — Dar al-Islam (1200–1450)

## Vocabulary

**Dar al-Islam** — Arabic for "House of Islam"; refers to the regions of the world where Islamic law and practice prevailed. (Significance: provides a framework for understanding how Islam created a shared civilization across politically divided regions.)

**Abbasid Caliphate** — The Islamic empire (750–1258) centered in Baghdad, claiming descent from the Prophet's uncle Abbas; the peak of classical Islamic civilization. (Significance: when the Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258, the political heart of the Islamic world was destroyed, forcing the fragmentation of Islamic political authority.)

**Caliph** — The political and (theoretically) religious successor to Muhammad, serving as head of the Islamic community (umma). (Significance: the title was claimed by multiple rulers after the Abbasid collapse, reflecting the fracturing of unified Islamic authority.)

**Sharia** — Islamic law derived from the Quran, Hadith (sayings of Muhammad), and scholarly interpretation; governs religious practice, family law, commerce, and governance. (Significance: provided legal and moral continuity across the politically fragmented Islamic world.)

**Ulama** — Islamic religious scholars who interpret sharia and administer religious law; the learned class of Islam. (Significance: maintained cultural and religious continuity even when political structures collapsed; comparable to the Chinese scholar-gentry in their role.)

**Sufism** — A mystical tradition within Islam emphasizing direct personal experience of God through prayer, music, poetry, and ascetic practice. (Significance: Sufi missionaries were the primary agents of Islam's spread into sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia, and Southeast Asia by adapting Islamic practice to local cultures.)

**Mamluk Sultanate** — A Muslim state (1250–1517) in Egypt and Syria ruled by enslaved soldiers (mamluks) who overthrew their Ayyubid masters. (Significance: defeated the Mongols at the Battle of Ain Jalut in 1260, halting Mongol westward expansion and preserving Islamic civilization in the Middle East. See [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Mamluks]].)

**Delhi Sultanate** — A series of Muslim dynasties (1206–1526) ruling northern India from Delhi; the first major Muslim political power in South Asia. (Significance: introduced Persian-Islamic governance and culture to India; laid the foundations for the later Mughal Empire. See [[Unit-1-Ch-3/01-Key-Terms#Delhi Sultanate]].)

**Ibn Battuta** — A Moroccan Muslim scholar and traveler (1304–c.1368) who journeyed over 75,000 miles across the Islamic world, from Mali to China. (Significance: his *Rihla* (travel account) is one of the most important primary sources for 14th-century Islamic civilization and the extent of global connectivity.)

**House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma)** — A translation and research center in Abbasid Baghdad that preserved and expanded Greek, Persian, and Indian knowledge. (Significance: Islamic scholars preserved classical texts that would later fuel the European Renaissance; mathematics, medicine, and astronomy all advanced here.)

**Trans-Saharan Trade** — Long-distance commercial exchange across the Sahara Desert connecting North Africa to sub-Saharan West Africa, primarily exchanging gold and salt. (Significance: Islam spread along these routes to West African kingdoms like Mali; gold from West Africa financed Islamic commerce across the Mediterranean. See [[Unit-1-Ch-4/01-Key-Terms#Mali Empire]].)

**Mongol Empire** — The vast empire built by Genghis Khan and his descendants spanning from China to Eastern Europe; the Yuan, Ilkhanate, Chagatai Khanate, and Golden Horde were its successor states. (Significance: the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 ended the Abbasid Caliphate; many Mongol rulers later converted to Islam. See [[Unit-1-Ch-1/01-Key-Terms#Yuan Dynasty]].)

**Ilkhanate** — The Mongol khanate controlling Persia and the Middle East (1256–1335), founded by Hulagu Khan after the sack of Baghdad. (Significance: many Ilkhanate rulers converted to Islam within generations of the conquest, demonstrating how conquered cultures can eventually absorb conquerors.)

**Hajj** — The pilgrimage to Mecca required of all Muslims who are physically and financially able; one of the Five Pillars of Islam. (Significance: the hajj connected Muslims from across the world, spread ideas and goods, and reinforced Islamic unity despite political fragmentation. See [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Mansa Musa]].)

**Madrasas** — Islamic schools attached to mosques that provided education in religious sciences, law, and sometimes medicine and mathematics. (Significance: created a standardized Islamic intellectual culture across regions; produced the ulama who maintained religious continuity.)

**Five Pillars of Islam** — The core religious obligations of Islam: Shahada (declaration of faith), Salat (prayer), Zakat (almsgiving), Sawm (fasting during Ramadan), and Hajj (pilgrimage). (Significance: provided the unifying religious framework that made the Dar al-Islam a coherent civilization despite its political diversity.)

**Arabesque** — Geometric and floral patterns used in Islamic art and architecture, reflecting the prohibition on depicting living beings in religious contexts. (Significance: created a distinctive Islamic artistic tradition that spread across the Dar al-Islam and influenced art globally.)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about how Islam spread — emphasize **trade**, **conquest**, and **Sufi missionaries** as the three main vectors. Also know the difference between what was lost (the Abbasid Caliphate) and what survived (sharia, ulama, Islamic trade networks) after 1258.
