# Conducting World War I

**Unit 7 — Global Conflict | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 469–479**

**AP Themes:** GOV, TEC, SIO
**Essential Question:** How did total war and new technologies change the nature of warfare and its effects on society?
**Learning Objective:** Explain the causes and effects of the conduct of World War I.

## What You Need to Know

Topic 7.3 examines *how* WWI was fought — the new technologies, the strategy of total war, the war's global reach, and the fatally flawed peace settlement that followed. The AP exam frequently tests the causes and effects of total war, the role of technology in creating stalemate, and why the Treaty of Versailles planted the seeds of WWII.

## Changes in Warfare

Before WWI, most Europeans expected a quick, glorious war. Instead, technological advances produced a four-year [[Concept Notes/Total War|Total War]] stalemate that killed approximately 17 million people. The defining feature of the Western Front was **trench warfare** — hundreds of miles of opposing trenches where soldiers lived for months in cold, muddy, rat-infested conditions. New weapons made offensive advances suicidal:

- **Poison gas** (chlorine, phosgene, mustard gas) caused horrific casualties and was later banned by international treaty
- **Machine guns** firing 500+ rounds per minute gave defenders a crushing advantage over attackers
- **Tanks**, introduced by the British in 1916, were designed to cross trenches but remained unreliable
- **Airplanes** began as reconnaissance tools, evolved into fighters and bombers by 1915
- **U-boats** (German submarines) terrorized Atlantic shipping lanes

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves causation here: *why* did new technology produce stalemate rather than quick victory? Defensive weapons (machine guns, barbed wire, trenches) vastly outpaced offensive tactics. Attackers had to cross open ground while defenders fired from cover.

## Total War

**Total war** meant mobilizing an entire nation's resources — economic, social, and psychological — for the war effort. Key features:

- **Economic mobilization:** governments controlled production quotas, wages, prices, and rationing
- **Labor mobilization:** women replaced men in factories and on farms; workers imported from China filled gaps in Britain and France
- **Propaganda:** all belligerents used posters, newspapers, and films to demonize enemies, encourage enlistment, and maintain civilian morale
- **Censorship:** governments suppressed anti-war voices and controlled media

> [!tip]
> Propaganda is a form of SIO (Social Interactions and Organization) theme evidence — it shows how war restructured the relationship between governments and civilians.

## The Global Dimension

WWI extended far beyond Europe because the major powers controlled colonial empires worldwide. Colonial troops fought in battles they had little personal stake in:

- **ANZAC** troops (Australia & New Zealand) suffered devastating losses at **Gallipoli** (1915) — a failed Allied campaign to knock the Ottomans out of the war
- India contributed ~1.5 million soldiers to the British Army
- France deployed ~50,000 West African troops; ~9,000 Indochinese soldiers
- Japan seized German Pacific colonies (Marshall Islands, Mariana Islands, Carolines)
- Arab troops aided British forces against the Ottomans, motivated by British promises of self-rule after the war

The British also used economic ties and democracy rhetoric to draw the United States in. The sinking of the **Lusitania** (May 1915, 1,198 dead including ~128 Americans) and the intercepted **Zimmermann Telegram** (1917, Germany offering Mexico U.S. territory) finally pushed the U.S. to declare war in April 1917.

## Women and the War

Total war transformed women's social roles. Women replaced men in factories, farms, and essential services. Thousands served as nurses, ambulance drivers, and switchboard operators at the front. Russia, Serbia, Romania, and Bulgaria even allowed women in combat; Russia's Maria Bochkareva led the First Russian Women's Battalion of Death (1917). These changes built momentum for women's suffrage movements in the postwar period.

## The Paris Peace Conference and Treaty of Versailles

The 1919 **Paris Peace Conference** was dominated by the **Big Four**: Woodrow Wilson (USA), David Lloyd George (Britain), Georges Clemenceau (France), and Vittorio Orlando (Italy). Their visions clashed:

- **Wilson** wanted "peace without victory" — national [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]], no territorial annexations, and a **League of Nations** to prevent future wars (his **Fourteen Points**)
- **Clemenceau** demanded maximum punishment for Germany, arguing France had suffered most
- **Lloyd George** mediated but ultimately supported harsh terms under domestic political pressure
- **Orlando** walked out when Italy was denied promised territories (Dalmatia, Fiume), fueling Italian nationalist resentment

The resulting **Treaty of Versailles** imposed crushing terms on Germany: billions in **reparations**, loss of all colonies, severe military restrictions, and sole war guilt (the "war guilt clause"). Wilson's idealism was largely defeated. The U.S. Senate refused to ratify the treaty or join the League. Russia was excluded because of its Bolshevik revolution.

Wilson's principle of **self-determination** did reshape European borders: Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia emerged from the wreckage of the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse the Fourteen Points with the Treaty of Versailles — Wilson *proposed* the Fourteen Points but the final treaty ignored most of them. The harsh peace Clemenceau imposed (not Wilson's vision) created the conditions for WWII.

## Why This Matters for the AP Exam

The effects of the Treaty of Versailles are one of the most-tested connections in Unit 7. The reparations burden destroyed Germany's economy (hyperinflation), the [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic\|Weimar Republic]]'s association with national humiliation discredited democratic governance, and resentment created fertile ground for the Nazi Party's rise barely 15 years later. This is a direct causal chain from Topic 7.3 → [[Unit-7.5/Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview|Topic 7.5]] (Unresolved Tensions) → [[Unit-7.9/Causation in Global Conflict Overview|Topic 7.9]] (Causes of WWII).

> [!exam-tip]
> SAQ/LEQ connection: Compare the Treaty of Versailles (1919) with the Peace of Westphalia (1648, [[Unit-3.3/Empires: Belief Systems Overview|Topic 3.3]]). Westphalia ended religious wars by respecting state sovereignty; Versailles humiliated Germany while simultaneously redrawing borders through self-determination — a contradiction that bred resentment.
