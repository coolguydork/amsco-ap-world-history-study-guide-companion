## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to two images: a photograph titled "Removing the Dead from the Trenches" (not published until September 1919, after the war ended) and a British Parliamentary Recruiting Committee poster published in 1915.*

**Question 1.** Comparing the WWI poster from Great Britain and the photo of the trenches supports the interpretation that the British government wanted to:

- A) persuade civilians of the seriousness of the war
- B) recruit soldiers by appealing to their fear of the enemy
- C) emphasize the positive side of military service
- D) portray military service as realistically as possible

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The recruiting poster glorifies military service to attract volunteers, while the photograph of trench dead was suppressed until 1919 — after the war ended. The contrast shows the government wanted recruits to see war as positive and heroic, not as the gruesome reality shown in the photo. Choice A is wrong because the poster downplays seriousness. Choice D is the opposite of the poster's intent.

**Question 2.** Images such as the trench photo supported the claim that World War I was a war that was:

- A) made more deadly by new technology
- B) decided by a handful of victories in large battles
- C) caused by economic rather than political forces
- D) fought on behalf of the principle of self-determination

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The photograph documents mass casualties in trenches — directly resulting from machine guns, barbed wire, and artillery that made offensive advances suicidal. Choices B, C, and D are not supported by the visual evidence; the photo speaks to *how* soldiers died (industrial weapons in trenches), not *why* the war started or how it ended.

**Question 3.** Taken together, the two images demonstrated which of the following trends during World War I?

- A) Britain's powerful role on the global stage declined.
- B) People became more realistic about the costs of war.
- C) The mass casualties from the conflict broke down class barriers.
- D) The role of women changed during the war.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The gap between the glorifying 1915 recruiting poster and the grim 1919 trench photograph captures the arc of the war: initial enthusiasm gave way to harsh disillusionment. By the time the photo was published, the public had learned the brutal reality the poster had concealed. Choices A, C, and D are not supported by these specific images.

## Short-Answer Questions

**SAQ 1.** Use the poem "Dulce et Decorum Est" by Wilfred Owen (1920) to answer all parts.

*"If you could hear at every jolt, the blood / Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs... / My friend, you would not tell with such high zest / To children ardent for some desperate glory, / The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est / Pro patria mori."*

**(A)** Identify Owen's point of view in the poem.

> [!tip] Model Response — Point of View
> Owen's point of view is deeply anti-war and disillusioned. As a British soldier who witnessed the horror of trench warfare and poison gas attacks firsthand, Owen rejects the patriotic narrative that dying for one's country is noble. He calls that idea "The old Lie" — directly challenging the propaganda and cultural attitudes that encouraged young men to enlist. His perspective is shaped by direct experience of industrialized mass death, which he contrasts with the naive enthusiasm of civilians who had not seen the trenches.

**(B)** Explain ONE reason the British government would object to Owen's point of view.

> [!tip] Model Response — Government Objection
> The British government relied on patriotic sentiment to maintain voluntary enlistment and civilian morale — both essential for total war. Owen's poem directly undermines this by exposing the gap between propaganda's glorification of military service and the actual horror of trench warfare. If widely circulated during the war, the poem could have discouraged enlistment, strengthened anti-war movements, and eroded public willingness to accept the casualties and sacrifices total war required. The government had an interest in suppressing exactly this kind of firsthand testimony from the front.

**(C)** Explain ONE aspect of WWI context that explains why many soldiers fought for a political state or empire they did not culturally identify with.

> [!tip] Model Response — Context for Colonial/Minority Service
> Many colonial soldiers — from India, West Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and Indochina — fought for European empires they were subjects of but did not culturally belong to. These soldiers were conscripted or recruited under colonial authority structures; refusal was not a realistic option. Some fought in exchange for promises of postwar self-rule or increased autonomy from their colonizers. Others were motivated by economic incentives (military pay). The imperial system created legal and economic coercion that made service effectively compulsory even where formal conscription was not applied — illustrating how total war extended the reach of imperial governance over colonial populations.

**SAQ 2.** Answer all parts of the question.

**(A)** Identify ONE way that advances in war technologies affected the development of World War I.

> [!tip] Model Response — Technology Effect
> The development and widespread use of machine guns produced a four-year stalemate on the Western Front. Machine guns firing 500+ rounds per minute gave defending forces an overwhelming advantage over any advancing infantry, making traditional offensive tactics suicidal. Neither side could break through the other's defensive lines without catastrophic casualties, which is why battles like Verdun (1916) produced over a million casualties with minimal territorial change. The stalemate prolonged the war from the expected quick campaign into a four-year grinding conflict, dramatically increasing total casualties and deepening the social and economic disruption of total war.

**(B)** Explain ONE way that propaganda or media influenced European society during WWI.

> [!tip] Model Response — Propaganda's Social Impact
> Government propaganda extended state authority into the psychological lives of civilians in an unprecedented way. By demonizing enemies, exaggerating atrocities (as in the British Bryce Report on German atrocities in Belgium), and glorifying sacrifice, governments shaped mass opinion to support the war effort and justify censorship and restrictions on civil liberties. This normalization of state-managed information created a new relationship between government and citizen — one in which the state actively managed what civilians believed about the war. The long-term effect was a deep public distrust of media and government once soldiers returned and contradicted the official narrative, contributing to the disillusionment of the "Lost Generation."

**(C)** Explain ONE way the Paris Peace Conference left problems for Europe to confront.

> [!tip] Model Response — Unresolved Problems
> The Treaty of Versailles imposed massive reparations on Germany, requiring it to pay billions of dollars for war damages. This economic burden devastated the German economy through the 1920s, causing hyperinflation that wiped out the savings of the German middle class. The resulting economic desperation, combined with the political humiliation of the "war guilt clause" that assigned Germany sole responsibility for WWI, created deep resentment toward the Weimar Republic that had signed the treaty. This resentment was directly exploited by the Nazi Party, which rose to power in 1933 by promising to undo Versailles — illustrating how the peace settlement's harshness planted the seeds of WWII.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

*In one to three paragraphs, explain how governments used a variety of methods to conduct World War I.*

> [!tip] Model Response
> Governments conducting WWI moved far beyond traditional military strategy to mobilize entire nations in what historians call "total war." Economically, belligerent governments took control of industrial production, setting output quotas, controlling prices and wages, rationing food, and directing factories toward war materials. Workers who had previously been employed in civilian industries were redirected to military production; women and imported laborers filled gaps left by conscripted men.
>
> Psychologically, governments deployed massive propaganda campaigns to maintain civilian morale, encourage enlistment, and demonize enemies. Posters, newspapers, and government-commissioned art shaped public perception of the war and suppressed dissent. Britain's Bryce Report, for example, documented German atrocities in Belgium in ways designed to outrage neutral nations. All belligerents censored media that undermined the war effort.
>
> Militarily, governments deployed new technologies — poison gas, machine guns, tanks, airplanes, and submarine blockades — that transformed warfare into industrialized mass killing. The stalemate these technologies produced required governments to keep mobilizing more men through conscription, deepening the cycle of total war commitment. Ultimately, governments also mobilized their colonial empires: troops from India, Africa, Australia, New Zealand, and Indochina fought in a European war, demonstrating how deeply imperial structures shaped the conduct of the conflict.
