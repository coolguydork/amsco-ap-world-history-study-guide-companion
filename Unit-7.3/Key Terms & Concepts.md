# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 7.3 — Conducting World War I | AMSCO pp. 469–479**

---

**total war**
Conflict in which entire nations mobilize all resources — economic, social, military, and psychological — for the war effort; civilians and civilian production become as important as the military itself. WWI was the first large-scale total war: governments controlled factories, set prices, rationed food, censored media, and deployed propaganda. WWII would take total war even further. AP angle: total war explains *why* WWI changed civilian life so dramatically.

**conscription**
Compulsory military service (the draft). All major powers except Britain entered WWI with universal conscription, allowing them to field mass armies of millions. Britain relied on volunteers until 1916 when casualties made conscription necessary. By war's end, 1 in 4 British men had served. AP angle: conscription is a key feature of total war — it shows how governments mobilized entire populations, not just professional armies.

**trench warfare**
The defining military tactic of the Western Front: opposing armies dug hundreds of miles of trenches facing each other, separated by a deadly "no man's land." Soldiers lived in trenches for months — cold, muddy, rat-infested — fighting over tiny strips of territory. Machine guns, barbed wire, and artillery made crossing no man's land suicidal. Result: a four-year **stalemate**. Immortalized in Erich Maria Remarque's *[[Concept Notes/All Quiet on the Western Front\|All Quiet on the Western Front]]* (1929).

**stalemate**
A military deadlock where neither side can gain decisive advantage. The Western Front became a four-year stalemate because defensive technology (machine guns, barbed wire, trenches) vastly outpaced offensive tactics. The stalemate only broke in 1918 when U.S. troops tipped the balance of resources toward the Allies.

**poison gas**
Chemical warfare agents — chlorine, phosgene, and mustard gas — deployed extensively on the Western Front. Caused horrific casualties: blindness, lung damage, slow death. Soldiers were issued gas masks but were not always able to use them in time. After the war, the 1925 Geneva Protocol banned poison gas in warfare. AP angle: illustrates how industrial technology transformed warfare into industrialized mass killing.

**machine guns**
Rapid-fire weapons capable of 500+ rounds per minute; gave WWI defenders a crushing advantage over attackers advancing across open ground. Made traditional cavalry charges and infantry assaults suicidal. A primary reason defensive warfare produced stalemate on the Western Front.

**tanks**
Armored, tracked military vehicles introduced by the British in 1916 (Battle of the Somme) to cross trenches and barbed wire. Originally called "landships" by the Royal Navy; renamed "tanks" during development as a security cover — prototypes were shipped disguised as water tanks. Initially unreliable, but they pioneered combined-arms warfare and became decisive in WWII.

**airplanes**
First used in significant numbers during WWI, initially for reconnaissance of enemy lines. By 1915 fitted with machine guns; individual "air aces" fought "dogfights." Too light and limited to be strategically decisive in WWI, but they established the concept of air power that would dominate WWII.

**submarines**
Underwater warships that transformed naval warfare from surface battles to commerce raiding. Germany's U-boats wreaked havoc on Allied Atlantic shipping. Their unrestricted use — attacking neutral and civilian vessels without warning — was a major factor drawing the United States into WWI.

**U-boat**
German submarine (*Unterseeboot*). Germany's policy of unrestricted U-boat warfare targeted any vessel in the war zone, including neutral ships and ocean liners. The sinking of the **Lusitania** (1915) and resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare in 1917 were key causes of U.S. entry into WWI.

**Lusitania**
British ocean liner sunk by a German U-boat on May 7, 1915; 1,198 killed, including ~128 Americans. The sinking inflamed U.S. public opinion against Germany. Germany briefly halted unrestricted submarine warfare after diplomatic protests, then resumed it in 1917 — the final trigger for U.S. entry into the war.

**Zimmermann Telegram**
Secret January 1917 German diplomatic message to Mexico proposing a military alliance: Germany would help Mexico recover Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona if Mexico joined the war against the U.S. British intelligence intercepted and decoded it; when published in American newspapers it caused outrage. The final catalyst for U.S. declaration of war in April 1917.

**propaganda**
Government-produced communication designed to shape public opinion through selective, exaggerated, or false information. All WWI belligerents used propaganda heavily: recruitment posters, newspaper articles, films. American and British propaganda demonized the German army; German propaganda demonized the Allies. AP angle: propaganda is a defining feature of total war — it shows governments mobilizing civilian psychology as a war resource.

**global war**
A conflict spanning multiple continents and involving nations worldwide. WWI was the first truly global war since the Seven Years' War (18th c.), drawing in combatants through colonial ties. Battles were fought in Africa, the Middle East, the Pacific, and the Atlantic, not just Europe. Japan seized German Pacific colonies; ANZAC troops fought in Gallipoli; Indian, African, and Indochinese soldiers fought in Europe.

**ANZAC**
Australia and New Zealand Army Corps. About 500,000 Australians and New Zealanders enlisted for Britain in WWI. Their most famous campaign was the disastrous **Gallipoli** landing (1915). The ANZAC experience became foundational to Australian and New Zealand national identity. AP angle: illustrates how colonial populations were mobilized for a European war they had little direct stake in.

**Gallipoli**
Peninsula in northwestern Turkey (Ottoman Empire); site of a failed Allied amphibious campaign (April 1915–January 1916) aimed at knocking the Ottomans out of the war and opening a supply route to Russia. Heavy ANZAC and Allied losses with no strategic gain. Became a symbol of military incompetence and of the human cost of colonial service.

**[[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]]**
Woodrow Wilson's principle that peoples have the right to determine their own political future. Applied at the Paris Peace Conference to reshape European borders after the collapse of the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires — creating Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia. However, self-determination was selectively applied and *not* extended to colonial peoples in Asia and Africa, fueling anticolonial nationalism.

**Paris Peace Conference**
1919 negotiations in Paris among the victorious Allied powers (Germany, Russia excluded) that produced the Treaty of Versailles and other peace treaties. Dominated by conflicting visions — Wilson's idealism vs. Clemenceau's demands for punishment. Redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East but left many grievances unresolved, setting conditions for WWII.

**Big Four**
The four principal Allied leaders at the Paris Peace Conference: Woodrow Wilson (USA), David Lloyd George (Britain), Georges Clemenceau (France), and Vittorio Orlando (Italy). Their competing visions shaped the final treaty. Orlando walked out when Italy was denied promised territories; Russia was excluded as a Bolshevik state.

**Fourteen Points**
Woodrow Wilson's 1918 peace program: 14 principles including freedom of the seas, national self-determination, open diplomacy, and a **League of Nations**. Represented idealistic internationalism. Largely ignored at the Paris Peace Conference — Clemenceau's punitive vision prevailed. The U.S. Senate then refused to ratify Versailles or join the League, leaving Wilson's program a failure.

**League of Nations**
International peacekeeping organization proposed by Wilson in the Fourteen Points; established by the Treaty of Versailles. Designed to resolve disputes through open negotiation rather than secret alliances. Fatal weakness: the U.S. Senate refused to join. Without American power, the League lacked teeth and failed to prevent WWII aggression.

**reparations**
Financial penalties imposed on Germany by the Treaty of Versailles to compensate Allies for war damage. Germany was required to pay billions of dollars — a burden that devastated its economy, fueled hyperinflation through the 1920s, and generated bitter resentment. Widely viewed by historians as a direct cause of the conditions that enabled Nazi rise to power.

**Treaty of Versailles**
The 1919 peace treaty with Germany; the central product of the Paris Peace Conference. Key terms: billions in reparations, surrender of all colonies, severe military restrictions, and the "war guilt clause" (Article 231) forcing Germany to accept sole responsibility for WWI. Germans found the treaty humiliating. The [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic\|Weimar Republic]] that signed it became deeply unpopular, creating the political conditions for the Nazi Party.

**Weimar Republic**
The democratic German government established after WWI's end (1919–1933). Accepted the Treaty of Versailles, making it permanently associated with national humiliation and economic hardship. Struggled with hyperinflation, reparations, political extremism from left and right. Its instability enabled the Nazi Party to win power in 1933 — barely 15 years after the war ended.

**All Quiet on the Western Front**
1929 anti-war novel by German author Erich Maria Remarque, based on his own WWI experience as a young soldier. Depicts the psychological and physical devastation of trench warfare from a German soldier's perspective. One of the most widely read war novels in history; adapted into a celebrated 1930 film. Illustrates the disillusionment of the "[[Concept Notes/Lost Generation\|Lost Generation]]" — young men whose lives were destroyed by industrialized warfare.

> High-frequency AP connections: **total war → propaganda → SIO theme** (how war restructures society); **reparations + war guilt clause → Weimar instability → Nazi rise** (direct causal chain across Topics 7.3 → 7.5 → 7.9); **Fourteen Points vs. Treaty of Versailles** (idealism vs. punitive peace).

> [!warning]
> Common mistake: students conflate the **Fourteen Points** (Wilson's *proposal*) with the **Treaty of Versailles** (what was actually signed). The treaty largely rejected Wilson's proposals in favor of Clemenceau's punitive approach.
