# People, States & Movements

**Unit 7.3 — Conducting World War I | AMSCO pp. 469–479**

## Key Leaders at the Paris Peace Conference

**Woodrow Wilson** (1856–1924)
28th President of the United States (1913–1921). Led the U.S. into WWI in April 1917 after the Zimmermann Telegram and resumed German submarine warfare. His **Fourteen Points** outlined an idealistic postwar vision: national [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]], freedom of the seas, open diplomacy, and a **League of Nations**. At the Paris Peace Conference, Wilson argued for "peace without victory" — no punitive terms for Germany. He largely failed: Clemenceau's demands prevailed, and the U.S. Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles or join the League. His legacy: the self-determination principle did reshape European borders, but his internationalist vision died at home.

> [!exam-tip]
> Wilson's failure is itself exam-testable: *why* did the U.S. not join the League? Senate opposition (isolationism). This is why the League lacked power to prevent WWII.

**Georges Clemenceau** (1841–1929)
French prime minister at the Paris Peace Conference, nicknamed "The Tiger." France had suffered the most devastating battlefield damage of any Western Allied power — much of northern France was destroyed. Clemenceau demanded maximum punishment for Germany: massive reparations, permanent military restrictions, and territorial losses. He argued that Wilson's idealism was dangerously naive. His vision largely prevailed in the Treaty of Versailles. AP angle: Clemenceau represents the punitive peace that created Weimar instability and the conditions for Nazi rise.

**David Lloyd George** (1863–1945)
British prime minister at the Paris Peace Conference. Took a middle position between Wilson's idealism and Clemenceau's revenge-seeking. Often acted as an intermediary. Privately feared that humiliating Germany would create future instability, but agreed to harsh terms under intense domestic political pressure from British voters who wanted Germany punished. Represents the difficulty of democratic leaders balancing long-term statesmanship against short-term popular demands.

**Vittorio Orlando** (1860–1952)
Italian prime minister at the Paris Peace Conference. Italy had joined the Allies in 1915 after being promised significant territorial gains (Dalmatia and other Adriatic territories). When the conference refused Italy's demands — particularly the city of Fiume — Orlando walked out in protest. Italy received far less than promised. This "mutilated victory" (*vittoria mutilata*) fueled Italian nationalist resentment and created fertile ground for Mussolini's Fascist movement.

> [!tip]
> Remember the Big Four tensions as a spectrum: Wilson (idealism) ←→ Lloyd George (pragmatism) ←→ Clemenceau (punishment). Orlando was a fourth voice demanding territorial reward — his walkout illustrates how the conference alienated even victorious allies.

## Other Key Figures

**Wilfred Owen** (1893–1918)
British poet and soldier; wrote "Dulce et Decorum Est," one of WWI's most powerful anti-war poems. Describes the horror of a gas attack; its final lines call patriotic war rhetoric "The old Lie." Killed in combat one week before the armistice. AMSCO uses his poem to open Topic 7.3 — it exemplifies the disillusionment of soldiers who experienced industrial warfare firsthand.

**Erich Maria Remarque** (1898–1970)
German author who served as a young soldier in WWI. Wrote *[[Concept Notes/All Quiet on the Western Front\|All Quiet on the Western Front]]* (1929), a devastating account of trench warfare from the German perspective. The novel became one of the most widely read anti-war works in history. Represents the cultural response to industrialized warfare and the disillusionment of the "[[Concept Notes/Lost Generation\|Lost Generation]]."

**T.E. Lawrence ("Lawrence of Arabia")** (1888–1935)
British officer who organized and supported the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire during WWI. Arab troops, motivated by British promises of postwar self-rule, attacked Ottoman forts and helped capture Baghdad, Damascus, and Jerusalem. The British promises of Arab self-determination were later contradicted by the Sykes-Picot Agreement (secret plan to divide the Middle East between Britain and France), fueling decades of Arab resentment.

**Maria Bochkareva** (1889–1920)
Russian soldier who commanded the First Russian Women's Battalion of Death (1917), an all-female military unit created by the Russian government as propaganda to shame men into continuing to fight. Represents both the expanded role of women in total war and the limits of that role — created for propaganda purposes, not as genuine gender equality.

## Key States and Powers

**Germany / [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic\|Weimar Republic]]**
The war ended with Germany's defeat in November 1918; Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated. The new democratic **Weimar Republic** was forced to sign the humiliating Treaty of Versailles. The reparations burden, hyperinflation, and association with national defeat made Weimar perpetually fragile. It survived until 1933 when the Nazi Party took power — a direct legacy of the punitive peace.

**Ottoman Empire**
A Central Power that controlled much of the Middle East; fought the Allies on multiple fronts. Arab troops supported by Britain helped capture key Ottoman cities. The empire was dismembered by postwar treaties; its collapse created the modern Middle Eastern state system under British and French mandates — with consequences still felt today.

**United States**
Initially neutral; entered the war April 1917 after the Zimmermann Telegram and resumed submarine warfare. Fresh U.S. troops and resources tipped the military balance toward the Allies. President Wilson's postwar idealism was then largely defeated at Paris and back home. The U.S. never joined the League of Nations — a pivotal failure of postwar internationalism.

**Colonial Populations**
One of the most AP-testable aspects of 7.3: the war was *global* because of colonialism. Colonial subjects from India (~1.5 million), West Africa, Algeria, Indochina, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and Arab territories all fought in a war driven by European rivalries. Many were promised self-rule after the war; most promises were broken. This betrayal accelerated anticolonial nationalist movements in the interwar period (**[[Unit-7.5/Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview|Topic 7.5]]**).

> [!exam-tip]
> For DBQ/LEQ: colonial participation in WWI is powerful evidence for *both* the global nature of imperialism (TEC, ECN themes) *and* the origins of 20th-century anticolonial nationalism (SIO theme). Many colonial soldiers returned home having fought for empires that still refused to treat them as equals.
