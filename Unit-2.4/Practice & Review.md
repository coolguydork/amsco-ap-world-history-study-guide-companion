*Refer to the Camel Saddles chart (AMSCO p. 108):*

---

**Question 1.** Which was the most important impact of the improvements in saddles in assisting the spread of Islam in Africa?

(A) They allowed younger people to ride camels.
(B) They increased the demand for camels.
(C) They contributed to increased trans-Saharan trade.
(D) They strengthened Islamic armies.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The East African (Somali) load saddle was the breakthrough — it allowed camels to carry up to 600 lbs of cargo, making bulk commercial exchange across the Sahara viable. More commerce meant more contact between Muslim merchants and West African societies, which is the mechanism through which Islam spread. Choice D is tempting but secondary: the visibility saddle (North Arabian) aided military use, but the primary historical significance in this context is commercial, not military.

---

**Question 2.** The information on camel saddles is an example of how

(A) people adapted technology based on their needs
(B) differences in camels suited them for different tasks
(C) innovation results from outside influences
(D) climate variations influenced the relationship between people and animals

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Each saddle was developed by a specific regional group to solve a specific problem: South Arabians needed comfortable riding, North Arabians needed battlefield visibility, Somalis needed cargo transport. The diversity of designs reflects deliberate adaptation to local needs — a classic example of technology shaped by context. Choice B is wrong because the chart is about saddle types, not camel breeds. Choice C is partially plausible but not directly supported; choice D misreads the evidence.

---

**Question 3.** Where was the saddle developed that had the greatest impact on trade throughout the Middle East and Africa?

(A) Along the Mediterranean coast
(B) Near the Islamic centers of Timbuktu and Gao
(C) In countries of the Sahara
(D) On East African trade routes

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The Somali (East African) saddle — designed for carrying loads rather than riders — had the greatest impact on trade because it enabled camels to function as cargo animals. The Somalis were semi-nomadic and needed to transport their possessions, so they engineered a saddle for load-bearing. Choices A, B, and C describe the wrong regions entirely. *(AMSCO p. 104)*

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Stimulus-Based (AMSCO p. 109)

**Source A:** "Between the 11th and the 17th centuries West Africa was the leading supplier of gold to the international economy. African gold contributed to the functioning of the domestic economy in Europe… And it contributed to the wealth of the great states of the Western Sudan."
— Anthony G. Hopkins, *An Economic History of West Africa*, 1973

**Source B:** "Copper mined in Mali came to be a crucial element in trade patterns. It could be exchanged with the peoples to the south who controlled the sources of gold. Kola nuts, animal skins, slaves, grain, meat, and even dairy products were also transported by trading networks."
— J. Rotondo-McCord, 1998

**(A)** Identify ONE example of transportation technology that led to growth in interregional trade during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> The camel, specifically equipped with the Somali load-bearing saddle, was the key transportation technology enabling trans-Saharan trade growth. Camels could carry up to 600 pounds of cargo, survive extreme desert heat, and go extended periods without water — making sustained crossing of the Sahara possible at commercial scale.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which Mali's wealth contributed to the expansion of its government during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Mali's rulers used revenue from taxing trans-Saharan trade to fund military forces equipped with horses and iron weapons. These forces enabled territorial expansion — Mali absorbed resource-rich areas and trade nodes, which in turn generated even more taxable commerce. The result was a feedback loop in which commercial wealth directly funded and enabled political expansion, making Mali's government larger and more powerful than Ghana's had been.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which a resource from Mali was used in Sub-Saharan trade during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Copper mined in Mali functioned as a trade currency within sub-Saharan exchange networks. As Source B explains, Malian copper could be exchanged with southern peoples who controlled gold sources — meaning copper served as an intermediary commodity that unlocked access to gold, the most valuable export in trans-Saharan commerce. This made copper mining economically strategic for Mali beyond its direct value.

### SAQ 2 — Open (AMSCO p. 109)

**(A)** Identify ONE economic impact Muslim traders had on Sub-Saharan Africa.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Muslim traders introduced new trade goods (textiles, paper, horses) and integrated West African gold markets into the broader Afro-Eurasian monetary system. By the 9th century CE, West African gold was supporting monetary systems across Europe and the Middle East — an economic integration driven by Muslim merchant networks.

**(B)** Identify ONE cultural influence Muslim traders had on Sub-Saharan Africa.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Muslim traders introduced Islam into the Mali region in the 9th century, beginning a process of religious and cultural diffusion. Over subsequent centuries, ruling elites converted (gaining access to Islamic commercial networks and legitimacy), mosques and schools were built in cities like Timbuktu, and the Arabic script became part of Mali's intellectual culture.

**(C)** Explain ONE similarity between trans-Saharan trade and trade on the Silk Roads in Asia.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Both the trans-Saharan and Silk Road networks spread Islam via commerce rather than conquest. On the Silk Roads, Muslim merchants carried their faith into Central Asia and China; on trans-Saharan routes, Muslim merchants from North Africa carried it into West Africa. In both cases, the mechanism was the same: merchants established trade relationships, mosques appeared in commercial cities, and local rulers converted to gain access to Islamic trade networks. The spread of religion as a byproduct of commerce is a shared pattern across both networks. *(See [[Unit-2.1/Themes & Historical Reasoning|Themes & Historical Reasoning]])*

## Historical Reasoning Practice

**Think As a Historian — Identifying Historical Processes (AMSCO p. 110)**

Which of the following identifies a historical *process* (not just a fact)? Explain why.

1. "The government of Mali's power grew as it taxed nearly all trade entering West Africa."
2. "Trans-Saharan trade followed seven north-south and two east-west routes."
3. "In East Africa, a large variety of imports and exports overlapped."
4. "Mansa Musa's reign deepened the support for Islam in Mali."

> [!tip] Model Response — Historical Reasoning
> Statements **1 and 4** identify historical processes. Statement 1 explains *how* Mali's government expanded — through a mechanism (taxing trade), not just a fact about geography or route counts. Statement 4 explains *how* Islam's institutional presence grew — through Mansa Musa's patronage of mosques and schools, which created lasting religious infrastructure. In contrast, statements 2 and 3 are descriptive facts about geography and trade patterns; they tell you *what* existed, not *how* something changed over time. The AP exam rewards statements that identify agency, mechanism, and sequence.

## Reflect: Essential Question

**In 1–3 paragraphs: What were the causes and effects of trans-Saharan trade, and how did the growth of empires influence trade and communication?**

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> Trans-Saharan trade grew for two interconnected reasons: technological and political. Technologically, the spread of camels into North Africa (arriving c. 3rd century BCE, replacing horses after ~300 CE) provided the only animal capable of crossing the Sahara with heavy loads. The Somali load-bearing saddle, allowing camels to carry up to 600 lbs, was the critical innovation that made bulk commerce viable. Politically, Muslim merchants from Southwest Asia dramatically expanded the trade network in the 7th–8th centuries, bringing commercial networks, shared legal frameworks, and religious ties that lowered transaction costs across the desert.
>
> The effects were transformative. Economically, West African gold became foundational to monetary systems across Europe and the Middle East, and the gold-salt exchange generated extraordinary wealth for West African states. Culturally, Islam spread into sub-Saharan Africa not through conquest but through commerce — merchants established relationships, rulers converted for economic and political advantage, and cities like Timbuktu became world-class Islamic intellectual centers. These changes were lasting: Islam remains a major religion in West Africa today.
>
> Empire growth both depended on and amplified trade. Ghana, then Mali, then Songhai each profited by taxing the routes, using revenue to fund military forces that expanded territory, absorbed more trade nodes, and generated more revenue. Mansa Musa's 1324 hajj — with 100 camels and a retinue of thousands — was itself an act of imperial communication, projecting Mali's wealth and Islamic credentials to the entire Afro-Eurasian world. Empires didn't just benefit from trade; they shaped its scale, security, and reach.
