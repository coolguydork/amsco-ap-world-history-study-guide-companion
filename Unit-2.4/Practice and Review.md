# Topic 2.4 — Practice and Review

## Multiple Choice Practice

**Question 1**

The camel saddle developed by Somali peoples was historically significant primarily because it:

A) Allowed soldiers to ride camels in battle more effectively than on horses  
B) Made it possible for camels to carry heavy loads of trade goods across the Sahara, enabling large-scale trans-Saharan commerce  
C) Gave Muslim merchants from Southwest Asia an advantage over Berber traders in North Africa  
D) Reduced the amount of water camels needed on long desert journeys

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The load-bearing saddle (up to 600 lbs) transformed camels from riding animals into viable cargo carriers for long-distance trade. This is the technology that made trans-Saharan commerce at scale possible. A is about a different saddle design (the North Arabian battle saddle). C is not supported. D misunderstands camels' water capacity — that's a biological feature, not a saddle innovation.

**Question 2**

Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca is historically significant for all of the following reasons EXCEPT:

A) It displayed the extraordinary wealth of Mali to North Africa and the wider Islamic world  
B) It led to gold inflation in Egypt that depressed prices for years  
C) Upon his return, he established Islamic schools and mosques in Timbuktu  
D) It marked the first time a sub-Saharan African ruler had converted to Islam

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> Islam had been present in West Africa since the 9th century, and Mali's founder Sundiata was likely Muslim. Mansa Musa's pilgrimage *deepened* and publicized Mali's Islamic connections — it did not represent a first conversion. A, B, and C are all accurate effects of the 1324 pilgrimage.

**Question 3**

The trans-Saharan trade routes and the Indian Ocean trade routes were similar in that both:

A) Were controlled primarily by Chinese merchants  
B) Relied on the same seasonal wind patterns to time merchant voyages  
C) Connected distant regions through the exchange of goods, spread Islam, and generated wealthy trading cities  
D) Were disrupted by the Mongol conquests in the 13th century

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Both networks spread Islam through merchant activity (not conquest), generated wealthy trading cities (Timbuktu, Kilwa), and connected regions with complementary goods. A is wrong — Chinese merchants dominated Indian Ocean segments, not trans-Saharan. B is wrong — monsoon winds apply only to the Indian Ocean. D is partially relevant for the Indian Ocean but not the trans-Saharan routes, which were not directly disrupted by the Mongols.

## SAQ Practice

**SAQ Prompt:**

> *"From Timbuktu I sailed down the Nile [Niger] on a small boat... The buying and selling of its inhabitants is done with cowry-shells, and the same is the case at Malli [the city of Mali]."*
> — Ibn Battuta (1304–1368)

**Part A**: Briefly explain ONE way in which this source is useful for understanding trade in West Africa in the 14th century.

**Part B**: Briefly explain ONE cause of the growth of trans-Saharan trade between c. 1200 and c. 1450.

**Part C**: Briefly explain ONE effect of trans-Saharan trade on West African societies during this period.

> [!tip] Model Responses
> **Part A**: Ibn Battuta's account provides firsthand evidence of commercial practices in Mali, including the use of cowrie shells as currency — evidence of West Africa's integration into a larger Indian Ocean exchange system (cowries came from the Indian Ocean). His description of river travel between cities gives historians evidence about West African infrastructure and urban commercial networks.
>
> **Part B**: The establishment and expansion of the Mali Empire in the early 1200s dramatically increased trans-Saharan commerce. By taxing virtually all goods entering West Africa, Mali created a stable commercial environment, protected trade routes with iron-armed cavalry, and used gold wealth to attract more traders. The arrival of Muslim merchants (who had better access to North African networks, horses, and books) also expanded the volume and sophistication of trans-Saharan exchange.
>
> **Part C**: Trans-Saharan trade transformed cities like Timbuktu from trading posts into major centers of Islamic scholarship. Mali's rulers, enriched by trade taxation, used their wealth to fund mosques, schools, and scholars — turning Timbuktu into one of the world's great intellectual cities by the 1400s. The spread of Islam through West Africa also connected the region to a broader Afro-Eurasian intellectual and cultural network.

## Comparison: Trans-Saharan vs. Indian Ocean Trade

| Dimension | Trans-Saharan | Indian Ocean |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|
| Primary goods | Gold, ivory, slaves northward; salt, horses, books southward | Spices, porcelain, textiles, gold, slaves |
| Transportation tech | Camels + load-bearing saddles | Dhows, junks, lateen sails, astrolabe |
| Environmental knowledge | Oasis locations, camel endurance | Monsoon wind patterns |
| How Islam spread | Through North African/Arab merchant networks | Through Arab/Persian merchant diasporas |
| Key trading cities | Timbuktu, Gao, Cairo | Calicut, Malacca, Kilwa, Hormuz |
| Scale | Regional (Africa + Mediterranean) | Intercontinental (Africa, Asia, Middle East) |

## Mansa Musa's Pilgrimage: Why It Matters

Think about what a single journey can reveal:

1. **Economic proof**: 100 camels each carrying 300 lbs of gold. Mali's wealth was real and staggering.
2. **Religious depth**: This was a *sincere* pilgrimage, not just a political display — Mansa Musa's faith drove his actions after returning (schools, mosques, scholarship sponsorship).
3. **Global connection**: The gold inflation crisis in Egypt shows that Mali was *integrated* into a global economy, not isolated. What happened in Timbuktu affected prices in Cairo.
4. **Legacy**: Timbuktu became a major center of Islamic scholarship. The religious institutions Mansa Musa built outlasted the Mali Empire itself.

> [!exam-tip]
> If you can write two paragraphs about Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage — explaining both its immediate effects (gold inflation) and its long-term consequences (Timbuktu as a center of learning) — you can answer almost any AP question about trans-Saharan trade or Islam in Africa.
