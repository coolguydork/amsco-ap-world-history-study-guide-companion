# Unit 2.4: Practice Questions — Trans-Saharan Trade Routes (c. 1200–1450)

> [!exam-tip]
> AP questions on this topic most often involve: (1) a primary source about Mansa Musa's hajj or Ibn Battuta's account of Mali; (2) a comparison between the Trans-Saharan and another trade network (Silk Roads or Indian Ocean); (3) a question about how Islam spread via commerce. Practice using the causation chain: complementary resources → trade → political power → Islamic conversion → scholarly culture.

---

## Multiple-Choice Questions

**Question 1**

*"He brought with him gold and provisions. He entered Cairo in a grand procession and stopped at the sultan's court. He distributed so much gold that the market price of gold fell in Egypt and has not recovered to this day — it has been twelve years since then, and the gold price is still depressed."*
— Al-Umari, Arab historian, c. 1337, describing Mansa Musa's hajj of 1324–1325

Which of the following conclusions is best supported by the passage?

A) The Mali Empire's wealth derived primarily from the exploitation of enslaved labor in goldfields
B) Mansa Musa's pilgrimage demonstrated the extraordinary wealth of West Africa generated by the trans-Saharan gold trade
C) The Islamic world's entire gold supply was dependent on West African sources by the 14th century
D) Mansa Musa's gift-giving caused a permanent and irreversible decline in Egyptian commercial prosperity

**Correct Answer: B**
*The passage's detail about gold prices falling for twelve years is direct evidence of the scale of West African gold production — the amount Mansa Musa distributed was enough to affect Mediterranean gold markets for over a decade. This supports the claim that trans-Saharan trade generated extraordinary wealth in Mali, not that Egypt was wholly dependent on West African gold or that the decline was permanent.*

**Question 2**

The gold-salt exchange at the heart of the Trans-Saharan trade is best understood as evidence of which of the following historical patterns?

A) West African kingdoms' military conquest of Saharan salt-mining regions
B) The Islamic world's deliberate strategy to control African commodity production
C) Complementary resource distribution as a driver of self-sustaining long-distance trade
D) The failure of West African states to develop indigenous salt-production technology

**Correct Answer: C**
*The gold-salt trade is the AP exam's primary example of complementary resources driving commerce. Neither gold nor salt could be produced in sufficient quantities in the same region — this asymmetry created a natural commercial relationship that persisted for centuries without requiring external pressure or military domination.*

**Question 3**

Which of the following best explains how Islam spread along the Trans-Saharan trade routes between c. 700 and 1450?

A) Islamic armies conquered West African kingdoms and forced rulers to convert
B) Muslim merchants established diasporic communities at trading hubs, and West African rulers converted for commercial and diplomatic advantages
C) Abbasid Caliphate missionaries built schools in West African cities funded by the central caliphate treasury
D) The Mongol Empire spread Islam along all trade routes under its control, including those crossing the Sahara

**Correct Answer: B**
*Islam spread in West Africa via the same merchant-diaspora model seen in the Indian Ocean network. Rulers converted for practical advantages — shared Islamic commercial law, access to Muslim trading partners, diplomatic credibility in the wider Islamic world — not under military compulsion. Conversion was typically elite and urban first, with rural populations maintaining indigenous practices.*

**Question 4**

The founding of the Mali Empire by Sundiata Keita (c. 1235) and its subsequent control of trans-Saharan trade routes most directly illustrates which of the following?

A) That military conquest of gold-producing regions was the only path to West African imperial wealth
B) That controlling the transit points of complementary-resource trade, rather than the production sites themselves, was the key to West African imperial power
C) That Islamic identity was a prerequisite for West African state-building in this period
D) That West African empires depended on North African military support to defend their trade routes

**Correct Answer: B**
*Mali's rulers taxed gold as it crossed their territory without directly mining it — a transit-taxation model inherited from Ghana. Control of the chokepoint between the gold-producing forests and the Saharan caravan routes was the source of Mali's wealth, not direct resource extraction.*

**Question 5**

Timbuktu's emergence as a major Islamic scholarly center in the 13th and 14th centuries is best explained by which of the following?

A) The Abbasid Caliphate's deliberate relocation of its administrative center to West Africa
B) The Mongol destruction of Baghdad (1258) forcing Islamic scholars to seek new patronage centers
C) Trans-Saharan trade wealth funding patronage of Islamic schools, mosques, and manuscript libraries
D) Mansa Musa's personal theological training, which attracted scholars to his court

**Correct Answer: C**
*Timbuktu's scholarly prominence was directly funded by trans-Saharan trade revenues — wealthy Mali rulers and merchants patronized Islamic educational institutions because they had the resources to do so. The Mongol sack of Baghdad did increase scholar mobility, but Timbuktu's rise was primarily a product of local wealth generated by trade.*

**Question 6**

Ibn Battuta's account of Mali (1352–1353) is most valuable to historians as:

A) An unbiased, objective account of West African political structures
B) A primary source reflecting an outside Muslim scholar's perspective on how an African Islamic society compared to his expectations
C) Evidence that Mali was the wealthiest state in the world by the 14th century
D) A diplomatic record created to facilitate trade negotiations between North Africa and Mali

**Correct Answer: B**
*Ibn Battuta was a Moroccan Muslim traveler whose account reveals as much about his expectations and cultural standards as about Mali itself — he praises Mali's security and Islamic observance while expressing surprise at practices (such as women's relative public freedom) that differed from North African norms. This makes his account a primary source with a particular perspective, not objective reporting.*

**Question 7**

The dromedary camel's role in Trans-Saharan trade is most analogous to which of the following in the Indian Ocean trade network?

A) The astrolabe
B) The dhow
C) The lateen sail
D) The compass

**Correct Answer: B**
*Both the dromedary camel and the dhow were the primary transportation technologies enabling their respective trade networks — without camels, regular Saharan crossings were impossible; without dhows, regular Indian Ocean crossings were impossible. Both are enabling technologies specific to their environmental contexts.*

**Question 8**

Which of the following best describes the relationship between Islam and political authority in the Mali Empire?

A) Mali's rulers were strictly orthodox Muslims who enforced Islamic law on the entire population
B) Islam was adopted by elites and merchants for practical advantages while rural populations often maintained indigenous practices — a pattern of religious syncretism
C) Islam was a foreign religion that Mali's rulers tolerated but did not personally practice
D) Mali's rulers used Islam to justify military conquest of non-Muslim neighbors across West Africa

**Correct Answer: B**
*The "elite Islam" pattern in Mali — urban and ruling-class conversion alongside rural maintenance of indigenous practices — is a key AP World History concept. Mansa Musa performed both Islamic prayers and traditional African rituals, reflecting a deliberate dual legitimacy strategy rather than strict Islamic orthodoxy.*

**Question 9**

*"The trans-Saharan trade declined in importance between 1450 and 1600 not because of any internal failure, but because of external disruption."*

Which of the following best supports this claim?

A) The spread of bubonic plague along trans-Saharan routes reduced the population of Sahelian cities
B) The Songhai Empire's conquest of Timbuktu disrupted the scholarly networks that sustained trade
C) Portuguese maritime exploration opened direct sea routes to West African gold, bypassing the Saharan overland routes
D) Mali's collapse was caused by Tuareg nomads cutting off northern trade connections

**Correct Answer: C**
*The most important external disruption to the trans-Saharan trade was Portuguese maritime contact with West Africa beginning in the 1440s–1480s, which offered European buyers a sea route to West African gold that bypassed the Saharan land routes and the North African middlemen who profited from them. This gradually redirected West African gold toward the Atlantic economy.*

**Question 10**

The Berber and Tuareg peoples of the Sahara are best described in the context of the trans-Saharan trade as:

A) Passive observers who neither participated in nor benefited from trans-Saharan commerce
B) Subjects of the Mali Empire who transported goods under state direction
C) Independent intermediaries who provided essential desert expertise and logistics, exercising their own commercial and political power
D) North African merchants who organized caravans from Mediterranean cities southward

**Correct Answer: C**
*Berber and Tuareg caravan leaders were not subjects of Mali or servants of North African merchants — they were independent specialists whose knowledge of desert routes, water sources, and logistics was irreplaceable. They levied their own tolls and maintained their own power in the deep Sahara, where neither Mali nor North African states could project authority.*

## Short Answer Questions (SAQ)

### SAQ 1

*"Between c. 1200 and 1450, the Trans-Saharan trade network played a central role in shaping the political, economic, and cultural development of West Africa."*

**a)** Identify ONE political consequence of trans-Saharan trade for a West African state in this period.

**b)** Identify ONE cultural consequence of trans-Saharan trade for West Africa in this period.

**c)** Explain how the relationship between commerce and Islam in West Africa differed from how Islam had spread in other regions (such as the Arabian Peninsula or Central Asia).

> [!tip]
> **Top-scoring response would include:**
> - (a) Trans-Saharan trade revenues allowed Mali rulers to fund armies and project imperial authority across a vast territory; or Mansa Musa's hajj used trade wealth to build diplomatic relationships with North African and Middle Eastern states
> - (b) Islam spread to elite and merchant classes via trade contacts, producing Timbuktu as a major Islamic scholarly center; or the Sankore Mosque-University's manuscript collections reflect trade-funded intellectual culture
> - (c) In West Africa, Islam spread primarily via commercial contact and voluntary conversion for practical advantages — not through military conquest (as in Arabia) or steppe empire patronage (as in Central Asia); this produced a syncretistic "elite Islam" where rulers balanced Islamic and indigenous religious practices

### SAQ 2

Use the following image description: *A page from the 1375 Catalan Atlas showing a crowned African ruler — labeled as Mansa Musa — seated on a throne and holding a large gold nugget, with a caravan of camels depicted approaching his kingdom from the northwest.*

**a)** Explain ONE way this image provides evidence about trans-Saharan trade in the period c. 1200–1450.

**b)** Explain ONE limitation of using this image as historical evidence about West Africa.

**c)** Explain ONE reason why Mansa Musa's hajj of 1324–1325 had such significant effects beyond West Africa.

> [!tip]
> **Top-scoring response would include:**
> - (a) The gold nugget and caravan imagery directly reflects the gold-salt trade and West Africa's role as a gold producer; the map's existence demonstrates that Mali's wealth was known in Europe via Islamic intermediaries
> - (b) The image was made by a European cartographer based on secondhand accounts — it may exaggerate, misrepresent, or reflect European/Mediterranean stereotypes about African wealth rather than Mali's actual political structure or culture
> - (c) Mansa Musa's distribution of gold in Egypt depressed gold prices across the Mediterranean for over a decade — a concrete economic disruption; his architectural patronage in Timbuktu (bringing back an Andalusian architect) physically transformed the city; his pilgrimage connected Mali to the broader Islamic scholarly world

## Document-Based Question (DBQ) Prompt

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which trans-Saharan trade shaped the development of West African states and societies in the period c. 1000–1450.

> [!tip]
> **Top-scoring DBQ outline would include:**
> - **Thesis:** A defensible claim about degree — e.g., "Trans-Saharan trade was the primary engine of West African state formation, driving both political centralization (Ghana, Mali, Songhai) and cultural transformation (spread of Islam, growth of Timbuktu), though indigenous political traditions and non-commercial factors also shaped West African development."
> - **Body paragraphs should address:**
>   - Political: Mali's transit-taxation model; how trade revenues funded armies and imperial administration
>   - Cultural/Religious: Islam's spread via merchant diasporas; Timbuktu's scholarly culture; religious syncretism
>   - Social: Griot class and oral tradition; social stratification around merchant wealth; trans-Saharan slave trade
>   - Limits of trade as sole explanation: Sundiata's military unification preceded full commercial dominance; indigenous spiritual authority remained significant alongside Islamic identity
> - **Complexity point:** Argue that trade shaped West Africa differently across regions — urban centers (Timbuktu) were deeply transformed, rural populations much less so; or argue that the trans-Saharan trade's long-term significance was its eventual disruption by Portuguese maritime contact, which reoriented West African gold toward the Atlantic economy
> - **HAPP analysis:** Consider Ibn Battuta (Muslim scholar, outside perspective, purpose: record travels for Islamic audience), Al-Umari (Arab historian using secondhand accounts, purpose: describe known world to Islamic readers), the Catalan Atlas (European cartographer, audience: European navigators, shows limits of European knowledge of Africa)
