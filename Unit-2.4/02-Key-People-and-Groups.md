# Unit 2.4: Key People and Groups — Exchange in the Americas, Africa, and Europe

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves Mansa Musa as a primary source stimulus — his pilgrimage is documented in Islamic sources and is excellent evidence of both trans-Saharan trade and African Muslim identity. Know the details of his 1324–1325 hajj.

---

## Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337)
**Time Period:** Early 14th century
**Region:** Mali Empire (West Africa) → North Africa → Mecca

- The most powerful ruler of the Mali Empire, controlling the gold-producing regions of West Africa and the trans-Saharan trade routes that brought that gold to North Africa and the Mediterranean
- His pilgrimage (*hajj*) to Mecca in 1324–1325 became legendary: he traveled with an enormous entourage (estimates range from 60,000 to 80,000 people) and distributed so much gold along the route through Cairo and the Mediterranean that he caused inflation in Egypt that lasted years
- His journey placed Mali on Islamic and European world maps — a Catalan atlas of 1375 depicts him on his throne, holding a gold nugget, demonstrating how his pilgrimage reshaped European geographic knowledge of Africa

## Sundiata Keita (r. c. 1235–1255)
**Time Period:** Mid-13th century
**Region:** West Africa (founder of Mali Empire)

- The legendary founder of the Mali Empire who overthrew the Sosso king Sumanguru Kante and established Mali's dominance over the former Ghana Empire's gold trade
- His story is preserved in the *Epic of Sundiata*, an oral epic maintained by griots — primary evidence for both the historical events and the cultural importance of oral tradition in West Africa
- His state-building used military conquest to control trade routes — a model of how African rulers built empires through command of commerce rather than just territory

## Venetian and Genoese Merchants
**Time Period:** 12th–15th centuries
**Region:** Northern Italy → Mediterranean → Black Sea → Central Asia

- Italian city-state merchants were the primary European participants in long-distance luxury trade, functioning as the western terminal of Silk Road goods reaching Europe
- Venice built its commercial empire on monopoly control of Byzantine trade routes; Genoa built its network around the Black Sea, establishing Caffa (in Crimea) as its key eastern outpost — the city where the Black Death entered Europe
- Their commercial innovations — double-entry bookkeeping, marine insurance, letters of credit, banking — transformed European commercial culture and created the financial infrastructure that would eventually fund the Age of Exploration

## The Aztec (Mexica) Empire and Pochteca Merchants
**Time Period:** c. 1300–1521
**Region:** Mesoamerica (central Mexico)

- The Aztec Empire was the dominant political and commercial power of central Mexico in the period c. 1300–1450, with Tenochtitlán growing into one of the largest cities in the world
- The *pochteca* — professional long-distance merchants organized in hereditary guilds — traveled far beyond Aztec political borders trading luxury goods (jade, cacao, quetzal feathers, obsidian) and reporting back intelligence to the state
- The Aztec market at Tlatelolco (sister city of Tenochtitlán) impressed Spanish conquistadors who compared it favorably to the markets of Seville — evidence of sophisticated commercial culture in the Americas independent of any Old World contact

## The Inca (Tawantinsuyu) State
**Time Period:** c. 1400–1532
**Region:** Andean South America (modern Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, Chile, Argentina)

- The Inca Empire, consolidated under rulers like Pachacuti (r. 1438–1471), was the largest state in the Americas at the time of European contact, extending 2,500 miles along the Andes
- The Inca did not use a market-based commercial economy — instead, the state extracted labor (*mit'a*) and redistributed goods through state storehouses (*qollqa*) along a massive road network; this was one of history's most sophisticated non-market redistributive economic systems
- The Inca road system (~25,000 miles) was the infrastructure enabling this redistribution; runners (*chasquis*) could relay messages from one end of the empire to the other in days — a communication system that rivaled the Mongol yam

## Cahokia and the Mississippian People
**Time Period:** c. 900–1400
**Region:** Eastern North America (modern Illinois, surrounding regions)

- Cahokia (near modern St. Louis) was the largest pre-Columbian city north of Mexico; population estimates at its peak (c. 1100 CE) range from about 10,000 to 20,000 — comparable in scale to many European cities of the same era, though the specific comparison to London depends on which estimates you use for both cities
- The Mississippian culture traded widely along river networks, exchanging copper, shells, obsidian, and ceremonial objects across vast distances; evidence of this trade network is found in archaeological sites from the Gulf Coast to the Great Lakes
- Cahokia's decline (c. 1300–1400) is debated — possible causes include environmental degradation, political instability, and the collapse of trade relationships; its decline before European contact illustrates that American civilizations also experienced rise and fall independent of any external influence

## European Peasants and Survivors of the Black Death
**Time Period:** Mid-to-late 14th century
**Region:** Europe broadly

- Often overlooked as historical actors: the European peasants and laborers who survived the Black Death found themselves in a dramatically changed labor market — with so many workers dead, survivors could demand higher wages and better conditions
- In England, this led to the Peasants' Revolt (1381); in France, to the Jacquerie (1358) — evidence that the Black Death's unintended consequence was to give unprecedented bargaining power to the laboring poor
- This social disruption — an unintended consequence of Silk Road trade network connectivity — is one of the most important examples of how commercial networks created social change in unexpected ways
