# People, States & Movements — Trans-Saharan Trade Routes

---

## Key People

**Sundiata Keita (r. c. 1235–c. 1255)**
Founder of the Mali Empire, known as "the Lion Prince." His father had ruled a small kingdom in present-day Guinea; when his father died, rivals invaded, killed most of the royal family, and captured the throne. They spared the young Sundiata because his disability made him seem non-threatening. He proved them wrong — he learned to fight, gathered allies during exile, returned in 1235, defeated his enemies, and reclaimed the throne. Sundiata is believed to have been Muslim and used his faith to build trade relationships with North African and Arab merchants, cultivating a thriving gold trade. His personal story became legend within Mali. *(AMSCO pp. 105–106)*

**Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337)**
The most famous ruler of Mali, grand-nephew of Sundiata. Known primarily for his religious devotion and his spectacular 1324 hajj to Mecca, which displayed Mali's wealth to the entire Afro-Eurasian world. His caravan included 100 camels, thousands of enslaved people and soldiers, and enormous quantities of gold distributed freely to hosts along the route. The influx of gold into Middle Eastern markets reportedly caused inflation in Egypt. On his return, Mansa Musa built mosques in Muslim trading cities and established religious schools in Timbuktu. Though most West Africans retained traditional religious beliefs, his reign deepened Islam's institutional presence in Mali. Mali declined within a century of his death. *(AMSCO pp. 106–107)*

> [!exam-tip]
> Mansa Musa's hajj (1324) is one of the most frequently tested events in AP World History — it appears in MCQ stimulus sources, SAQ prompts, and DBQ documents. Know: (1) what it demonstrated about Mali's wealth, (2) its effect on Mediterranean gold markets, and (3) what he built on returning (Islamic institutions in Timbuktu).

**Ibn Battuta (1304–1353)**
Moroccan Muslim scholar and the most prolific traveler of the pre-modern world. His written accounts documented trans-Saharan trade, Mali society, and commerce along the Niger River in vivid detail. He is cited in AMSCO's opening for Topic 2.4 as a firsthand source describing trade at Mali's cities. His travelogue is a primary source frequently used in AP exam document-based questions. *(AMSCO p. 103)*

**Al-Bakri (c. 1014–1094)**
An 11th-century Arab geographer from Al-Andalus (Spain) who documented the Ghana Empire's gold trade without ever visiting West Africa himself — he compiled descriptions from merchants and travelers. His work is one of the earliest written records of West African commercial and political life.

## Key States & Empires

**Ghana Empire (c. 5th century–c. 1100s CE)**
The first major West African state to profit systematically from trans-Saharan gold. Ghana sat at a strategic middle position on the trade routes, taxing goods passing through its territory. By c. 800 CE, its gold had become famous throughout Europe and Asia. Ghana used military forces equipped with horses and iron weapons — purchased with tax revenue — to expand and protect its commercial interests. Wars with neighboring societies permanently weakened Ghana by the 12th century. *(See [[Unit-1.5/Africa Overview|Africa Overview]])*

**Mali Empire (founded 1235; peak c. 1300–1400)**
West Africa's dominant power during the high period of trans-Saharan trade. Founded by Sundiata Keita, Mali not only controlled the gold trade but taxed virtually all trade entering West Africa — a revenue base larger than Ghana's. Major cities Timbuktu and Gao accumulated enormous wealth and became centers of Muslim intellectual life. The empire's currency system (cowrie shells, gold dust, salt, cloth) enabled commerce across a vast territory. Mali declined in the late 1400s and was superseded by Songhai. *(AMSCO pp. 105–107)*

**Songhai Empire (dominant after 1468)**
The successor empire to Mali in West Africa. Sunni Ali conquered Timbuktu in 1468, making Songhai the new regional power. Following the same processes as Mali — controlling trade routes, taxing commerce, expanding militarily — Songhai became larger and richer than Mali. Timbuktu remained an intellectual and commercial center under Songhai. *(AMSCO p. 107)*

## Comparison: West African and Other 13th-Century Empires

The AMSCO textbook situates Mali alongside other contemporary empires, highlighting that large-scale political organization and commercial wealth were a global phenomenon in this period. *(AMSCO p. 107)*

| Empire | Location | Peak Years | Major City | Key Legacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mali** | West Africa | 1200s–1400s | Timbuktu | Connected West & North Africa; spread Islam |
| **Al-Andalus** | Spain | 711–1492 | Córdoba | Preserved Greek learning; fostered cross-cultural trade |
| **Byzantine Empire** | Middle East | 330–1453 | Constantinople | Carried on Roman legacy; linked Asia, Europe, Africa |
| **Kievan Rus** | Russia | 900s–1200s | Kiev | First large civilization in Russia; spread Christianity east |

> [!tip]
> The AP exam loves this kind of comparison — similar time period, different regions, different legacies. Mali's comparative advantage: gold wealth and Islamic scholarship. Its weakness: succession crises and external military pressure eventually gave way to Songhai.

## Religious Movement: Islam into West Africa

Islam did not arrive in West Africa through conquest — it came via the trade routes. North African Muslim merchants introduced Islam to the Mali region in the 9th century, generations before Sundiata founded the empire. By the 14th century, Mali's ruling class and merchant community were Muslim, giving them access to the broader networks of Islamic commerce from Spain to Central Asia. Mansa Musa's patronage of mosques and schools institutionalized Islam in West Africa in ways that outlasted his empire. *(AMSCO pp. 103, 106)*
