# Topic 2.4 — Key Terms and People

## Key People

**Sundiata (the "Lion Prince," r. c. 1235–1255)** — Founder of the Mali Empire. Crippled as a child and exiled, he returned to defeat his enemies in 1235 and reclaim the throne. His story — of the underestimated warrior returning from exile — made him a legendary figure. Used Muslim connections to establish trade relationships with North African merchants; cultivated Mali's gold trade. AMSCO describes his story as one of becoming beloved through adversity and astute leadership.

**Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337)** — Grand-nephew of Sundiata; most famous ruler of Mali. His 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca — with 100 gold-laden camels and thousands of attendants — displayed Mali's wealth to the world and caused gold inflation in Egypt. Upon returning, he established schools and mosques in Timbuktu, deepening Islam's presence in West Africa. One of the wealthiest individuals in human history.

**Ibn Battuta (1304–1368/1377)** — Muslim scholar and traveler from Morocco (also covered in Topic 2.5). Traveled to Mali, among many other places, providing firsthand accounts of West African cities and trade. His quote about traveling from Timbuktu by boat on the Niger River opens the AMSCO chapter on trans-Saharan trade.

## Key Places

**Mali** — West African empire (c. 1235–c. 1400s) stretching across much of modern Mali, Senegal, and Guinea. Founded by Sundiata; reached its peak under Mansa Musa. Based on control of trans-Saharan gold trade and taxation of all goods entering West Africa.

**Timbuktu** — City on the Niger River bend; part of the Mali (and later Songhai) Empire. One of the world's great centers of Islamic scholarship by the 1400s. By the 1500s, books produced and sold in Timbuktu fetched higher prices than most other goods. (AMSCO p. 105)

**Songhai Kingdom** — Succeeded Mali as the dominant West African power by the late 1400s. Larger and richer than Mali; followed the same pattern of trade-based wealth and Islamic scholarship.

**Sahara Desert** — 3.6 million square miles; about the size of China. Contains only about 800 square miles of oases. The world's largest hot desert — and for centuries, the highway connecting Sub-Saharan Africa to the Mediterranean world via camel caravan.

**Arabian Desert** — The homeland of camels; the origin point of the animal technology that made trans-Saharan trade possible.

**Mecca** — Islam's holiest city (in modern Saudi Arabia). The destination of the *hajj* (pilgrimage) that every Muslim is expected to make. Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage here is the most famous event in trans-Saharan history.

## Technology

**Camel saddle** — Multiple varieties existed for different purposes; the Somali load-bearing saddle (carrying up to 600 lbs) was the most significant for trade. Without this design, camels could not serve as long-distance cargo animals.

**Trans-Saharan trade routes** — The network of overland caravan routes crossing the Sahara; seven north-south corridors plus two east-west routes. Connected Mediterranean North Africa to gold-rich sub-Saharan kingdoms.

---

## Economics

**Trans-Saharan trade** — The exchange system across the Sahara Desert: gold, ivory, hides, and slaves moved north; salt, textiles, horses, and books moved south. Operated for over 700 years; made West African kingdoms extraordinarily wealthy.

**Cowrie shells** — Currency used in Mali and some other West African states; small shells from the Indian Ocean that served as standardized exchange tokens. Evidence of how the Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan networks were indirectly connected.

**Oases** — Watered areas within the desert where underground water surfaces, making settlement possible. Critical rest stops for caravans crossing the Sahara; the "caravanserai of the Sahara."

## Comparing West African Empires

| Empire | Dates | Key Figure | Basis of Wealth | Key Legacy |
|--------|-------|-----------|----------------|-----------|
| Ghana | c. 700–1200 | — | Gold trade taxation | First great West African kingdom |
| Mali | c. 1235–1400s | Sundiata, Mansa Musa | Gold + trade taxation | Timbuktu; Islam; 1324 pilgrimage |
| Songhai | c. 1400s–1591 | Sunni Ali, Askia Muhammad | Gold + salt | Succeeded Mali; larger territory |

> [!tip]
> On comparison questions, the key difference between Ghana, Mali, and Songhai is *not* their trade goods (gold was always central) but their *political structures* and their *relationship to Islam*. Mali is the most deeply Islamic of the three; Songhai continued this pattern.

## Common Misconception

> [!warning]
> Modern Ghana (the country) is not located where the ancient Ghana Empire was. The Ghana Empire was in modern Mauritania and southwestern Mali — west of modern Ghana. The country was named "Ghana" in 1957 as a tribute to the ancient empire, but geographically they are different places. This trips up students frequently.
