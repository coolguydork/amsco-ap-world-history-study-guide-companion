# Topic 2.4 — Trans-Saharan Trade Routes

> [!summary] Essential Question
> What were the **causes and effects** of Trans-Saharan trade, and how did the growth of empires influence trade and communication?

## Context: The Sahara as a Highway, Not a Barrier

The Sahara Desert covers 3.6 million square miles — roughly the size of China. Only about 800 square miles of it are oases where agriculture is possible. For centuries, it seemed like an impenetrable wall between North Africa and Sub-Saharan Africa.

But it wasn't a wall. It was a highway — once you had the right technology.

That technology was the **camel**, carrying loads across a landscape of sand and rock that would have defeated horses and donkeys. When **Muslim merchants** from Southwest Asia arrived with camels and a commercial network spanning from Spain to Persia, they transformed the Sahara from an obstacle into a connector.

---

## The Technology of Trans-Saharan Trade: The Camel

Muslim merchants from Southwest Asia rode camels that had been domesticated on the Arabian Peninsula. Camels began to appear in North Africa after ~300 CE and gradually replaced horses and donkeys for desert travel.

**Why camels worked:**
- Could drink 50+ gallons at one time and go days without more water
- Tolerated extreme desert heat and cold
- Could eat thorny plants and salty water found in deserts
- Could not be controlled with a bit — but could be loaded heavily

**The key saddle innovation**: The Somali load-bearing saddle allowed camels to carry **up to 600 pounds**. This single design breakthrough transformed camels from riding animals into viable pack animals for long-distance trade. Without it, trans-Saharan commerce at scale was impossible.

Caravans crossing the Sahara could have thousands of camels — loaded with trade goods plus enough fresh water to survive between oases. The people leading the caravans generally walked the entire way.

---

## The Trans-Saharan Trade System

The routes consisted of **seven north-south trade corridors** and **two east-west routes**, connecting:

- North Africa and the Mediterranean world (Arab and Berber merchants)
- The Sahara oases (resting points)
- Sub-Saharan West Africa (gold kingdoms)

**North brought south**: salt, textiles, cloth, horses, books, paper
**South brought north**: gold, ivory, hides (leather), slaves

**Gold** was the most precious commodity. West African merchants acquired it from the waters of the Senegal River (near modern Senegal and Mauritania). Foreign traders came specifically for gold — and also for ivory and slaves. For more than 700 years, trans-Saharan trade brought considerable wealth to West African kingdoms, particularly **Ghana** and **Mali**.

**Islam traveled with the merchants.** As North African and Arab traders arrived in West Africa, they brought their faith. Islam spread into Sub-Saharan Africa not through conquest but through commercial relationships and the cultural prestige of literacy and learning.

## West African Empires: Ghana, Mali, and Songhai

### The Ghana Empire (c. 700s–1200s)

The Ghana Empire (in modern Mauritania/Mali — not modern Ghana) was the first great West African trading kingdom. By the 12th century, wars with neighboring societies had permanently weakened it. From its ruins arose several new trading societies — the most powerful of which was **Mali**.

### The Mali Empire (c. 1235–c. 1400s)

**[[Concept Notes/Mali Empire|Mali]]** became the dominant power in West Africa, built on gold wealth and trade taxation.

**Founding: Sundiata (Lion Prince)**

Mali's founding ruler was **Sundiata** — one of history's most compelling origin stories:

His father had ruled a small society in what is today Guinea. When his father died, rival groups invaded, killing most of the royal family and capturing the throne. They *didn't* kill the young Sundiata because he was crippled and seemed harmless. Despite his disability, he trained as a warrior. His enemies forced him into exile — but exile only strengthened him and built his network of allies.

In **1235**, Sundiata returned, defeated his enemies, and reclaimed the throne. His story — of the underestimated outsider who returned triumphant — made him beloved and legendary.

Sundiata was (probably) Muslim and used his faith to build trade relationships with North African and Arab merchants. Under his leadership, Mali's gold trade flourished.

**Mansa Musa and the 1324 Pilgrimage**

In the 14th century, Sundiata's grand-nephew **[[Mansa Musa]]** brought Mali to global attention.

A deeply devout Muslim, Mansa Musa undertook a *hajj* (pilgrimage to Mecca) in **1324** — but no ordinary pilgrimage:
- **100 camels** each carrying 300 pounds of gold
- **Thousands of slaves and soldiers**
- Gold distributed to everyone who hosted him along the way

His procession passed through Cairo and caused a **gold inflation crisis** that reportedly depressed Egyptian gold prices for a decade. The world suddenly knew that sub-Saharan Africa had gold — a lot of it.

Upon returning, Mansa Musa:
- Established religious schools in **Timbuktu**
- Built mosques in Muslim trading cities
- Sponsored scholars who wished to study further

**Timbuktu** became one of the world's great centers of Islamic learning. By the 1500s, books produced and sold in Timbuktu fetched higher prices than most other goods.

> [!exam-tip]
> Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage is one of the most frequently tested events in Unit 2. It demonstrates: (1) Mali's extraordinary wealth, (2) the connection between trans-Saharan trade and Islam, and (3) how a single act can have global economic ripple effects. Know it in detail.

**Mali's Decline**

Within 100 years of Mansa Musa's death, the Mali kingdom was declining. By the late 1400s, the **Songhai Kingdom** had taken its place as the West African powerhouse — larger and richer than Mali, following similar patterns of trade taxation and Islamic scholarship.

### The Role of States in Trade

The growth of trans-Saharan trade created a need for states to administer it:
- **Currency**: Mali used cowrie shells, cotton cloth, gold, glass beads, and salt as currency
- **Protection**: armies with horses and iron weapons (bought with tax revenue) protected trade routes
- **Taxation**: nearly all goods entering West Africa were taxed
- **Expansion**: empires expanded to control gold and salt sources directly

## Islam's Cultural Impact on West Africa

| Effect | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| Scholarship | Timbuktu became a world-class center of Islamic learning |
| Architecture | Mosques built in trading cities; new architectural styles |
| Language | Arabic literacy spread among elites and merchants |
| Governance | Islamic legal concepts influenced Mali's administration |
| Pilgrimage | Leaders like Mansa Musa demonstrated connection to the wider Islamic world |

---

## Continuity and Change

**Continuity**: The basic north-south commodity flow (gold/ivory south-to-north; salt/horses north-to-south) persisted for over 700 years. West Africa's role as a gold source was consistent from the Ghana Empire through Songhai.

**Change**: The arrival of Islam transformed the *cultural character* of trans-Saharan trade. By 1200, cities like Timbuktu were not just trading posts but Islamic scholarly centers. State structures grew more sophisticated to manage and tax the expanding trade.
