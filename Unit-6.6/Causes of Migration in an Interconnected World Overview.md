# Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World

**AMSCO pp. 417–428 | AP Period 3 | Themes: MIG, ECN, SIO | Skill: Causation**

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 6.6 focuses on the **causes** of migration — push and pull factors, labor systems, and the role of industrialization. The AP exam loves the coerced vs. voluntary distinction and asks about specific groups: Irish, Chinese, Indian, Italian, and Japanese migrants. Always connect migration to the demand for cheap labor left by the abolition of the slave trade.

## Essential Question

*How did environmental and economic factors contribute to patterns of migration between 1750 and 1900?*

---

## Context: A New Era of Migration

An increasingly global economy characterized by [[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview|economic imperialism]] and improvements in transportation created a new era of mass migration after 1750. As industrialization expanded, populations shifted from countryside to cities and from one continent to another. Some migrants traveled voluntarily — driven by push factors like poverty, famine, and persecution, and pull factors like available land and higher wages. Others were coerced or semi-coerced through slavery, indenture, or penal transportation.

The core AP argument: **the abolition of the transatlantic slave trade created a labor vacuum that colonial powers filled with new coerced and semi-coerced labor systems** — especially indentured servitude and contract labor from India and China.

---

## Migration through Labor Systems

### Slavery and Its Decline
Most nations in the Americas abolished the African slave trade in the early 19th century, but slavery itself continued longer. The last holdouts were the United States (1865), Cuba (1886), and Brazil (1888). As slavery declined, demand for plantation labor did not — imperial powers turned to replacement systems.

### Indentured Servitude
[[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Indentured servants]] worked for a fixed term (typically five to seven years) in exchange for passage, room, and board, after which they were legally free. Many were desperately poor and signed contracts voluntarily; others were pressured or deceived. Indian indentured workers were sent to Mauritius (from 1834), British Guiana, Dutch Guiana, Natal, Fiji, Trinidad, and other Caribbean islands. Over 1.5 million Indians were shipped to colonies before Britain abolished the indenture system in 1916. Indentured Indian workers permanently transformed the demographics of Mauritius, Fiji, and Trinidad.

> [!tip]
> Indenture is often called "a new form of slavery" on the AP exam. Know that it replaced enslaved labor after abolition in the British Empire (1833) — and that it created Indian diaspora communities across the Caribbean and Indian Ocean world.

### Asian Contract Laborers
Chinese and Indian workers were recruited — often by force or trickery — as the earliest substitutes for enslaved labor. Britain sent 200 Chinese to Trinidad as early as 1806. Between roughly the 1840s and 1870s, 250,000–500,000 Chinese workers arrived in British, French, Dutch, and Spanish colonies. About 125,000 went to Cuba alone, where 80% worked the sugar plantations. Contract workers lived with exploitative conditions, subsistence wages, and no legal protections — the press of the era described the system as "a new form of slavery." International pressure ended the contracts: Britain stopped in 1855, the US banned the trade in 1862, and a China-Spain treaty in 1877 freed remaining workers in Cuba.

In Southeast Asia two particular systems operated between 1880 and 1938: the **kangani** system (Ceylon and Malaya), where foremen recruited from their own extended families, and the **maistry** system (Burma), with defined hierarchies sending workers to plantations under highly exploitative conditions.

### Penal Colonies
Britain established a penal colony in Australia in 1788 after losing its original one in Georgia during the American Revolution. Convicts from England, Scotland, Ireland, and India were transported there for terms of hard labor. Actual imprisonment was rare — most worked for free settlers or on government road and railway projects. By 1850, Britain ended convict transportation to Australia (a stay there was no longer considered a punishment), and Australia became a major free-settler colony, especially after gold was discovered in 1851. France maintained similar penal colonies in New Caledonia and French Guiana (Devil's Island), using them for both criminals and political prisoners until well into the 20th century.

## Migration in the Face of Challenges

### India
Poverty drove most Indian emigration. Indentured laborers began arriving in Mauritius in 1834, and by 1880 were present across the Caribbean, Africa, Fiji, and Southeast Asia. Many signed five-year contracts, renewed them, and eventually settled permanently.

### China
The Chinese diaspora accelerated from the mid-19th century onward. Push factors included the poverty and disorder of the [[Concept Notes/Opium Trade|Opium Wars]] and the devastating [[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview|Taiping Rebellion]] (1850–1864), which killed over 20 million people. Pull factors included the California, South Australian, and western Canada gold rushes (1850s) and demand for construction labor on the US Transcontinental Railroad. The vast majority of Chinese emigrants were male peasants who intended to return home after earning money abroad.

### Ireland
The [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Great Famine]] of 1845–1852, caused by potato blight and worsened by British colonial policies (repeal of the Corn Laws, continued food exports), killed roughly 1 million Irish and drove over a million to emigrate immediately. Most went to the United States; many others went to Britain, Canada, or Australia. Emigration continued for decades after the famine ended — as many people left in the four years after the famine as during its peak. Push factors also included British abolition of the Irish Parliament (1801) and religious discrimination against Catholics and Protestant dissenters.

### Italy
Italian emigration followed the unification of Italy in 1861 and continued through 1900. Over 4 million Italians left during those decades, driven primarily by poverty, land subdivision across generations, and organized crime in the south. More than half went to other European countries; most of the rest went to North and South America. Italian workers in Argentina often returned home after earning money — an early example of **sojourner migration** enabled by improved steamship travel.

## Migration to Settler Colonies

### The British Settler Empire
British citizens emigrated in large numbers to "settler colonies" — Canada, South Africa, Australia, New Zealand — intending permanent residence. Others went to non-settler colonies (India, Malaya, Kenya) temporarily as soldiers, administrators in the **Colonial Service**, plantation managers, engineers, or geologists. British technical experts like Andrew Geddes Bain (Cape Town, 1816) spread Western science and technology, and were numerous enough in the colonies to form their own kind of diaspora.

Britain invested more in Argentina than in India during the 19th century. British businessmen, bankers, and engineers founded banks, built railroads, and developed export trade in Argentine agricultural products — a textbook example of [[Unit-6.5/Economic Imperialism Overview|informal imperialism]].

### Japan
Before the Meiji Restoration (1868), Japan was closed to the world. By 1893 the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration\|Meiji]] government had established the **Colonization Society** to export Japan's surplus population alongside commercial goods. Early attempts at a settler colony in Mexico failed; more successfully, Japan sent contract workers to Hawaii's sugar plantations starting in 1885 under a formal government agreement, with approximately 29,000 Japanese workers arriving in Hawaii over the next nine years. Thousands more went to Australia, New Caledonia, and Fiji. Anti-Japanese sentiment in the US led to the **Gentlemen's Agreement of 1907**, an informal US-Japan arrangement limiting further emigration; it was ultimately ended by the Immigration Act of 1924.

---

## Migration, Transportation, and Urbanization

Improvements in transportation — especially the steamship — changed the nature of migration. Workers could now travel with reasonable speed and even return home after their contracts ended, enabling sojourner migration patterns. Since most industry was located in urban areas, both internal and external migrants tended to settle in cities, which grew in size and influence around the globe. Urbanization and migration thus reinforced each other as twin consequences of industrialization.

## AP Exam Tips

> [!exam-tip]
> **The big distinction**: Voluntary migration (push/pull) vs. coerced/semi-coerced migration (slavery, indenture, contract labor, convict transportation). The AP exam often asks you to categorize migrants and explain the **causes** — not just list where people went.

> [!warning]
> Don't say slavery "ended" in the 19th century. It ended gradually and unevenly — the US (1865), Cuba (1886), Brazil (1888). On the AP, this matters because the **continuation** of slavery is why contract and indentured labor became the primary replacement systems.

> [!tip]
> **CCOT angle**: Labor systems change — from enslaved Africans → indentured Indians and Chinese → free wage labor — but the underlying goal (cheap labor for colonial agriculture) remains constant. That continuity is excellent evidence for CCOT essays.
