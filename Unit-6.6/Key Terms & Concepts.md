# Key Terms & Concepts

**AMSCO pp. 417–428 | AP Period 3 | Themes: MIG, ECN, SIO**

---

**slavery**
System of coerced labor in which people are legally owned as property. Though most of the Americas abolished the slave *trade* in the early 19th century, slavery itself persisted — US until 1865, Cuba until 1886, Brazil until 1888. Declining slavery created a labor vacuum that colonial powers filled with indenture and contract labor. In spite of abolition in the Americas, Africans continued enslaving one another into the 20th century.

**indentured servants**
Workers bound by contract to labor for a fixed term (typically five years) in exchange for passage, food, and lodging, after which they were legally free. Used extensively in British colonies to replace enslaved labor after 1833. Indian and Chinese workers under indenture shaped the demographics of Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad, and other colonies. Many intended to return home after their term but ultimately settled permanently.

> [!tip]
> Indenture vs. slavery: indentured servants were not property and had a path to freedom — but exploitation was severe. The AP exam expects you to know this distinction and identify indenture as a **semi-coerced** replacement for slavery.

**contract laborers**
Workers — mostly Chinese and Indian — recruited or coerced under labor contracts to work in colonial enterprises (plantations, railroads, mines). Often worked for subsistence wages under brutal conditions. Criticized by the contemporary press as "a new form of slavery." Chinese contract workers were sent to Cuba, Hawaii, California, and Southeast Asia; India's contract workers went to Mauritius, Fiji, British Guiana, and Natal.

**Colonial Service**
The British government's overseas administrative bureaucracy. Educated British men served as district officers, commissioners, and judges in India, Africa, and Malaya. Migration into the Colonial Service was voluntary and driven by career opportunity and imperial ideology — a classic example of pull-factor migration by the professional class.

**Colonization Society**
Japanese government organization established in 1893 to export Japan's surplus population and commercial goods overseas. Early attempt to establish an agricultural colony in Mexico failed; more successfully directed Japanese contract workers to Peru (790 workers, 1899) and facilitated labor migration to Hawaii's sugar plantations. Reflects Japan's late-19th-century drive to acquire overseas influence and relieve rural population pressure.

**penal colony**
Settlement established to house convicted criminals transported from their home country as punishment. Britain designated Australia as a penal colony in 1788 after losing the American colonies. Most convicts worked for free settlers or on government projects rather than being imprisoned. Britain ended convict transportation to Australia by 1850. France maintained penal colonies in New Caledonia and French Guiana (Devil's Island) until the mid-20th century.

**convicts**
Criminals transported from Britain and its colonies (including India) to penal colonies, primarily in Australia. Over 160,000 convicts were transported to Australia between 1788 and 1868. Actual imprisonment was rare — convicts typically worked for free settlers or on government road and rail projects. Most stayed after earning freedom because return passage was expensive. Represents a coerced migration pattern driven entirely by colonial penal policy.

**diaspora**
Dispersed population living outside their homeland while maintaining cultural identity. Major diasporas of the 19th century include the Chinese diaspora (Southeast Asia, Americas, Australia), the Indian diaspora (Caribbean, Africa, Oceania), the Irish diaspora (US, Britain, Canada, Australia), and the Italian diaspora (Argentina, Europe). The African diaspora — involuntary emigration of millions through the slave trade — was one of the largest in history.

> [!exam-tip]
> "Diaspora" is an AP exam favorite. Know at least two specific examples: the Indian diaspora (indentured workers who reshaped demographics in Fiji, Mauritius, Trinidad) and the Irish diaspora (driven by the Great Famine). Diaspora = dispersal + maintained cultural identity.

**emigrate**
To leave one's home country to settle in another. Driven by push factors (poverty, famine, political persecution, overpopulation, religious discrimination) and pull factors (economic opportunity, available land, higher wages, freedom). Between 1815 and 1914, 55–65 million Europeans emigrated — the largest voluntary migration in world history. Key groups: Irish (to US), Italians (to Argentina and Europe), British (to settler colonies), Chinese (to Southeast Asia and Americas), Japanese (to Hawaii and Pacific).

**Great Famine**
Irish famine of 1845–1852 caused by a potato blight, with mass death worsened by British colonial policies — continued export of food from Ireland and the repeal of the Corn Laws that had protected grain prices. Killed roughly 1 million people and drove over a million to emigrate immediately, with millions more leaving in the following decades. The most dramatic push-factor migration of the 19th century. The Irish diaspora permanently transformed the demographics of the United States, Britain, Canada, and Australia.

> [!warning]
> The Great Famine was not just a natural disaster. The AP exam may ask about the **role of British colonial policy** — continued food exports during starvation, eviction of tenant farmers after the Corn Laws repeal — as a contributing cause. Frame it as both environmental (blight) and political (colonial exploitation).

**[[Concept Notes/Taiping Rebellion\|Taiping Rebellion]]**
Massive Chinese civil war (1850–1864) led by Hong Xiuquan against the [[Concept Notes/Qing Dynasty\|Qing Dynasty]]; estimated 20+ million deaths. The chaos and poverty it caused pushed large numbers of Chinese peasants to emigrate to the Americas, Australia, and Southeast Asia. A key push factor for the Chinese diaspora.
