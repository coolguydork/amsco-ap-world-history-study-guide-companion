## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the map "Movements of Free and Enslaved People, 1750–1900" (AMSCO p. 426), which shows five numbered migration routes: (1) British to Australia, (2) Indians to Mauritius and Caribbean, (3) Chinese to Southeast Asia, (4) Africans (enslaved), (5) Japanese to North America.*

---

**Question 1.** Which movement included forced migrants before about 1850?

A. 1: British to Australia
B. 3: Indians to Southeast Asia
C. 4: Chinese to Southeast Asia
D. 5: Japanese to North America

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> British convicts were forcibly transported to Australia beginning in 1788 — a coerced migration pattern that predates 1850. The African slave trade (Movement 4) also involved forced migration, but option C labels it as Chinese, not African. Choice A is the only option that correctly identifies a forced pre-1850 migration. Indian and Japanese movements were primarily voluntary or semi-coerced but not forced in the same way.

---

**Question 2.** Indian migrations to places such as Mauritius and Caribbean islands led most directly to which long-term effect?

A. The abolition movement and eventual ending of the slave trade network across the Atlantic Ocean
B. The infusion of Indian cultural expressions, such as methods of food preparation, into local traditions
C. The development of nationalism and movements for self-determination in places that received Indian immigrants
D. The intensification of Indian efforts to create multinational corporations by establishing sugar plantations in these areas

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Indian indentured workers brought their home cultures — foods, religious practices, languages — to Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad, and other destinations, permanently reshaping local traditions. Choice A is incorrect (abolition preceded Indian indenture, not the other way around). Choice C overstates the political effect; Indian diaspora nationalism is a later development. Choice D is anachronistic; Indian workers were laborers, not corporate investors.

---

**Question 3.** Which best describes a global change between 1750 and 1900 that resulted from the movements shown on the map?

A. Many countries became more ethnically diverse.
B. The percentage of people who died in the same country where they were born increased.
C. The influence of Europeans overseas decreased.
D. The percentage of people enslaved increased.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Mass migration — both voluntary and coerced — introduced new ethnic and cultural communities into receiving societies worldwide. Australia became British, Irish, and Chinese; Trinidad became Indian and African; California became Chinese and European. Choice B is the opposite of what happened. Choice C is wrong — European imperial influence expanded in this period. Choice D is wrong — slavery was declining and eventually abolished.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Question 1 refers to the following passage:*

> "Due to its structural weakness, the local silk industry could not withstand foreign competition from the silk of Japan and China and later on the introduction of artificial fabrics... Having grown in relative prosperity, these peasants were facing limitations that threatened to send them economically a few steps backward... So it was that many peasants arrived at the year 1887 with a sense of malaise. They did not have much land, and what little they had did not promise to make them a 'good' living... These drawbacks made a number of peasants look for other ways out of their dilemma — namely, how to make enough money quickly to guarantee their status as landowners."
>
> — Paul Tabar, *Immigration and Human Development: Evidence from Lebanon*, 2009

**Part A.** Identify ONE way that interregional trade affected Lebanese emigrants in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A
> Interregional trade undermined the Lebanese silk industry by exposing it to competition from cheaper Japanese and Chinese silk on the world market. As global trade networks expanded, Lebanese silk producers could not compete with industrialized Asian producers, destroying a key source of rural income and pushing peasants to consider emigration as an economic solution.

**Part B.** Explain Akram Khater's argument about Lebanese emigrants.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B
> Khater argues that Lebanese emigration was driven primarily by economic anxiety rather than absolute poverty. After a period of relative prosperity, peasants found themselves squeezed by rising land prices, shrinking inheritances, and the collapse of the silk industry. Unable to secure enough land to maintain their social status, and finding that nearby cities offered no relief, emigration to the Americas became the only viable option for preserving landowner status and avoiding descent into the landless laboring class.

**Part C.** Explain ONE way in which urbanization influenced Lebanese migration in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C
> Regional urban centers like Aleppo and Bursa, which might have absorbed rural migrants, were themselves experiencing economic crises during this period and could not provide sufficient opportunities. This urban economic weakness meant that internal migration from village to city was not a viable alternative, making international emigration — especially to the Americas — the preferred path for Lebanese peasants seeking economic security. Urbanization thus influenced Lebanese migration by failing to provide a domestic outlet for rural population pressure.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

**1.** In one to three paragraphs, explain how environmental and economic factors contributed to patterns of migration between 1750 and 1900.

> [!tip] Model Response
> Environmental factors contributed directly to specific migration patterns, most dramatically through the Irish Great Famine of 1845–1852. The potato blight that destroyed the Irish crop for four consecutive years killed roughly one million people and forced over a million more to emigrate immediately, with millions following in subsequent decades. The famine interacted with economic factors — British colonial policies that continued food exports from Ireland during starvation, and the repeal of the Corn Laws that increased tenant evictions — to make emigration the only viable option for survival.
>
> Economic factors operated more broadly across all migration patterns in this period. Colonial demand for cheap plantation labor after the abolition of the slave trade drove the creation of the indenture and contract labor systems that transported over 1.5 million Indians and hundreds of thousands of Chinese workers to colonies worldwide. Poverty and land scarcity pushed Italian peasants and Chinese rural families abroad; gold rushes in California and Australia, higher wages in Argentina, and construction work on the Transcontinental Railroad pulled them to specific destinations. Improvements in transportation technology — especially the steamship — made migration economically feasible for more people and enabled sojourner patterns where migrants worked temporarily abroad before returning home.

## Think as a Historian: Connect Migration to Other Developments

*The Industrial Revolution led to: (1) improvements in transportation, (2) fundamental changes in the way goods were produced, (3) the move from mercantilism to capitalism, (4) imperialism, (5) changes in land used for agricultural purposes.*

**Choose three and explain the connection to migration between 1750 and 1900.**

> [!tip] Model Response
> **Transportation improvements** made mass migration logistically possible. Steamships cut ocean crossing times and costs, enabling the movement of millions of Europeans to the Americas and Australasia. Railroads attracted Chinese and Irish labor to construction projects in the US and Canada. Faster travel also enabled sojourner migration — Italian workers in Argentina or Japanese workers in Hawaii who planned to return home — which would have been impossible by sail.
>
> **The move from mercantilism to capitalism** created the economic conditions underlying both voluntary and coerced migration. Capitalist plantation agriculture demanded flexible, cheap labor after the abolition of slavery; this demand was met by Indian and Chinese indentured and contract workers. British investment in Argentina — more than in India itself — drew British businessmen and engineers as voluntary migrants. The global commodity markets created by capitalism gave specific purpose to migration: going to California to mine gold, to Cuba to work the sugar plantations, to the US to build railroads.
>
> **Imperialism** created the colonial infrastructure that absorbed migrant workers. British settler colonies in Canada, Australia, South Africa, and New Zealand were direct destinations for voluntary British migrants. The Colonial Service drew educated British men as career administrators. Indian indentured workers were sent to British colonies specifically to replace enslaved labor on colonial plantations. French penal colonies in New Caledonia and French Guiana served imperial labor and security functions. Without the colonial world built by imperialism, the scale and direction of 19th-century migration would have been fundamentally different.
