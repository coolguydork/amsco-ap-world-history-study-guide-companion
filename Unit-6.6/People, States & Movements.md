# People, States & Movements

**AMSCO pp. 417–428 | AP Period 3**

---

## Key Individuals

**Andrew Geddes Bain**
Scottish-born engineer and geologist who emigrated to Cape Town, South Africa in 1816. Built eight major roads and mountain passes; later produced the first comprehensive geological map of South Africa (1852) and reported on copper deposits in Namaqualand. Bain represents a broader pattern: British technical experts (engineers, geologists, surveyors) migrated to colonies throughout Africa and South Asia, spreading Western science while collaborating with local engineers. British engineers were so numerous in the colonies that they formed their own kind of diaspora.

**Andrew Carnegie**
Scottish immigrant who emigrated to the United States and became one of the wealthiest industrialists in American history. Represents the pull-factor success story of voluntary European emigration — from economic hardship in Scotland to dominance in the American steel industry. A useful contrast to the coerced migration stories that dominate this topic.

**Harriet Tubman**
Escaped enslaved person who became a conductor on the Underground Railroad, guiding others to freedom. While primarily a Unit 5 figure, Tubman is referenced here to contextualize coerced migration and the final decades of American slavery. The fact that the US abolished slavery only in 1865 — decades after Britain (1833) — helps explain why American demand for alternative labor systems lagged behind other colonial powers.

## Key States

**Ireland**
Emigration from Ireland resulted from overlapping push factors: British political domination (abolition of the Irish Parliament in 1801), religious discrimination against Catholics and Protestant dissenters, evictions of tenant farmers after the Corn Laws repeal (1846), and above all the Great Famine of 1845–1852 that destroyed the potato crop for four consecutive years. Over a million died; over a million more emigrated immediately. Most went to the United States, with significant numbers to Britain, Canada, and Australia. Emigration continued for decades after the famine — a testimony to how deeply British colonial policy had destabilized Irish society.

**China**
Chinese emigration accelerated in the mid-19th century driven by poverty, the disorder of the [[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview\|Taiping Rebellion]] (1850–1864), and the Opium Wars, which made emigration physically easier. Pull factors included California and Australian gold rushes (1850s), construction of the US Transcontinental Railroad, and plantation and factory work. The vast majority of Chinese emigrants were landless male peasants who intended to return home after earning money, but many settled permanently. Destination included Southeast Asia (pre-existing communities), the Americas, Australia, New Zealand, and Europe.

**India**
Poverty was the primary push factor for Indian emigration. The British began sending Indians to Mauritius as indentured laborers in 1834, and by 1880 Indian workers were present across British Guiana, Dutch Guiana, Natal, Fiji, Trinidad, and other Caribbean islands. Most signed five-year contracts; many renewed and eventually settled permanently. Over 1.5 million Indians were shipped to colonies before Britain abolished the indenture system in 1916. Indian migrant workers permanently transformed the demographics and cultures of Mauritius, Fiji, and Trinidad — a powerful example of how labor migration reshapes societies.

**Italy**
The first wave of Italian emigration began after unification in 1861 and continued until roughly 1900. Over 4 million Italians emigrated in those four decades. The main push factor was poverty — especially in southern Italy where land was subdivided across generations and organized crime destabilized the economy. Pull factors included Argentina's pro-immigration policies and higher wages. Many Italian workers engaged in **sojourner migration** — working temporarily in Argentina then returning home — made possible by improved steamship travel. Those who left often sent remittances to family, encouraging further emigration.

**Japan**
Japan was closed to the world before the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration\|Meiji Restoration]] (1868). By 1893 the Meiji government had established the Colonization Society to relieve rural population pressure and expand Japanese commercial influence abroad. A failed attempt to settle a colony in Mexico (1892) was followed by the more successful dispatch of 790 contract workers to Peru in 1899. Beginning in 1885, a formal Japan-Hawaii agreement sent approximately 29,000 Japanese laborers to Hawaiian sugar plantations over nine years. Anti-Japanese sentiment on the US West Coast culminated in the **Gentlemen's Agreement of 1907** — an informal arrangement by which Japan agreed to stop issuing passports to laborers emigrating to the US and the US agreed not to formally ban Japanese immigration. The agreement was ultimately ended by the Immigration Act of 1924.

**Australia**
Transformed from a penal dumping ground into a major settler colony. Britain began shipping convicts to Australia in 1788 and ended the practice by 1850 (by which point a stay there was no longer considered much of a punishment). Australia attracted free settlers increasingly through the 19th century, especially after the discovery of gold in 1851 drew approximately 50,000 Chinese migrants during the gold rush. Eventually became one of Britain's most successful settler colonies, with significant Irish, British, and Chinese migrant communities. Represents all three migration types: coerced (convicts), voluntary (free settlers), and labor migration (Chinese contract workers).

**Argentina**
Part of Britain's "informal empire" in the 19th century — Britain invested more in Argentina than in India. British businessmen, bankers, engineers, and traders settled there, founding banks, building railroads, developing agricultural exports, and importing luxuries for the Argentine middle class. The Italian community was even larger: Italians were so central to Argentina's economy and culture that Mendoza erected a monument to Italian immigrants (Plaza Italia). Argentina represents the pull-factor side of voluntary migration: pro-immigration policies, higher wages, and available land drew both British and Italian migrants.

## Key Movements and Systems

**Coerced Migration Spectrum**
The AP exam distinguishes among degrees of coercion. At the most coerced end: **chattel slavery** (Africans, 1500s–1888). In the middle: **indentured servitude** and **contract labor** (Indians and Chinese, 1806–1916), where workers technically consented but were often deceived or pressured, worked under exploitative conditions, and had little ability to leave. At the voluntary end: **free emigration** (Irish, Italians, Japanese, British) driven by push/pull factors. **Convict transportation** (British penal colonies, 1788–1868) is also coerced but distinct from labor systems.

**The Kangani and Maistry Systems**
Two semi-coercive labor recruitment systems operating in Southeast Asia between 1880 and 1938. The **kangani** system (Ceylon and Malaya) used foremen who recruited within their own extended family networks. The **maistry** system (Burma) employed supervisors working within structured hierarchies to send laborers to plantations. Both resulted in highly exploitative conditions for Indian workers in Southeast Asia.

**The Gentlemen's Agreement (1907)**
Informal diplomatic arrangement between the US and Japan in which Japan agreed to stop issuing passports to laborers emigrating to the US, and the US agreed not to formally restrict Japanese immigration. Never formally ratified; ended by the US Immigration Act of 1924. Reflects the anti-immigrant backlash that voluntary migration from Asia generated in the United States and foreshadows 20th-century immigration restriction movements.
