Industrialization is the structural cause of the migration era. Steamship technology (cuts ocean travel time and cost), railroad construction (destination for Chinese and Irish labor), and the shift to capitalist plantation agriculture (creates demand for cheap colonial labor) all flow from the Industrial Revolution. Unit 5.9 also covers urbanization — the process that pulls internal migrants into factory cities and is intensified by the very migration patterns described in 6.6.

## Compare With

**[[Unit-4.7/Changing Social Hierarchies Overview\|Changing Social Hierarchies Overview]]**
Unit 4.7 covers the social hierarchies created by the Atlantic slave trade and colonial plantation systems — the same infrastructure that 6.6 inherits and transforms. The comparison question the AP expects: How did the labor systems of the early modern period (enslaved Africans) compare to the labor systems of the 19th century (indentured Indians and Chinese contract workers)? Continuity: colonial economies still demanded cheap labor for plantation agriculture. Change: the legal form of labor transformed from chattel property to contractual servitude with a path to freedom. The geographic source of labor shifted from Africa to South and East Asia.

## Cross-Unit Connections

**[[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade\|Atlantic Slave Trade]]**
The Atlantic slave trade and 6.6 are the two ends of the same story. Abolition of the slave trade (Britain 1807, US 1808) directly caused the creation of indenture and contract labor systems. The demand for plantation labor — cotton, sugar, rubber — didn't disappear; only its legal form changed. The African diaspora is the largest involuntary diaspora in history; 6.6 adds the Indian and Chinese diasporas as its 19th-century successors.

**[[Concept Notes/Capitalism\|Capitalism]]**
The transition from mercantilism to capitalism created the economic conditions for mass migration. Capitalist plantation agriculture demanded low-cost labor; capitalist industrial production demanded factory workers. The mobility of capital (British investment in Argentina, for instance) preceded and enabled the mobility of labor. Remittances — migrants sending money home — are themselves a feature of the global capitalist economy.

**[[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration\|Meiji Restoration]]**
Japan's transformation after 1868 is the direct cause of Japanese overseas emigration. The Meiji government established the Colonization Society (1893), negotiated the 1885 Japan-Hawaii labor agreement, and managed Japanese emigration as a tool of foreign policy. The Gentlemen's Agreement (1907) that curtailed Japanese immigration to the US is both a migration story and a story of Japanese-American diplomatic relations in the new age of imperialism.

**[[Concept Notes/Opium Trade\|Opium Trade]]**
The Opium Wars (1839–1842, 1856–1860) weakened Qing China, increased poverty in coastal regions, and made emigration physically easier by opening Chinese ports. Chinese emigration to Southeast Asia accelerated after the first Opium War. The combination of the Opium Wars and the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864) created the desperate conditions that drove millions of Chinese to seek opportunities abroad.

## AP Essay Angles

**Causation LEQ (likely prompt structure):** "Explain the causes of mass migration in the period 1750–1900." Key argument: both structural causes (industrial demand for labor, transportation improvements, abolition of slavery) and specific triggers (Great Famine, Taiping Rebellion, gold rushes) drove migration. The coerced vs. voluntary distinction must be addressed.

**CCOT LEQ:** "Evaluate the extent to which labor systems changed in the period 1750–1900." Thesis: Significant change in legal form (slavery → indenture → free migration) but continuity in the colonial demand for cheap labor and the exploitation of migrant workers.

**Comparison SAQ:** Chinese migration vs. Indian migration; voluntary Irish migration vs. coerced indentured migration; British settler migration vs. Asian contract labor.
