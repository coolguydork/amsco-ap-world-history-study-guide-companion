# Key Terms & Concepts

> [!exam-tip]
> Know these terms cold — they appear in MCQ answer choices and DBQ/LEQ rubric language. Pay special attention to terms that describe the *limits* of revolution: who was excluded and why.

---

**Popular sovereignty**
The principle that legitimate political authority comes from the consent of the governed. Derived from Enlightenment thinkers (especially Locke and Rousseau), this idea underpinned every major Atlantic revolution of the period and directly challenged the divine right of kings. (AMSCO p. 285)

**[[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]]**
A shared identity based on common language, culture, history, and territory — and the political belief that people sharing such an identity should govern themselves as a unified nation. Nationalism both drove Atlantic revolutions (colonial peoples rejecting foreign rule) and European unification movements (Italians and Germans seeking one state). After 1871, extreme nationalism would become a source of dangerous inter-state competition. (AMSCO pp. 285, 290–293)

**Declaration of Independence (1776)**
American colonial document drafted by Thomas Jefferson, drawing directly on John Locke's language of "unalienable rights." Declared that governments derive just powers from the consent of the governed, and that the colonies were severing ties with Britain. A model for later revolutionary documents. (AMSCO p. 285)

**Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789)**
French Revolutionary document adopted by the National Assembly. Stated that all men are born free and equal, that sovereignty belongs to the nation, and that government exists to protect natural rights. Inspired by the American Declaration; later used as a model by Toussaint L'Ouverture in Haiti. (AMSCO p. 286)

**liberté, égalité, et fraternité**
The three-word slogan of the French Revolution — liberty, equality, and fraternity — which summarized its radical political ideals and were spread across Europe through Napoleon's conquests. (AMSCO p. 286)

**Estates-General**
France's medieval legislative body, representing the three estates: clergy, nobility, and commoners. Called by Louis XVI in 1789 to address France's fiscal crisis. Inequality in voting procedures led commoners to break away and form the National Assembly, triggering the Revolution. (AMSCO p. 286)

**Bastille**
A royal fortress and prison in Paris stormed by revolutionary crowds on July 14, 1789. The event became a defining symbol of the French Revolution — the moment ordinary people rose against royal tyranny. July 14 is still commemorated as French Independence Day. (AMSCO p. 286)

**Reign of Terror**
The radical phase of the French Revolution (1793–1794) during which the Jacobin-led Committee of Public Safety executed thousands of perceived enemies of the revolution, including King Louis XVI and Queen Marie Antoinette, as well as moderate opponents. Led by Maximilien Robespierre. (AMSCO p. 286)

**Maroons**
Individuals who had escaped slavery in Haiti and lived independently before the 1791 revolt. Maroons joined the enslaved Africans led by Toussaint L'Ouverture in the Haitian Revolution, providing experienced fighters. Communities of escaped enslaved people also existed throughout the Caribbean and Americas. (AMSCO p. 287)

**[[Concept Notes/Philosophes\|Philosophes]]**
French Enlightenment intellectuals (e.g., Montesquieu, Voltaire, Rousseau) who popularized ideas about natural rights, religious liberty, and representative government. Their writings circulated widely in Europe and the Americas, giving intellectual justification to revolutionaries. (AMSCO pp. 276, 286)

**Creoles**
In colonial Latin America, people of European ancestry born in the Americas. Better educated and wealthier than mestizos or indigenous people, creoles led most Latin American independence movements. They resented Spain's [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilism]] and peninsulares' monopoly on high government posts. (AMSCO p. 288)

**[[Concept Notes/Peninsulares\|Peninsulares]]**
Colonists born in Spain or Portugal, considered the social elite in colonial Latin America. They monopolized top government and church positions in the colonies, fueling resentment among creoles. See also [[Unit-4.7/Changing Social Hierarchies Overview|Changing Social Hierarchies Overview]] for the full casta hierarchy. (AMSCO pp. 255, 288)

**[[Concept Notes/Mestizos\|Mestizos]]**
In colonial Latin America, people of mixed European and indigenous ancestry. Below creoles in the social hierarchy; often worked in towns, mines, or on estates. Wanted political power and a share of colonial wealth. See also [[Unit-4.7/Changing Social Hierarchies Overview|Changing Social Hierarchies Overview]]. (AMSCO pp. 256, 288)

**Mulattoes**
People of mixed African and European or indigenous ancestry in colonial Latin America. Occupied one of the lower rungs of the casta system, above only indigenous peoples and the enslaved. (AMSCO p. 288)

**Caudillos**
Strong regional leaders who emerged in Latin America after independence, commanding armies loyal to them personally rather than to constitutional governments. Caudillos intervened in national politics and often undermined representative institutions — a key reason why Latin American democracies struggled after independence. (AMSCO p. 289)

**Realpolitik**
"The politics of reality" — practical, pragmatic political strategy that prioritizes power and results over ideals or morality. Associated especially with Count di Cavour (Italian unification) and Otto von Bismarck (German unification), both of whom manipulated wars and alliances to achieve national goals. (AMSCO p. 290)

**Risorgimento**
Italian for "resurgence" — the 19th-century movement for Italian cultural and political unification. Associated with Giuseppe Mazzini, who had been agitating for Italian unity since the early 1800s. Cavour adapted Risorgimento ideals into his realpolitik strategy. (AMSCO p. 290)

**Propaganda Movement**
A Filipino nationalist movement of the 1880s–1890s that advocated for greater Philippine autonomy from Spain through publications, pamphlets, and magazines. Led by figures including José Rizal. It did not initially call for independence but was viewed with suspicion by Spanish authorities. (AMSCO pp. 289–290)

**Ottomanism**
A 19th-century Ottoman reform movement that aimed to create a more modern, unified state by minimizing ethnic, linguistic, and religious differences across the empire — for example, through standardized schooling and curriculum. Ironically, it intensified subject peoples' nationalist desires by highlighting their differences from the Ottoman mainstream. (AMSCO p. 293)

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse **realpolitik** with opportunism. Cavour and Bismarck had clear nationalist goals — they just pursued them through manipulation rather than idealism. The AP exam may ask you to explain how realpolitik differed from Romantic nationalism (Mazzini's approach).
