# Practice & Review

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 5.2 appears frequently in comparison questions about the Atlantic revolutions and in LEQs about the causes and limits of nationalist/revolutionary movements. Know all four revolutions, their causes, and — especially — who was excluded from their benefits.

---

## Multiple-Choice Questions

Questions 1–3 refer to the comparison table below (AMSCO p. 294):

| Revolution | One Major Cause | Two Major Results |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------|
| American | Opposition to taxation without representation | Established independence; Created a written constitution |
| French | Opposition to the growing concentration of wealth | Overthrew monarchy; Ended feudalism and serfdom |
| Haitian | Opposition to slavery | Led to end of slavery; Redistributed land to free Blacks and freed men and women |
| Creole | Opposition to European control | Established several independent countries; Led to social conflicts |

---

**Question 1:** How were the Creole Revolutions *not* like the American, French, and Haitian Revolutions?

A) The Creole Revolutions were mostly against Spain.
B) The Creole Revolutions united all social classes.
C) The Creole Revolutions occurred more quickly.
D) The Creole Revolutions were the only ones that succeeded.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The American Revolution was against Britain, the French Revolution was an internal transformation of French society, and the Haitian Revolution was primarily against France. The Creole Revolutions in Latin America were distinctively directed against Spanish imperial rule. B is wrong because the Creole revolutions famously *did not* unite all social classes — creole elites excluded mestizos, indigenous peoples, and the enslaved. D is false because all four revolutions succeeded in some form.

---

**Question 2:** Which revolution most directly addressed the unequal distribution of economic opportunity and resources?

A) American
B) Haitian
C) French
D) Creole

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Haitian Revolution is the best answer because it both ended slavery (the most extreme economic exploitation) and directly redistributed land from plantation owners to formerly enslaved people and free Black people. The French Revolution ended feudalism and serfdom — also economically significant — but it did not redistribute land as directly. The American Revolution primarily addressed political independence, not economic redistribution. The Creole Revolutions left the existing economic hierarchy largely intact, with creole elites replacing peninsulares.

---

**Question 3:** Which generalization applies to *all* the revolutions listed in the table?

A) All resulted in newly independent countries.
B) All advocated racial equality.
C) All were reversed within a generation of their completion.
D) All were inspired by Enlightenment ideals.

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> All four revolutions drew on Enlightenment ideas about natural rights, popular sovereignty, and opposition to tyranny — even the Haitian revolutionaries explicitly engaged Enlightenment philosophy (L'Ouverture was well-read in it). A is wrong because France was already an independent country before its revolution. B is wrong because the American and French revolutions preserved slavery and denied rights to non-European peoples. C is wrong — the American, Haitian, and most Latin American republics were not reversed within a generation.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1: Bolívar Source Analysis (AMSCO p. 295)

**Source:** Simón Bolívar, speech to the Council of Angostura, 1819:
> "We are not European; we are not Indian; we are but a mixed species of aborigines and Spaniards. Americans by birth and Europeans by law, we find ourselves engaged in a dual conflict: we are disputing with the natives for titles of ownership, and at the same time we are struggling to maintain ourselves in the country that gave us birth against the opposition of the invaders."

**Part A: Identify Bolívar's intended audience.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Identify
> Bolívar's intended audience was the Congress of Angostura (1819) — a governing assembly of creole leaders and independence fighters in what would become Gran Colombia. He was speaking to educated Latin American elites who shared his creole identity and needed political and ideological justification for their independence cause. He was also implicitly addressing European powers (especially Britain) whose support he sought for Latin American independence.

**Part B: Explain ONE way Bolívar's ideas support or reject the Enlightenment.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation/Continuity
> Bolívar's speech *supports* Enlightenment ideals by invoking the natural rights of the people of the Americas to self-determination. His argument — that "Americans by birth" should not be governed by the "opposition of the invaders" (Spain) — draws directly on Locke's idea that legitimate government requires the consent of the governed. By framing Spanish colonial rule as illegitimate because it violates the rights of those who were born in the Americas, Bolívar applies Enlightenment political philosophy to a colonial context, echoing the arguments Jefferson made against British rule in 1776.

**Part C: Explain ONE long-term impact of Bolívar's actions.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> One long-term impact of Bolívar's actions was the political instability of post-independence Latin America. Bolívar's military campaigns broke up Spanish colonial control, but the new republics he helped create fractured along regional and class lines — Gran Colombia itself dissolved by 1830. Without strong constitutional institutions, power passed to caudillos — regional strongmen who used military force rather than representative government. This pattern of instability, with civilian governments repeatedly overthrown by military leaders, shaped Latin American politics well into the 20th century.

### SAQ 2: Open Questions (AMSCO p. 295)

**Part A: Identify ONE reason why nationalism thrived in the period 1750–1900.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Identify
> Nationalism thrived in the period 1750–1900 partly because Napoleon's conquests across Europe spread both revolutionary ideals and nationalist backlash. As French armies occupied German and Italian states, local populations developed a stronger sense of shared cultural identity in opposition to French rule. This "reactive nationalism" — the feeling that people with a common language, history, and culture should govern themselves — intensified in the aftermath of Napoleonic occupation and accelerated movements for German and Italian unification.

**Part B: Explain ONE way nationalist movements in Italy and Germany were similar.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Both Italian unification under Cavour and German unification under Bismarck relied on **realpolitik** — the use of pragmatic power politics and deliberately engineered wars to achieve nationalist goals, rather than appealing to idealistic principles. Cavour maneuvered France into war with Austria in 1858 to weaken Austrian influence over the Italian Peninsula. Similarly, Bismarck engineered three successive wars (against Denmark, Austria, and France) to bring German states under Prussian leadership. In both cases, nationalist unification was achieved not through popular uprisings alone but through the calculated use of state military power by skilled diplomatic manipulators.

**Part C: Explain ONE way ideas of revolutions in the Americas differed in the period 1750–1900.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> The American and Haitian Revolutions differed significantly in how broadly they applied the Enlightenment principle that all people possess natural rights. The American Revolution proclaimed that "all men are created equal" but preserved slavery and denied political rights to women and non-property-owning white men — limiting the revolution's benefits to a narrow group. The Haitian Revolution, by contrast, was led by enslaved Africans who applied Enlightenment universalism to its logical conclusion: all people, regardless of race, deserve freedom and equal citizenship. The Haitian constitution of 1801 granted equality and citizenship to *all* residents — the most radical application of Enlightenment ideals of any Atlantic revolution.

## Think As a Historian: Comparing Arguments (AMSCO pp. 295–296)

**Source A:** John Dickinson, Continental Congress, July 1, 1776 — *against* independence
**Source B:** Declaration of Independence, July 4, 1776 — *for* independence

**Question 1: Dickinson's view on separating from Britain?**

> [!tip] Model Response
> Dickinson opposed independence. He argued that the colonies already enjoyed liberty under the British monarchy and that independence would lead to "licentious anarchy and popular tyranny." Using a body-politic metaphor, he claimed that the king and Parliament, as the "head" of the empire, were necessary to maintain unity and prevent civil war. His core claim: separation would make things worse, not better.

**Question 2: Structure of Dickinson's argument?**

> [!tip] Model Response
> Dickinson's argument is *deductive*: he starts from a general claim (that kings are necessary to maintain political order, just as the head governs the body) and applies it to the specific situation of American independence (therefore separating from the king would produce chaos). He uses analogy (body/head metaphor), rhetorical question ("have we not enjoyed liberty?"), and a prediction of negative consequences (anarchy, tyranny) rather than citing specific facts or grievances.

**Question 3: Main claim of the Declaration of Independence?**

> [!tip] Model Response
> The Declaration's central claim is that the King of Great Britain has forfeited his right to govern the colonies through a "history of repeated injuries and usurpations." Because government derives its just powers from the consent of the governed, and the King has repeatedly violated that consent, the colonies have the right — indeed the obligation — to declare themselves free and independent states.

**Question 4: How the Declaration draws on Locke and Enlightenment thinkers?**

> [!tip] Model Response
> The Declaration draws directly on Locke's argument that government exists to protect natural rights and that when government violates those rights, the people have the right to alter or abolish it. The list of specific grievances (laws refused, assemblies dissolved, people's lives destroyed) mirrors Locke's argument in the *Second Treatise* that a king who acts tyrannically has placed himself in a state of war with the people. The Declaration also reflects Enlightenment empiricism — it builds its case from specific, enumerable facts rather than abstract theology.

**Question 5: Deductive reasoning in the Declaration?**

> [!tip] Model Response
> The Declaration's structure is deductive: (1) all governments derive just powers from the consent of the governed [major premise — Lockean natural rights theory]; (2) the King has repeatedly violated the colonists' rights and denied their consent [minor premise — the list of grievances]; (3) therefore, the colonies have the right to be free and independent states [conclusion]. This syllogistic structure gave the Declaration both rhetorical and logical force: if the reader accepts the Enlightenment premises, the conclusion follows necessarily.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

**In 1–3 paragraphs, explain the causes and effects of the various revolutions in the period from 1750 to 1900, including influences of the Enlightenment and emerging nationalism.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> The revolutions of 1750–1900 shared a common ideological foundation: Enlightenment ideas about natural rights, popular sovereignty, and government by consent. Thinkers such as Locke, Rousseau, and Montesquieu provided revolutionaries across the Atlantic world with the language to challenge monarchies and colonial rule. Economic grievances amplified ideological ones — British mercantilism alienated American colonists, French fiscal crisis mobilized commoners, and Spanish mercantilist restrictions frustrated Latin American creoles. Each revolution also inspired the next: the American Revolution demonstrated that colonists could win; the French Revolution's Declaration of Rights became a template; Haitian revolutionaries applied those rights to enslaved people where others would not.
>
> The effects of the Atlantic revolutions were transformative but uneven. New republics and constitutions replaced monarchies and colonial administrations, establishing popular sovereignty as the new basis for political legitimacy. Yet the gap between revolutionary ideals and revolutionary realities was stark: the American Revolution preserved slavery for nearly a century; Latin American creoles replaced peninsulares but maintained the casta hierarchy; women remained legally subordinate across all the new republics. Only Haiti actually extended revolutionary rights to the formerly enslaved — and was punished with diplomatic isolation and forced debt.
>
> Beyond the Atlantic world, nationalism — the sense of shared cultural identity as a basis for political unity — drove European unification movements in Italy and Germany. Cavour and Bismarck used realpolitik rather than idealism to unify their nations through manufactured wars, producing two powerful new states by 1871. In Asia, Filipino intellectuals absorbed European nationalist ideas and organized the Propaganda Movement, which evolved into the Philippine Revolution (1896) when Spanish repression made autonomy impossible. By 1900, the revolutionary and nationalist tradition had permanently restructured political authority across the globe — while leaving deep social inequalities largely intact, setting the stage for the reform and revolutionary movements of the 20th century.
