# Topic 5.2 — Revolutions Compared

---

## Comparing the Atlantic Revolutions

The American, French, Haitian, and Latin American revolutions all drew from [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment]] ideas, but they differed enormously in who led them, what they demanded, and what they achieved.

### Causes

| Revolution | Political Cause | Economic Cause | Social Cause |
|-----------|----------------|---------------|-------------|
| American | Colonial legislatures vs. Parliament; no representation | British mercantilism | Growing sense of colonial identity |
| French | Estates inequality; monarchy's refusal to reform | Massive government debt; food scarcity | 97% of society (commoners) had little political voice |
| Haitian | French Revolution's ideals of liberty rang hollow under slavery | Plantation economy depended on enslaved labor | Enslaved people + Maroons had no rights at all |
| Latin American (Creole) | Peninsulares held key government posts | Spanish mercantilism blocked creole economic freedom | Complex racial hierarchy; creoles at disadvantage vs. peninsulares |

### Leaders

| Revolution | Key Leader(s) | Background | Enlightenment Connection |
|-----------|--------------|-----------|-------------------------|
| American | Thomas Jefferson, George Washington | Colonial elites | Directly read Locke; borrowed "unalienable rights" |
| French | Marquis de Lafayette → Robespierre → Napoleon | Aristocrat → radical lawyer → general | Deeply immersed in philosophe thinking |
| Haitian | Toussaint L'Ouverture, Jean-Jacques Dessalines | Formerly enslaved; self-educated | L'Ouverture well-read in Enlightenment thought |
| Latin American | Simón Bolívar, José de San Martín, Manuela Saenz | Wealthy creole elites | "Jamaica Letter" (1815) references Enlightenment ideals directly |

### Outcomes and Limitations

| Revolution | What Changed | What Stayed the Same |
|-----------|-------------|---------------------|
| American | Constitutional republic; independence from Britain | Slavery continued; women's rights unchanged; Indigenous people dispossessed |
| French | Feudalism abolished; Declaration of Rights of Man; Napoleon's legal codes spread across Europe | Napoleon became a new authoritarian ruler; class privilege persisted |
| Haitian | Complete abolition of slavery; Black-led independent nation | Massive indemnity paid to France for 122 years; economic isolation |
| Latin American | Independence from Spain/Portugal; legal end of some social distinctions | Caudillos replaced Spanish rule; creole elites maintained power; women gained almost nothing |

## Comparing the French and Haitian Revolutions

These two revolutions are especially important to compare because the Haitian Revolution grew *directly* out of the French Revolution — and then exposed France's hypocrisy.

**Similarities:**
- Both rooted in Enlightenment ideas about natural rights and equality
- Both challenged legal systems that divided people into estates/classes
- Both used violent means to overthrow oppressive systems

**Differences:**
- France's restraints were legal and political; Haiti's were the absolute denial of humanity through chattel slavery
- The Haitian Revolution was led by enslaved people — far more radical than France's bourgeois revolution
- Haiti achieved the complete abolition of slavery; France went through cycles of abolition, restoration, and re-abolition
- Haitian leaders (L'Ouverture, Dessalines) had to fight France, Spain, AND Britain simultaneously

> [!exam-tip]
> When comparing the French and Haitian Revolutions on the AP exam, emphasize this: both used Enlightenment rhetoric, but the Haitians faced far more severe oppression and achieved a far more radical transformation (the only successful slave revolution in history).

## Nationalism and Unification: Italy vs. Germany

Both Italy and Germany unified in the 1860s–1870s using **realpolitik** — but their paths differed.

| | Italian Unification | German Unification |
|--|--------------------|--------------------|
| Key leader | Count di Cavour (Prime Minister of Piedmont-Sardinia) | Otto von Bismarck (Chancellor of Prussia) |
| Strategy | Diplomatic manipulation + military alliance (Garibaldi's Red Shirts) | Three engineered wars (Denmark 1864, Austria 1866, France 1870) |
| Role of France | France briefly allied, then backed out | France defeated in Franco-Prussian War (1870) |
| Ideological champion | Giuseppe Mazzini (Risorgimento) | Prussian nationalism |
| Completed | 1861 (Kingdom of Italy) | 1871 (German Empire) |
| Key territory gained | Unified Italian Peninsula | Alsace-Lorraine (from France) |
| Long-term consequence | Southern poverty; emigration | New great power → competing alliances → WWI |

> [!tip]
> Both Cavour and Bismarck were conservatives who used nationalism as a **tool** to achieve political goals — not because they were idealistic nationalists. Realpolitik means using whatever works, including war and manipulation.

## New Zealand: Resistance Nationalism

The **New Zealand Wars** are an often-overlooked example of colonial resistance:
- Maori (Polynesian people) had inhabited New Zealand since mid-1200s
- British annexed New Zealand in 1840; pressure for Maori land increased
- The wars produced a sense of **Maori nationalism** — shared identity in resistance to British colonialism
- By 1872, British had won militarily; but Maori cultural identity persisted

This is a useful comparison point: nationalism could be a **tool of resistance** against colonial powers, not just a European phenomenon.
