# Topic 5.2 — Nationalism and Revolutions: Overview

**AP Unit:** 5 — Revolutions | **Period:** 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900) | **AMSCO:** pp. 285–293

**Essential Question:** What were the causes and effects of the various revolutions in the period 1750–1900, including the influence of the Enlightenment and emerging nationalism?

---

## The Big Picture

The [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment]] generated ideas about natural rights, popular sovereignty, and constitutional government. Those ideas collided with political realities — colonial rule, absolute monarchy, slavery, ethnic oppression — and produced a wave of revolutions from the 1770s through the 1820s. Nationalism then continued reshaping borders through unification movements in Italy and Germany and independence movements in the Balkans and Latin America well into the 1870s.

> [!exam-tip]
> A key AP skill is comparing revolutions. Know the causes, leaders, outcomes, and limitations of each revolution — especially the Haitian Revolution, which is frequently tested because it was the only successful slave revolution in history.

## The American Revolution

Rooted in Enlightenment philosophy and frustration with British mercantilism, the American Revolution also reflected the political maturity colonial legislatures had developed over decades of relative self-governance.

- **Declaration of Independence** (July 4, 1776): Jefferson used Locke's "unalienable rights," changing "property" to "the pursuit of happiness"; declared independence from British rule
- Americans triumphed 1783 with crucial French aid
- Influenced French and Latin American revolutionaries — proof that Enlightenment ideals could become reality

---

## The French Revolution

France in the 1780s combined Enlightenment idealism with a real fiscal crisis — the government was broke from financing wars, including the American Revolution.

**Key events:**
- **Estates-General** (1789): clergy + nobility + commoners (97% of French society); commoners broke away and formed the National Assembly
- **Bastille stormed** (July 14, 1789) — French Independence Day; symbolic blow against monarchy
- **Declaration of the Rights of Man** — abolished feudalism; declared basic human rights
- **Reign of Terror**: Jacobins executed thousands, including Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette
- **Napoleon Bonaparte** became Emperor (1804) — exported Enlightenment-influenced legal codes across Europe

> [!tip]
> France's slogan — *liberté, égalité, fraternité* (liberty, equality, fraternity) — perfectly encapsulates Enlightenment ideals translated into revolutionary action.

## The Haitian Revolution

The most radical revolution of the era — enslaved people overthrowing their masters and founding an independent nation.

- Haiti (western third of Hispaniola) was France's richest sugar/coffee colony
- Slave revolt began 1791; soon joined by **Maroons** (escaped slaves)
- **Toussaint L'Ouverture**: formerly enslaved; well-read in Enlightenment thought; brilliant general; played French, Spanish, and British against each other; constitution of 1801 (equality and citizenship for all; declared himself governor for life)
- L'Ouverture betrayed by French, imprisoned; died in France 1803
- **Jean-Jacques Dessalines** declared permanent independence: **January 1, 1804**

Haiti's significance:
- First country in Latin America to win independence
- First Black-led country in the Western Hemisphere
- Only country to become permanently independent as a result of a slave uprising

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam LOVES the Haitian Revolution because it tests whether you understand the Enlightenment's contradictions. France declared "liberty, equality, fraternity" but kept enslaving people. Haitians called France's bluff.

## Creole Revolutions in Latin America

Latin American independence movements were driven primarily by **creoles** (people of European ancestry born in the Americas) who resented Spanish mercantilism and the political dominance of *peninsulares* (those born in Spain).

**Social hierarchy (top to bottom):**
1. Peninsulares (born in Spain/Portugal) — held important government jobs
2. Creoles (European ancestry, born in Americas) — wealthy but politically excluded
3. Mestizos (European + indigenous ancestry)
4. Mulattoes (African + European or indigenous ancestry)
5. Indigenous people and African slaves

**Why creoles revolted:**
- Opposed Spain's mercantilism (forced to buy from and sell only to Spain)
- Wanted political power (government jobs went to peninsulares)
- Inspired by American and French revolutions

**Simón Bolívar** (Venezuela, 1783–1830): key leader; liberated Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru; envisioned Gran Colombia as a federation like the United States; "Jamaica Letter" (1815) expressed his vision and frustration.

**Aftermath:** **Caudillos** (strong local military leaders) often stepped in after independence, undermining representative government and the rule of law. Women gained very little — unable to vote, enter contracts, or receive education (exception: **Manuela Saenz**, colonel who saved Bolívar's life).

## Later Challenges to Spanish Colonialism

- **Puerto Rico and Cuba** (1868): uprisings against Spain; **Lola Rodriguez de Tié** — poet and revolutionary who wrote "La Borinqueña"
- **Philippines**: **Propaganda Movement** — educated Filipinos (José Rizal) called for autonomy (not independence); Rizal arrested 1892, executed 1896; **Philippine Revolution** began 1896

## Nationalism and Unification in Europe

Nationalism — the feeling that people who share a language, culture, and history should live in an independent nation-state — drove **unification** movements as well as independence movements.

### Italian Unification
- **Count di Cavour** (Prime Minister of Piedmont-Sardinia): used *realpolitik* (practical politics); manipulated Napoleon III of France into war with Austria; allied with Garibaldi's Red Shirts
- **Giuseppe Mazzini**: ideological champion of Italian resurgence (**Risorgimento**)
- **Giuseppe Garibaldi**: military leader (Red Shirts) who fought in the Kingdom of Naples
- Italy united under the House of Savoy

### German Unification
- **Otto von Bismarck**: Prussian leader; used *realpolitik*; engineered 3 wars:
  1. Prussia + Austria vs. Denmark (1864)
  2. Prussia vs. Austria (Seven Weeks' War, 1866)
  3. Prussia vs. France (Franco-Prussian War, 1870)
- **German Empire founded 1871**; included Alsace-Lorraine (long part of France → major grievance → later helps cause WWI)

### Balkan Nationalism
- Ottoman Empire declining since 17th century; failed to conquer Vienna (1683)
- Greece: 350+ years under Ottoman rule; Greek nationalism + British/French/Russian naval intervention → Greek independence (1827–1832)
- **Ottomanism** (1870s–80s): attempt to create unified multi-ethnic state through shared curriculum and standard law → backfired by intensifying minority nationalism

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the connection: Balkan nationalism → competing European alliances → [[Concept Notes/World War I|World War I]]. German and Italian unification created new great powers that upset the balance of power.
