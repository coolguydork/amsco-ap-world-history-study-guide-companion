# Themes & Historical Reasoning

## AP Themes in Topic 5.2

**GOV — Governance:** Atlantic revolutions challenged divine right and colonial authority, replacing them with popular sovereignty and republican government. Nationalist movements in Europe sought to align political borders with cultural communities — sometimes through constitutional means, often through war.

**CDI — Cultural Developments & Interactions:** Enlightenment ideas circulated across the Atlantic world, inspiring revolutionaries from Philadelphia to Port-au-Prince to Caracas to Manila. Revolutionary documents — the Declaration of Independence, the Declaration of the Rights of Man — became templates copied and adapted across the globe. [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] itself is a cultural phenomenon: people identifying through shared language, literature, customs, and history.

**SIO — Social Interactions & Organization:** Revolutions both challenged and reproduced social hierarchies. The casta system of colonial Latin America created the social tensions that motivated the creole-led independence movements — yet those same creoles maintained their dominance after independence. Women, indigenous peoples, and the enslaved were systematically excluded from the rights revolutions proclaimed.

## Historical Reasoning: Causation

### What caused the Atlantic Revolutions?

**Ideological causes** — [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment Overview]] supplied the intellectual framework: natural rights (Locke), popular sovereignty (Rousseau), separation of powers (Montesquieu). Without Enlightenment ideas, there was no language for challenging monarchy.

**Economic causes** — British [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilism]] restricted American colonial trade. France's fiscal crisis (partly caused by funding the American Revolution) triggered the Estates-General. Spain's mercantilism and peninsulares' monopoly on colonial offices alienated creoles in Latin America.

**Political causes** — Colonial legislatures in America had grown accustomed to self-governance. The French Estates-General's voting inequality made the commoners' grievances structural. Colonial subjects everywhere chafed under laws made without their representation.

**Cross-pollination** — Each revolution inspired the next. The American Revolution demonstrated that colonists could win. French revolutionaries applied universal-rights language more broadly. The Haitian Revolution showed that the enslaved could apply those same ideas to themselves — a conclusion that terrified slaveholders across the Americas.

## Historical Reasoning: Comparison

### Comparing the Atlantic Revolutions

| Dimension | American | French | Haitian | Latin American |
|-----------|----------|--------|---------|----------------|
| Who led it | Creole colonial elites | Middle class, radical urban poor | Enslaved Africans + Maroons | Creole elites |

| Outcome | Republic; constitutional democracy | Cycles of republic and empire | First Black republic | Multiple republics; caudillo instability |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves the comparison angle: "How did the goals and outcomes of the Haitian Revolution compare with those of the American or French Revolution?" Practice articulating both similarities (all drew on Enlightenment ideals) and differences (who was included vs. excluded, and what was preserved vs. overturned).

### Nationalism in Europe: Comparing Italian and German Unification

Both Cavour and Bismarck used **realpolitik** — both exploited wars for territorial gain — and both unified fragmented cultural regions into new nation-states. But their styles differed: Cavour relied more on diplomatic manipulation and incorporated revolutionary movements (Garibaldi, Mazzini); Bismarck relied primarily on Prussian military dominance and kept the revolutionary tradition subordinate to Prussian state power.

## Historical Reasoning: Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

**What changed:** The principle of dynastic legitimacy gave way to popular sovereignty and nationalism as the basis for political authority. Colonial relationships began to unravel across the Atlantic world. Two new great powers (Italy and Germany) emerged in Europe by 1871.

**What continued:** Social hierarchies proved remarkably resilient. Creole elites replaced peninsulares but retained land and power. Indigenous peoples and women remained politically marginalized across the new republics. Slavery persisted in the United States until 1865 and in Brazil until 1888, despite the revolutionary era's language of universal rights. The casta system (see [[Unit-4.7/Changing Social Hierarchies Overview|Changing Social Hierarchies Overview]]) lost legal standing in Latin America but persisted in practice.

## Historical Reasoning: Argumentation — The Limits of Revolution

One of the most exam-testable arguments about this period is the **gap between revolutionary ideals and revolutionary realities**. A strong AP essay would argue:

**Thesis:** While the Atlantic revolutions of 1750–1900 transformed political structures by replacing monarchical authority with popular sovereignty, they consistently excluded women, indigenous peoples, and the enslaved from the rights they proclaimed — revealing that revolutionary leaders sought to shift *who held power* rather than to fundamentally restructure social hierarchies.

**Evidence to support:**
- American Revolution preserved slavery (Jefferson enslaved 600+ people); women gained no political rights
- French Revolution proclaimed universal rights but denied them to enslaved people in colonies
- Latin American creoles excluded mestizos, indigenous peoples, and mulattoes from political power despite independence
- Only Haiti actually applied universal rights to enslaved people — and was punished by diplomatic isolation and forced debt to France

**Counterevidence to acknowledge:** The Haitian Revolution did abolish slavery; some Latin American constitutions outlawed it; the Declaration of Rights of Man inspired later feminist and abolitionist movements (connect to Olympe de Gouges and Wollstonecraft from [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment Overview]]).

## Think as a Historian: Comparing Arguments (p. 295–296)

AMSCO presents two contrasting arguments about American independence:

**John Dickinson (Continental Congress, July 1, 1776):** Against independence. Argued that Britain provided order and prevented "licentious anarchy." Used the body politic metaphor — the king as head who maintains unity. His claim: independence would lead to chaos, not liberty.

**Declaration of Independence (July 4, 1776):** For independence. Listed specific grievances (laws refused, assemblies dissolved, colonists taxed without consent). Concluded that colonies have the right to be free and independent states. Drew on Lockean natural rights language.

**Comparing these arguments:** Dickinson prioritized *stability* and *order*; Jefferson prioritized *rights* and *consent*. Both were arguing about the same set of facts (British actions) but reached opposite conclusions because they held different foundational assumptions about the purpose of government — a classic example of how the same evidence can support different claims.

> [!tip]
> The Dickinson/Jefferson comparison is a perfect Think as a Historian practice case. When the AP gives you two sources on the same topic, identify their *main claims*, find their *shared assumptions*, and then isolate *where they diverge* in their reasoning.
