# People, States & Movements

> [!exam-tip]
> Know each figure's role, ideology, and *limits* — the AP exam frequently pairs revolutionary leaders with questions about who benefited from their movements and who was left out.

---

## Revolutionary Leaders

**Thomas Jefferson** (1743–1826)
Principal author of the Declaration of Independence (1776), Jefferson borrowed John Locke's concept of "unalienable rights" to justify the American colonists' break from Britain. His ideals of natural rights and popular sovereignty provided a template for future revolutions, though Jefferson himself enslaved people — a foundational contradiction that the Haitian Revolution would expose. (AMSCO p. 285)

**Marquis de Lafayette** (1757–1834)
French noble and moderate early leader of the French Revolution; also a hero of the American Revolution. Represented the constitutionalist phase of the French Revolution before radical Jacobins took control. His involvement in both revolutions illustrates the transatlantic circulation of Enlightenment ideals.

**Maximilien Robespierre** (1758–1794)
Jacobin leader who dominated the radical phase of the French Revolution. As head of the Committee of Public Safety, he oversaw the Reign of Terror (1793–1794), executing thousands of perceived enemies of the revolution. Eventually arrested and guillotined himself. Robespierre represents the danger of revolutionary idealism turning into authoritarian violence. (AMSCO p. 286)

**Napoleon Bonaparte** (1769–1821)
Rose from military general to Emperor of France (1804). Napoleon spread revolutionary ideals — the Napoleonic Code, abolition of feudalism, religious tolerance — across Europe through conquest, but also undermined them by restoring monarchy and attempting to reimpose slavery in Haiti. His conquests paradoxically fueled nationalist backlash in territories he occupied, accelerating German and Italian nationalism. (AMSCO pp. 286, 291)

## The Haitian Revolution

**Toussaint L'Ouverture** (c. 1743–1803)
Formerly enslaved Haitian who became the military and political leader of the Haitian Revolution. Well-read in Enlightenment thought, L'Ouverture proved an exceptional general — playing France, Spain, and Britain against each other while cementing the abolition of slavery in Haiti. His 1801 constitution granted equality and citizenship to all residents and declared him governor-for-life. Betrayed and imprisoned by the French, he died in captivity in 1803. (AMSCO p. 287)

> [!exam-tip]
> L'Ouverture is the exam's favorite example of how Enlightenment ideas crossed racial boundaries — and how those who proclaimed "universal" rights refused to apply them to the enslaved. He applied Enlightenment philosophy more consistently than Jefferson or Robespierre.

**Jean-Jacques Dessalines** (c. 1758–1806)
L'Ouverture's successor, who in 1804 declared permanent Haitian independence from France. Under Dessalines, Haiti became the first Black-led republic in the Western Hemisphere and the only country born from a successful slave uprising. (AMSCO p. 287)

**Maroons**
Communities of people who had escaped slavery in Haiti (and across the Caribbean/Americas) and lived independently. They joined the enslaved Africans in the Haitian Revolution, providing experienced fighters. Their existence demonstrated ongoing resistance to slavery that predated the formal revolution. (AMSCO p. 287)

## Latin American Independence

**Simón Bolívar** (1783–1830)
Born into a wealthy creole family in Venezuela, Bolívar became the leading military and political figure of South American independence. His campaigns liberated Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru, briefly united in **Gran Colombia**. Bolívar's **"Jamaica Letter"** (1815) articulated Enlightenment-based arguments for Latin American [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]]: he described a liberal who believed in free markets and abolition of slavery. But Gran Colombia fractured, and Bolívar died frustrated by the fragmentation of his vision. (AMSCO pp. 288–289)

**Manuela Sáenz** (1797–1856)
One of the rare women who gained direct political and military agency during the Latin American independence era. An active participant in fighting alongside Bolívar, she rose to the rank of colonel and saved Bolívar's life on at least one occasion, earning the nickname "Liberator of the Liberator." Her career was the exception: most women gained little from the revolutions. (AMSCO p. 289)

**Caudillos**
Not a single figure but a political type that emerged after Latin American independence: strong regional leaders who commanded armies loyal to them personally rather than to constitutional governments. Caudillos undermined democracy and the rule of law across the new republics. They sometimes defended elite interests and sometimes championed indigenous or peasant causes, but consistently bypassed representative institutions. (AMSCO p. 289)

**Lola Rodríguez de Tió** (1843–1924)
Puerto Rican poet and revolutionary who used her writing to critique Spanish colonial rule. Her revolutionary rewrite of "La Borinqueña" called Puerto Ricans to fight for freedom. Her home became a meeting place for revolutionaries, and she was exiled to Venezuela after the 1868 uprising. Later exiled again for her Cuban independence advocacy — spending years in New York before returning to Cuba in 1898. A rare example of a woman's political leadership in 19th-century Latin America. (AMSCO p. 289)

## Philippine Revolution

**José Rizal** (1861–1896)
The most prominent figure of the Filipino **Propaganda Movement**, which called for greater Philippine autonomy from Spain through journalism and literature. Though the movement did not initially call for independence, Spanish authorities arrested Rizal in 1892 and executed him in 1896. His martyrdom shocked Filipinos and transformed the autonomy movement into the **Philippine Revolution** — the first serious nationalist military challenge to Spanish colonial rule. (AMSCO pp. 289–290)

## European Nationalist Leaders

**Count di Cavour** (Camillo Benso, 1810–1861)
Prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia and architect of Italian unification. A classical liberal who believed in natural rights and constitutional monarchy, Cavour pursued these goals through realpolitik rather than idealism. He maneuvered Napoleon III of France into war with Austria (1858), incorporated Mazzini's Risorgimento ideology, and allied with Garibaldi's forces in the south. Italian regions voted by plebiscite to join Piedmont, achieving unification. (AMSCO p. 290)

**Giuseppe Mazzini** (1805–1872)
Italian nationalist and romantic revolutionary who had been agitating for Italian unity (Risorgimento) since early in the 19th century. His philosophy was idealistic rather than political — he believed in cultural and moral renewal as the foundation of Italian nationhood. Cavour eventually adopted and redirected Mazzini's revolutionary energy for his own realpolitik program. (AMSCO p. 290)

**Giuseppe Garibaldi** (1807–1882)
Italian military leader who commanded the "Red Shirts" and fought for unification in the Kingdom of Naples and Sicily. Garibaldi allied with Cavour's political strategy, bringing the southern Italian peninsula into the unified Italian state. He represented the popular, bottom-up revolutionary force that complemented Cavour's top-down diplomacy. (AMSCO p. 290)

**Otto von Bismarck** (1815–1898)
Chancellor of Prussia and architect of German unification. Like Cavour, Bismarck favored realpolitik. He engineered three wars — against Denmark (1864), Austria (Seven Weeks' War, 1866), and France (Franco-Prussian War, 1870–71) — gaining territory with each victory. In 1871, he founded the **German Empire**, with the Prussian king as Kaiser. Bismarck's Germany added Alsace-Lorraine from France, creating a long-standing grievance. See also [[Unit-5.8/People, States & Movements|People, States & Movements]] for Bismarck's social welfare policies. (AMSCO pp. 291, 348)

## Maori and New Zealand

**Maori**
Polynesian inhabitants of New Zealand since at least the mid-1200s, organized into tribes (iwi) with a rich cultural tradition. After Britain formally annexed New Zealand in 1840, Maori tribes developed a sense of shared Maori nationalism and fought together in the New Zealand Wars (1840–1872) against British expansion. The British ultimately prevailed, but Maori national identity persisted. (AMSCO p. 286)
