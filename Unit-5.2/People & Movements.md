# Topic 5.2 — People & Movements

---

## Revolutionary Leaders

### Toussaint L'Ouverture (c. 1743–1803)
- Formerly enslaved on a Haitian plantation; self-educated; well-read in Enlightenment thought
- Joined slave revolt in 1791; became its military and political leader
- Played France, Spain, and Britain against each other with brilliant diplomacy and military skill
- Constitution of 1801: granted equality and citizenship to all residents; declared himself governor for life
- Also enacted **land reform**: plantations divided and distributed among formerly enslaved and free Black people
- Betrayed by France; imprisoned; died in France (1803) — but had cemented the abolition of slavery in Haiti
- **Legacy:** Set Haiti on the road to independence; symbol of enslaved peoples' capacity for self-governance

### Jean-Jacques Dessalines (1758–1806)
- L'Ouverture's successor; harsher military commander
- Declared Haitian independence **January 1, 1804** — named the country after the indigenous Taíno word for the island
- Haiti became the **first country in Latin America** to win independence and the **first Black-led country in the Western Hemisphere**

### Simón Bolívar (1783–1830)
- Born in Venezuela to a wealthy creole family with aristocratic Spanish ancestors
- Described himself as a "liberal who believed in a free market and the abolition of slavery"
- Military victories liberated Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru
- Formed **Gran Colombia** — a large federation he hoped would mirror the United States
- **"Jamaica Letter" (1815):** Articulated his vision for Latin American freedom; expressed frustration with Spanish colonialism
- Ultimately failed to hold Gran Colombia together; left power and died in 1830

> [!tip]
> Bolívar is often called "the Liberator" but his revolution had real limits: he feared mestizos, indigenous people, and mulattos taking power, and his governments were often authoritarian. "Liberty" for creoles did not mean equality for all.

### Manuela Saenz (1797–1856)
- Lover of Simón Bolívar; actively fought alongside him in battle
- Participated in the 1822 battle near Quito, Ecuador; rose to the rank of colonel
- Saved Bolívar's life on one occasion → earned the nickname **"Liberator of the Liberator"**
- Exceptional: in a revolution that gave women almost nothing, she carved out a genuine military and political role

### Count di Cavour (1810–1861)
- Prime Minister of Piedmont-Sardinia (the only native Italian dynasty — House of Savoy)
- Spoke French better than Italian; a classical liberal who also believed in realpolitik
- Maneuvered France into war with Austria (1858) to weaken Austrian influence on the Italian Peninsula
- Allied with Garibaldi's Red Shirts
- Died shortly after Italian unification was achieved

### Giuseppe Mazzini (1805–1872)
- Italian nationalist who had agitated for Italian **Risorgimento** (resurgence/rebirth) since the early 19th century
- Provided the romantic-nationalist ideology that Cavour later co-opted for practical politics
- Wanted a democratic Italian republic (not just unity under a monarchy)

### Giuseppe Garibaldi (1807–1882)
- Military leader of the Red Shirts
- Won military victories in the Kingdom of Naples (southern Italy)
- Accepted unity under the King of Piedmont rather than insisting on a republic — pragmatic decision that made unification possible

### Otto von Bismarck (1815–1898)
- Chancellor of Prussia; master of **realpolitik**
- Engineered 3 wars to unify Germany under Prussian leadership:
  1. Prussia + Austria vs. Denmark (1864)
  2. Prussia vs. Austria — Seven Weeks' War (1866)
  3. Prussia vs. France — Franco-Prussian War (1870)
- Founded the **German Empire (1871)**; became its first Chancellor
- Later designed social welfare programs (accident insurance, unemployment insurance, old age pensions) to undercut socialist support — see [[Unit-5.10/Overview]]

## Key Movements

### The Propaganda Movement (Philippines)
- Young Filipino students traveling to universities in Madrid and Barcelona encountered European nationalism and republicanism in the 1880s
- Returned with ideas about autonomy for the Philippines
- **José Rizal**: most prominent leader; contributed to magazines and pamphlets calling for greater Philippine autonomy (NOT full independence)
- Spanish authorities arrested Rizal (1892) and executed him (1896)
- Rizal's execution shocked Filipinos → sparked the **Philippine Revolution (1896)** — first serious challenge to Spanish rule

### Lola Rodriguez de Tié and Puerto Rican Resistance
- Recognized poet in an era of little educational opportunity for women
- Wrote "La Borinqueña" — a revolutionary song urging Puerto Ricans to fight for freedom
- Exiled multiple times: first to Venezuela, then to Cuba, then to New York
- Campaigned for social justice throughout her life

### The Maroons (Haiti)
- Escaped enslaved people who had established free communities in the Haitian mountains
- Joined the slave revolt of 1791; crucial early force in the Haitian Revolution
- Symbol of resistance to slavery long before L'Ouverture organized the broader rebellion

## Social Groups to Know

| Group | Definition | Role in Revolutions |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Creoles** | People of European ancestry born in the Americas | Led Latin American independence movements; resented peninsulares |
| **Peninsulares** | People born in Spain or Portugal | Held key colonial government jobs; targets of creole resentment |
| **Mestizos** | People of European + indigenous ancestry | Wanted political power; sometimes allied with creoles |
| **Mulattoes** | People of African + European or indigenous ancestry | Bottom of racial hierarchy in Latin America (above enslaved people) |
| **Maroons** | Escaped enslaved people who formed free communities | Key fighters in Haitian Revolution |
| **Caudillos** | Strong local military leaders with regional power bases | Stepped in after Latin American independence; undermined democracy and rule of law |
