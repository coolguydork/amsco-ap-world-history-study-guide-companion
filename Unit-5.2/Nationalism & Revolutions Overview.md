# Nationalism & Revolutions

**AP Themes:** `GOV` · `CDI` · `SIO`
**Essential Question:** What were the causes and effects of the various revolutions in the period from 1750 to 1900, including influences of the Enlightenment and emerging nationalism?

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the causes and limits of the Atlantic revolutions — especially how Enlightenment ideas spread unevenly and how revolutions excluded women, indigenous peoples, and the enslaved. Know all four major revolutions and their key figures cold.

## The Big Picture

Between 1750 and 1900, Enlightenment ideas about natural rights, popular sovereignty, and representative government ignited a wave of revolutions across the Atlantic world and beyond. While these revolutions challenged traditional monarchies and colonial rule, they also revealed deep limits: most new governments continued to exclude women, indigenous peoples, and enslaved Africans from the rights they proclaimed. Simultaneously, nationalism — the sense of shared identity based on language, culture, and history — drove movements for political unification in Europe, challenging large multi-ethnic empires.

---

## The American Revolution (1776–1783)

The American colonists combined Enlightenment ideology with practical economic and political grievances. Enlightenment philosophy — especially John Locke's ideas about natural rights and government by consent — gave the revolution its intellectual foundation, while opposition to British mercantilism and taxation without representation supplied the economic fuel. The **Declaration of Independence** (July 4, 1776), drafted by Thomas Jefferson, declared that all men possessed "unalienable rights" to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. With crucial military aid from France, the colonists won independence in 1783.

> [!tip]
> The American Revolution was the first successful colonial revolution against a European power, but it was limited: it preserved slavery and excluded women from political rights. Connect to 5.1 Enlightenment ideas and forward to 8.5 decolonization movements.

---

## The New Zealand Wars (1840–1872)

The Maori, Polynesian inhabitants of New Zealand since at least the mid-1200s, developed rich tribal cultures before European contact. Britain formally annexed New Zealand in 1840, intensifying pressure on Maori land. The resulting **New Zealand Wars** saw Maori tribes develop a sense of shared Maori nationalism as they fought together against British forces — but by 1872, the British had prevailed.

## The French Revolution (1789–1804)

France's revolution grew from both ideology and economic crisis. The government's chronic debt — worsened by financing the American Revolution — forced King Louis XVI to convene the **Estates-General** in spring 1789. When the commoners (representing 97% of French society) were outvoted by the clergy and nobility, they broke away to form the **National Assembly**. On July 14, 1789, Parisian crowds stormed the **Bastille**, a fortress-prison that symbolized royal tyranny. The early revolution produced two lasting achievements: abolition of feudalism and the **Declaration of the Rights of Man**, which proclaimed basic human rights under the slogan *liberté, égalité, et fraternité*.

The radical **Jacobins**, led by Robespierre, executed the king and thousands of opponents in the **Reign of Terror** (1792–1794). After years of turmoil, Napoleon Bonaparte became Emperor of France in 1804 — both spreading and ultimately undermining revolutionary ideals across Europe through his conquests.

> [!warning]
> The French Revolution is often confused with a steady march toward democracy. In fact it lurched between constitutionalism, radical terror, and military dictatorship before Napoleon took power. The exam may ask you to identify which *phase* of the revolution is being described.

## The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)

The Haitian Revolution is unique in world history: the only successful revolution led by enslaved people. On the French colony of Saint-Domingue, enslaved Africans began killing masters and burning plantations. They were joined by **Maroons** — those who had already escaped slavery. Inspired by the French Revolution and well-versed in Enlightenment thought, **Toussaint L'Ouverture** joined the revolt in 1791 and led a general rebellion. His army skillfully played France, Spain, and Britain against each other.

In 1801, L'Ouverture produced a constitution granting equality and citizenship to all residents and declared himself governor-for-life. He also initiated land reform, distributing former plantations to formerly enslaved and free Black people. The French betrayed and imprisoned him; he died in 1803. His successor, **Jean-Jacques Dessalines**, declared permanent Haitian independence in 1804. Haiti became the first Black-led republic in the Western Hemisphere and the only country born from a successful slave uprising.

> [!exam-tip]
> Haiti is the essential comparison case for the limits of revolution. The French and American revolutions proclaimed universal rights but preserved slavery. Only Haiti actually abolished it through revolution — a powerful point for DBQ and LEQ arguments about revolutionary change and continuity.

## Creole Revolutions in Latin America (c. 1810–1830s)

On the Latin American mainland, independence movements were led by **creoles** — people of European ancestry born in the Americas. Educated and influenced by Enlightenment ideas, creoles resented both Spain's mercantilism and peninsulares' hold on high government posts. Below creoles in the social hierarchy sat mestizos (European and indigenous ancestry), mulattoes (African and European or indigenous ancestry), and at the very bottom, indigenous peoples and the enslaved.

**Simón Bolívar**, born in Venezuela in 1783 into a wealthy creole family, became the leading voice for South American independence. His **"Jamaica Letter"** (1815) articulated Enlightenment-based arguments for Latin American self-determination. Bolívar's military campaigns liberated Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, and Peru, briefly united in **Gran Colombia**. However, the new nations were weakened by long wars of independence and the rise of **caudillos** — regional strongmen loyal to their armies rather than constitutional government.

The **limits of the creole revolutions** were significant. Despite constitutions that legally ended slavery and some social distinctions, conservative creole elites maintained power. Indigenous peoples were effectively disenfranchised by literacy requirements. Women remained legally subordinate — unable to vote or enter contracts — with rare exceptions like **Manuela Sáenz** (1797–1856), who fought alongside Bolívar and rose to the rank of colonel, earning the nickname "Liberator of the Liberator."

## Later Challenges to Spanish Colonialism

Spain's empire faced challenges throughout the 19th century. In **Puerto Rico** and **Cuba**, uprisings against Spanish rule began in 1868. Poet and revolutionary **Lola Rodríguez de Tió** used her writing to call Puerto Ricans to resist Spanish exploitation; her "La Borinqueña" became an anthem of revolt. She was exiled repeatedly for her activism.

In the **Philippines**, young educated Filipinos studying in Europe absorbed nationalist and republican ideas. **José Rizal** led the **Propaganda Movement**, which called for greater autonomy (not yet outright independence). His arrest in 1892 and execution in 1896 shocked Filipinos and sparked the **Philippine Revolution** — the first organized military challenge to Spanish colonial rule.

## Nationalism and Unification in Europe

Nationalism — the idea that people sharing a language, culture, and history should form one political state — threatened multi-ethnic empires and drove unification movements. Napoleon's conquests had paradoxically spread both revolutionary ideals and nationalist backlash across Europe.

**Italian Unification:** Count di **Cavour**, prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia, pursued unification through **realpolitik** — practical political manipulation rather than idealism. In 1858 he maneuvered France into a war with Austria to weaken Austrian control over the Italian Peninsula. He allied with **Giuseppe Garibaldi's** "Red Shirts" in the south and incorporated the revolutionary vision of **Giuseppe Mazzini** (Risorgimento — Italian resurgence). Regions voted by plebiscite to join Piedmont, completing Italian unification by the early 1860s–1870s.

**German Unification:** Prussian chancellor **Otto von Bismarck** used realpolitik to engineer three wars: against Denmark (1864), Austria (Seven Weeks' War, 1866), and France (Franco-Prussian War, 1870–71). Prussia won all three, gaining territory including Alsace-Lorraine from France. In 1871, Bismarck founded the **German Empire** — a powerful new state that would reshape European balance of power.

**Balkan Nationalism:** Ottoman decline opened space for nationalist movements among subject peoples. Greek nationalism combined Enlightenment ideas with reverence for ancient Greek culture. Greek independence was secured in 1827 with British, French, and Russian naval support. Serbia, Bulgaria, and Romania followed similar paths. The Ottomans responded with **Ottomanism** — standardizing curriculum and minimizing ethnic/religious differences — but this paradoxically intensified subject peoples' desire for independence.

> [!exam-tip]
> Both Cavour (Italy) and Bismarck (Germany) exemplify **realpolitik** — the use of practical power politics rather than idealistic principles to achieve national goals. The AP exam loves comparing these two figures as case studies in nationalism and state-building.

## Why It Matters

The revolutions of 1750–1900 permanently transformed the political landscape of the Atlantic world and beyond. Popular sovereignty and nationalism replaced divine right and dynastic loyalty as the foundations of political legitimacy. Yet the gap between revolutionary ideals and revolutionary realities — the exclusion of women, indigenous peoples, and the enslaved — became a defining tension that would drive future movements for rights and independence well into the 20th century. The nationalist movements that unified Italy and Germany set the stage for the alliance competition that led directly to World War I.
