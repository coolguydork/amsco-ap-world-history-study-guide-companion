# Key Terms — Unit 5.2: Nationalism and Revolutions

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Nationalism** — The political ideology that holds that people sharing a common language, culture, history, or ethnicity should form and govern their own independent state. (Became the dominant political force of the 19th century; drove both liberation movements and the suppression of minority groups within multiethnic empires.)

2. **American Revolution (1776–1783)** — The successful colonial revolt of thirteen British North American colonies, producing the world's first modern republic based on Enlightenment principles of natural rights and popular sovereignty. (Showed that colonial peoples could successfully revolt against European empires, inspiring later Atlantic revolutions.)

3. **French Revolution (1789–1799)** — The radical political and social transformation of France that abolished feudalism and absolute monarchy, declared universal rights of man, and eventually executed King Louis XVI; followed by the Reign of Terror and Napoleon's rise. (Most influential revolution in world history in terms of spreading democratic ideals and nationalist politics across Europe.)

4. **Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)** — The only successful slave revolt in history to produce an independent state; enslaved Africans in Saint-Domingue (French Caribbean) overthrew French colonial rule, abolished slavery, and established the Republic of Haiti — the first Black republic in the Western Hemisphere. (The most radical Atlantic Revolution; terrified slaveholders globally; directly resulted in France selling the Louisiana Territory to the USA.)

5. **Toussaint Louverture (1743–1803)** — Leader of the Haitian Revolution; a formerly enslaved man who organized enslaved people into a military force, invoked Enlightenment natural rights language, and negotiated with France before being captured by Napoleon; died in French prison. (His leadership and use of Enlightenment rhetoric illustrate how marginalized peoples appropriated the ideology of their oppressors.)

6. **Creole** — In the Latin American context, people of Spanish or Portuguese descent born in the Americas (not in Europe). (Creoles led most Latin American independence movements but generally preserved existing racial and social hierarchies after independence.)

7. **Simón Bolívar (1783–1830)** — Venezuelan-born creole leader who liberated much of northern South America from Spanish rule; dreamed of a united Latin American republic; known as "El Libertador." (His career illustrates both the power of nationalist ideas and their limits — his vision of pan-Latin American unity failed as regional interests prevailed.)

8. **José de San Martín (1778–1850)** — Argentine-born creole general who liberated Argentina, Chile, and Peru from Spanish rule in the 1810s–1820s. (Worked in coordination with Bolívar; together they ended Spanish colonial rule in South America.)

9. **Popular sovereignty** — The principle that political authority derives from and is exercised by the people. (Core Enlightenment concept embedded in all Atlantic revolutionary documents; challenged divine right monarchy and aristocratic privilege.)

10. **Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (1789)** — French revolutionary document proclaiming the rights of liberty, equality, and popular sovereignty; influenced by the American Declaration of Independence and Enlightenment natural rights theory. (Became a model for subsequent revolutionary movements; also notable for what it excluded — women, enslaved people, colonized subjects.)

11. **Reign of Terror (1793–1794)** — The most radical phase of the French Revolution, during which Maximilien Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety executed ~17,000 "enemies of the revolution." (Illustrates the danger of unchecked popular sovereignty; also illustrates the revolution's internal contradictions.)

12. **Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–1821)** — French military leader who rose from the Revolution, became Emperor (1804), and spread revolutionary legal codes (Napoleonic Code) across Europe while simultaneously suppressing democratic politics. (His conquests inadvertently spread nationalist ideas across Europe by demonstrating that national identity could be a powerful political force.)

13. **Napoleonic Code** — The legal code established by Napoleon (1804) that standardized laws across France and its territories, establishing legal equality, property rights, and secular government — while restricting women's rights. (Spread across Europe; shaped modern legal systems; illustrates how revolutions can both advance and limit rights.)

14. **Nationalism in Europe** — 19th-century movements seeking national self-determination, particularly in Greece (independence 1821–1830), Italy (unification 1861), and Germany (unification 1871), as well as Polish and Hungarian nationalist revolts. (Nationalism reshaped the map of Europe and eventually contributed to World War I as nationalist tensions within multinational empires exploded.)

15. **Liberalism (19th century)** — The political ideology that emphasized individual rights, constitutional government, free markets, and limited government interference; the political creed of the educated middle class. (Distinct from later 20th-century "liberalism"; 19th-century liberalism was often hostile to both aristocratic privilege and working-class radicalism.)

16. **Conservatism (19th century)** — The political ideology that emphasized tradition, hierarchy, religion, and gradual reform over revolution; championed by figures like Edmund Burke (*Reflections on the Revolution in France*, 1790) in response to the French Revolution. (The French Revolution's excesses produced conservative ideology as a systematic intellectual reaction.)

17. **Greek Independence (1821–1830)** — Successful nationalist revolt against Ottoman rule; the first successful European nationalist independence movement of the 19th century; gained international sympathy partly because of classical Greek cultural prestige. (Demonstrates nationalism as a political force; also demonstrates Great Power competition in the Ottoman Empire's decline.)

> [!exam-tip]
> AP exam questions on this topic frequently ask you to **compare** two or more Atlantic Revolutions — focusing on who participated, what Enlightenment ideas were applied, what was changed, and what was NOT changed (especially regarding slavery, women, and social hierarchy).
