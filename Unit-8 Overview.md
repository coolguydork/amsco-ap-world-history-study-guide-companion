**AP Period:** Period 4 — c. 1900 to the present
**Unit Theme:** *Cold War and Decolonization* — World War II destroyed the old colonial order and created two new worlds simultaneously: a superpower rivalry that divided the globe ideologically for 45 years, and a wave of independence that created more than 100 new nations from formerly colonized peoples.

---

## The Big Picture

Unit 8 covers roughly 1945 to 1991 — the Cold War era — but its roots reach back into Unit 7. WWII is the hinge: it killed 40–60 million people, annihilated European power, and elevated two ideologically incompatible superpowers whose rivalry would define global politics until 1991. The same war that created the Cold War also made decolonization inevitable — colonial powers emerged too economically devastated to sustain far-flung empires against determined independence movements.

The Cold War was never a "hot" war between the superpowers. Nuclear deterrence — **Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD)** — made direct US-Soviet conflict suicidal. Instead, they competed through proxy wars (Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Nicaragua), alliance systems ([[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]] vs. Warsaw Pact), arms races, and ideological propaganda. The hot violence of the Cold War fell almost entirely on the developing world. An estimated 40 million people died in Cold War proxy conflicts — none of them Americans or Soviets fighting each other.

Decolonization unfolded simultaneously. Within roughly three decades after 1945, the entire map of Africa and Asia was redrawn. More than 100 new nations emerged from the ruins of the British, French, Dutch, Belgian, and Portuguese empires. Independence was rarely given — it was won, through mass civil disobedience (India), armed struggle (Algeria, Vietnam), or diplomatic pressure (Ghana). But independence did not automatically bring stability: colonial borders that ignored ethnic realities, economies oriented toward extraction rather than development, and Cold War superpower interference created chronic instability in newly independent states.

For the AP exam, Unit 8's central argument is **causation and comparison**: understanding what caused the Cold War and decolonization, how superpower rivalry shaped both, and how effects varied across regions. The essential comparison is the Eastern vs. Western Hemisphere — both experienced proxy wars, superpower interference, and economic dependency, but the Eastern Hemisphere bore a disproportionate military cost, while Latin America and others navigated Cold War pressures against a backdrop of earlier independence. Master that comparison and you can answer virtually any Unit 8 LEQ or DBQ.

## Topic-by-Topic Narrative

### 8.1 — Setting the Stage for the Cold War & Decolonization

World War II produced two simultaneous transformations. First, it created the conditions for the Cold War: the wartime alliance between the US and USSR dissolved almost immediately after Germany's defeat, as their ideological incompatibility — liberal democracy vs. communist one-party rule — proved impossible to bridge. The Yalta (1945) and Potsdam (1945) conferences revealed escalating mutual distrust over Eastern Europe, where Soviet forces were already in position and Stalin was installing compliant governments. By 1947, the wartime partners had become adversaries.

Second, WWII made decolonization structurally inevitable. Britain, France, and the Netherlands emerged economically devastated, lacking the resources to suppress determined independence movements. The war had also dealt a decisive ideological blow to colonialism: colonial powers who had fought fascism in the name of freedom could not indefinitely deny that same freedom to their own colonial subjects. Japanese victories over European forces in Asia (Singapore, Indochina) shattered the myth of European invincibility, emboldening independence movements across the Pacific and Indian Ocean worlds.

The 1947 independence of India and Pakistan — the first major break in European empire — demonstrated that the colonial system could be dismantled and that independence could be won through mass organization rather than purely armed rebellion. It was a signal to independence movements from Ghana to Vietnam: the age of empire was ending.

> [!exam-tip]
> WWII is not just "before Unit 8" — it is the **structural cause** of both the Cold War and decolonization. AP exam questions that ask you to explain the origins of either phenomenon require you to start here: WWII weakened colonial powers AND elevated incompatible superpowers AND provided ideological ammunition for anti-colonialism ([[Concept Notes/Self-Determination|self-determination]], Atlantic Charter).

> [!tip]
> **Useful comparison:** After WWI, the US retreated into isolationism. After WWII, it committed globally — Marshall Plan, [[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]], Truman Doctrine. Why? The atomic bomb, the Soviet threat, and the lesson of appeasement all made non-engagement feel more dangerous than engagement.

### 8.2 — The Cold War

The Cold War's origins lay in an ideological incompatibility that predated WWII. The US championed capitalism and liberal democracy; the USSR championed communism and one-party authoritarian rule. These systems could coexist while fighting a common enemy but could not cooperate once the enemy was defeated. By 1947, the outlines of Cold War competition were clear.

The US responded to Soviet expansion with **containment** — the strategy, articulated by diplomat George Kennan, of preventing communism from spreading beyond its existing foothold without necessarily rolling it back. Two landmark initiatives operationalized containment: the **Truman Doctrine** (1947), pledging US support to countries resisting communist takeover, and the **Marshall Plan** (1948), providing $13 billion to rebuild Western European economies on the logic that economic instability breeds revolution.

The USSR created its own economic bloc — **COMECON** — to bind Eastern European satellite states to Soviet planning. The competition intensified into an arms race and Space Race that consumed enormous resources on both sides. By the 1960s, both nations had enough nuclear weapons to destroy civilization, producing the doctrine of **Mutual Assured Destruction (MAD)**: since any first strike guaranteed annihilation of both sides, neither could rationally attack first. The balance of terror became the Cold War's de facto peace mechanism.

Many newly independent African and Asian nations refused to align with either superpower. The 1955 Bandung Conference and the subsequent **Non-Aligned Movement** represented an attempt to leverage Cold War competition for national benefit — playing superpowers against each other to extract aid, investment, and diplomatic recognition without submitting to either ideological camp.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Marshall Plan is a classic AP example of **economic statecraft** — using financial aid as a tool of foreign policy. The US wasn't being purely altruistic; rebuilding Europe served American strategic interests by preventing communist revolutions in economically devastated countries.

> [!warning]
> The Cold War was **ideological** as much as strategic. Don't reduce it to a simple power struggle — both sides genuinely believed their system was superior and worth spreading globally.

### 8.3 — Effects of the Cold War

Because direct superpower conflict risked nuclear annihilation, the Cold War produced a pattern of **proxy wars** — conflicts where the US and USSR supported opposing sides without fighting each other. The hot violence fell on the developing world: Korea (3–4 million casualties, 1950–1953), Vietnam (approximately 4 million deaths, 1955–1975), Angola (decades of civil war), Nicaragua (the Contra War). In each case, local national and social conflicts were overlaid with superpower rivalry, making them simultaneously decolonization struggles and Cold War competitions.

Both superpowers constructed formal alliance systems that divided the world militarily. [[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]] (1949) bound Western Europe and North America into a collective defense pact; the Warsaw Pact (1955) formalized Soviet control over Eastern Europe. The US added regional alliances (SEATO, CENTO) to encircle the Soviet bloc with military partners — a direct expression of containment strategy.

The most dangerous moment came during the **Cuban Missile Crisis** (October 1962): Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba, 90 miles from Florida, brought the superpowers to the nuclear brink for 13 days before diplomatic back-channels produced a resolution. The crisis produced the first concrete arms control steps — the 1963 Hot Line and Nuclear Test-Ban Treaty — demonstrating that even implacable adversaries could cooperate when mutual extinction was the alternative.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Cuban Missile Crisis is a top AP example of both Cold War nuclear tension AND Cold War cooperation. The **post-crisis arms control agreements** are as important as the crisis itself — they show that adversarial states can still negotiate when deterrence logic demands it.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse proxy wars with decolonization wars — they often overlapped. Vietnam's war against France was a decolonization struggle; the American phase was a Cold War proxy conflict. Local causes (land reform, anti-colonialism) mixed with superpower ideology in almost every proxy conflict.

### 8.4 — Spread of Communism After 1900

The spread of communism was inseparable from the legacy of colonialism. Across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, colonial rule had produced deep land inequality — a small class of wealthy landlords over a vast landless peasantry. Communist movements offered a simple, powerful appeal: redistribute the land. Where governments were too weak or corrupt to reform themselves, communism found receptive audiences.

**China** was the central case. Mao Zedong's victory in the Chinese Civil War (1949) brought the world's most populous nation into the communist bloc, fundamentally shifting the Cold War's global balance. But Mao's China diverged from the Soviet model in catastrophic ways. The **Great Leap Forward** (1958–1962) — forced agricultural collectivization combined with backyard steel production — caused a famine killing an estimated 15–45 million people. The **Cultural Revolution** (1966–1976) mobilized youth as Red Guards to purge intellectuals and party officials, paralyzing Chinese education and professional life for a decade.

Elsewhere, the pattern repeated: **Cuba's 1959 revolution** under Fidel Castro brought communism to the Western Hemisphere 90 miles from Florida; CIA-backed coups overthrew elected reformers in **Guatemala** (1954) and **Iran** (1953) to prevent land reforms the US associated with communism; **Vietnam's** communist North defeated the South in 1975 after the US withdrawal. Each case shows Cold War superpower competition amplifying local social conflicts into geopolitical crises.

> [!exam-tip]
> The core AP causation argument: communism spread because of (1) land inequality from colonialism, (2) peasant support for redistribution, and (3) Cold War superpower competition. Practice framing **land reform** as the mechanism by which communism gained mass support.

> [!warning]
> China and the Soviet Union became **rivals**, not allies, from the early 1960s. Sino-Soviet border disputes and competition for influence in the Third World characterized their relationship. Never assume all communist states were a unified bloc.

### 8.5 — Decolonization After 1900

Decolonization was one of the defining global transformations of the 20th century. Within roughly three decades after WWII, dozens of new nations emerged across Africa and Asia as European empires collapsed under combined pressure from nationalist movements, Cold War politics, and economic exhaustion.

The paths to independence varied dramatically, and the form of independence shaped the nature of the new state. **India** (1947) achieved independence through [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]]'s mass civil disobedience but at the cost of partition into India and Pakistan — triggering catastrophic communal violence that displaced 10–20 million people and killed 500,000–1 million. **Ghana** (1957) negotiated independence peacefully under [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanist]] leader Kwame Nkrumah, becoming a model for sub-Saharan Africa and proclaiming a vision of continental African unity. **Algeria** fought a brutal eight-year war (1954–1962) against France, one of the bloodiest decolonization conflicts, leaving deep social wounds. **Vietnam's** independence struggle became entangled with the Cold War, drawing in first French and then American military intervention for three decades.

The Cold War gave nationalist movements unexpected leverage. Both superpowers publicly opposed European colonialism (for different ideological reasons), and newly independent states could play them against each other to extract aid and recognition. The [[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]] provided a diplomatic forum that amplified independence movements' voices and delegitimized colonialism in international law.

> [!exam-tip]
> Don't say decolonization was simply "given" by colonial powers. In most cases, colonial powers resisted — independence was won, not granted. The key factors were: WWII weakened empires, [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Charter\|Atlantic Charter]] self-determination rhetoric was weaponized by colonial subjects, and Cold War competition gave nationalists diplomatic leverage.

### 8.6 — Newly Independent States

Independence created new states but inherited old problems. Colonial boundary-drawing had deliberately cut across ethnic, religious, and linguistic lines — and when colonial powers departed, those contradictions exploded. The core AP argument: **decolonization created new states but inherited colonial structures** that made political stability and economic development profoundly difficult.

The **Middle East** became the era's most persistent conflict zone. The UN partition of Palestine (1947) and Israel's declaration of independence (1948) produced four Arab-Israeli wars between 1948 and 1973, Palestinian refugee crises, and ongoing territorial disputes. The 1978 **Camp David Accords** produced the first Arab-Israeli peace treaty (between Israel and Egypt only), but the Palestinian question remained unresolved.

**India and Pakistan's** partition imposed catastrophic costs on millions. Both nations claimed Kashmir; both eventually developed nuclear weapons; the rivalry produced multiple wars. South Asia also produced the world's first two female heads of government — Sirimavo Bandaranaike (Sri Lanka, 1960) and Indira Gandhi (India, 1966) — demonstrating that formal democracy in newly independent states could enable women's political leadership even in societies with deeply patriarchal traditions.

**Cambodia** illustrates the worst-case scenario: Cold War destabilization enabled the Khmer Rouge [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|genocide]] of 1975–1979, killing approximately 1.5–2 million people — one-quarter of Cambodia's population — in the "killing fields." Vietnam invaded in 1979 to stop the massacres, creating its own complex geopolitical entanglement.

> [!exam-tip]
> **The AP exam regularly asks for comparison of mass atrocities.** The Cambodian genocide connects to the Holocaust [[Unit-7.8/Mass Atrocities Overview\|Mass Atrocities Overview]] and to the Rwandan Genocide (Unit 9). Know the pattern: state-directed killing of a defined group, use of administrative capacity, and the international community's failure to intervene promptly.

> [!warning]
> Partition is a recurring AP comparison prompt. The India/Pakistan partition (1947) parallels the UN partition of Palestine (1947): both involved a European power drawing lines that ignored demographic realities, creating refugee crises and lasting territorial conflicts.

### 8.7 — Global Resistance to Established Power Structures

The 20th century was an age of resistance as well as an age of war. People challenged power through radically different means — Gandhi's nonviolent fasts, MLK's marches, student revolts in Paris and Prague, and terrorist campaigns in Belfast and Lima. What unites these movements is the AP's central question: how and why did individuals and groups challenge authority, and with what results?

**Nonviolent resistance** proved remarkably effective when leaders could generate international media attention and shame regimes that cared about their global image. [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Gandhi's]] model in India directly influenced **Martin Luther King Jr.'s** US Civil Rights Movement (1955–1968), which won the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act through marches, boycotts, and disciplined mass civil disobedience. **Nelson Mandela** and the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|African National Congress]] applied similar strategies against South African apartheid, with Mandela serving 27 years in prison before becoming South Africa's first democratically elected Black president in 1994.

**1968** was the single most explosive year of global resistance in the 20th century. Student uprisings erupted simultaneously in France, Czechoslovakia, the US, Mexico, Japan, Poland, and elsewhere — driven by shared frustrations about Vietnam, authoritarianism, and inequality. The simultaneity is itself significant: it reflects the growing **transnational consciousness** of a post-war generation connected by television and shared political culture.

**Eastern European reform movements** tested the limits of Soviet tolerance. The **Hungarian Revolution** (1956) and the **Prague Spring** (1968) were both crushed by Soviet tanks, demonstrating the ceiling on reform within the Soviet bloc — and marking that ceiling as the key variable Gorbachev would later abandon.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves comparing **methods of resistance**: nonviolent (Gandhi, MLK, Mandela) vs. armed/terrorist (IRA, Shining Path, al-Qaeda). Be able to explain why different contexts produced different strategies. Key variable: did the oppressive regime care about international opinion? If yes, nonviolence had leverage. If no, armed resistance was more likely.

### 8.8 — End of the Cold War

The Cold War ended not with a bang but with a collapse. Three interconnected causes drove the Soviet implosion: a fundamentally broken command economy, military overextension in Afghanistan, and reform policies by Mikhail Gorbachev that unleashed forces he could not control.

**Détente** in the 1970s temporarily stabilized superpower relations: Nixon's 1972 visit to Moscow produced SALT I (capping nuclear arsenals); Nixon's simultaneous visit to China exploited the Sino-Soviet split to pressure Moscow. But détente collapsed in 1979 when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan. The **Soviet-Afghan War** (1979–1989) became the USSR's Vietnam: a guerrilla war the modern army could not win, draining resources and demoralizing the military while the CIA armed Afghan mujahideen fighters.

**Gorbachev** came to power in 1985 as a reformer who believed the Soviet system could be saved if modernized. **Perestroika** (economic restructuring) and **glasnost** (political openness) were meant to revitalize socialism — instead, they destabilized it. Once citizens could criticize the government, decades of suppressed grievance poured out across the Soviet empire. Gorbachev abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine, signaling that Soviet troops would no longer intervene to preserve Eastern European communist governments. Eastern Europeans had waited for this signal for three decades.

In 1989, democratic revolutions swept Eastern Europe with startling speed: Poland's Solidarity movement brought Lech Wałęsa to the presidency; the **Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989**; Germany reunited in 1990. On December 25, 1991, the Soviet Union formally dissolved into 15 independent states. The Cold War was over.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam's most common trap: attributing the Soviet collapse to any **single cause**. Know at least three: (1) Soviet economic failure and military overextension (Afghanistan), (2) Gorbachev's reforms that backfired, (3) sustained US pressure via the Reagan arms buildup and SDI. The correct argument is that all three were necessary; none was sufficient alone.

### 8.9 — Causation in the Age of the Cold War and Decolonization

[[Unit-8.9/Causation in the Age of the Cold War & Decolonization Overview|Topic 8.9]] is the unit's synthesis chapter. AMSCO uses it to pull Unit 8 together around one central question: in what ways did the Cold War produce similar or different effects across the Eastern and Western Hemispheres?

The answer is the foundation of every Unit 8 LEQ. **Similarities** across hemispheres: both experienced superpower interference, proxy wars, and the economic dependency of former colonies on their former colonizers. **Differences**: the Eastern Hemisphere bore a disproportionate military cost (Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, Angola), while Latin America's Cold War was fought primarily through coups, elections, and economic pressure rather than full-scale wars. Western Europe, rebuilt by the Marshall Plan, became economically prosperous; the Third World generally did not.

The broader lesson is that Cold War effects were filtered through local conditions — colonial history, ethnic makeup, economic structure, and the strength of civil society — in ways that produced very different outcomes in seemingly similar situations. Understanding these variations is what AP causation and comparison questions are actually testing.

## AP Exam Strategy

### Highest-Frequency LEQ Prompts for Unit 8

- Explain the causes of the Cold War
- Explain the causes and/or effects of decolonization after 1900
- Compare independence movements across two regions (e.g., India vs. Algeria; Ghana vs. Vietnam)
- Evaluate the extent to which the Cold War produced similar effects in the Eastern and Western Hemispheres
- Explain the causes of the end of the Cold War
- Compare methods of resistance to established power structures after 1900

### Top Causal Chains

1. **WWII → Cold War:** Soviet occupation of Eastern Europe + US atomic monopoly + ideological incompatibility → mutual distrust → containment + Soviet expansion → Cold War
2. **WWII → Decolonization:** European economic devastation + self-determination rhetoric + weakened military capacity → colonial independence movements gain leverage → 100+ new nations 1945–1975
3. **Colonial inequality → Communism spreads:** Land inequality from colonialism + failed government reform + Cold War superpower competition → communist land reform appeal → China, Vietnam, Cuba
4. **Soviet overextension → Cold War end:** Afghan quagmire + economic stagnation + Gorbachev reforms + Reagan pressure → glasnost/perestroika backfire → Eastern European revolutions → Soviet collapse
