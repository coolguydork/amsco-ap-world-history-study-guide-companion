# Key Terms & Concepts

*8 terms | AMSCO pp. 608–614*

---

**détente**
Period of relaxed Cold War tensions (roughly 1969–1979) under Nixon/Kissinger and Brezhnev, characterized by SALT I, Nixon's opening to China, and increased trade. Ended by the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979). Détente represents an attempt to *manage* the Cold War rather than win it — both sides found restraint temporarily useful when facing other pressures. On the AP exam, contrast détente with the Reagan-era confrontational approach that preceded Soviet collapse.

**Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty (SALT)**
US-Soviet arms control agreements (SALT I, 1972; SALT II, 1979) that froze or capped nuclear delivery vehicles — the first major bilateral nuclear arms control agreements. SALT I was a product of détente and Nixon's 1972 Moscow summit. SALT II was never ratified by the US Senate because the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan (1979). Distinguish SALT from the later [[Concept Notes/NATO\|INF Treaty]]: SALT capped weapons, while INF *eliminated* an entire class.

**perestroika**
Gorbachev's economic "restructuring" policy introduced in 1985, which attempted to reform the Soviet command economy by allowing limited private enterprise and decentralizing planning. The reforms were too partial to fix the economy but sufficient to destabilize the existing system — a classic case of an authoritarian state weakened by halfway reforms. AP essays frequently ask why the Soviet Union collapsed; perestroika's unintended consequences are a key part of the answer.

**glasnost**
Gorbachev's policy of political "openness" introduced alongside perestroika. By allowing freedom of expression and reducing censorship, glasnost unleashed long-suppressed public criticism of the Soviet government. Once people could speak freely about the system's failures — economic stagnation, Afghanistan casualties, Chernobyl cover-up — they demanded more than reform. Glasnost is why Gorbachev's reforms backfired: political openness without economic success is destabilizing.

**Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)**
Reagan's 1983 proposal for a space-based missile defense system to intercept Soviet ballistic missiles. Nicknamed "Star Wars" by critics. The system was never built and remained technologically unproven, but its significance was economic and psychological: the Soviets lacked the resources to counter it or match it, adding pressure to their already-stressed economy. SDI contributed to both Soviet anxiety and the willingness to negotiate arms reductions.

**Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty (INF)**
1987 treaty between Reagan and Gorbachev eliminating all ground-launched nuclear missiles with ranges of 500–5,500 km — the first arms control agreement to *eliminate* an entire class of nuclear weapons rather than merely cap them. The INF is a landmark because it showed both sides could agree on actual disarmament, not just limitation. It signaled the Cold War was winding down and gave Gorbachev political cover to pursue further reforms at home.

**Ronald Reagan**
US President (1981–1989) who intensified the Cold War through massive military spending increases, the Reagan Doctrine (supporting anti-communist movements globally), and the SDI proposal. Reagan's aggressive posture increased pressure on the Soviet economy. He is also credited with diplomatic breakthroughs — his personal rapport with Gorbachev enabled the INF Treaty. Historians debate whether Reagan's military pressure *caused* the Soviet collapse or whether internal Soviet failures were primary.

**Mikhail Gorbachev**
Last Soviet leader (1985–1991), who introduced glasnost and perestroika to save — not end — the Soviet system. He refused to use military force to suppress Eastern European revolutions in 1989, breaking from Soviet precedent. His reforms inadvertently accelerated the USSR's collapse. Gorbachev is a critical AP figure because he illustrates how reform from within an authoritarian system can be more destabilizing than external pressure: once he loosened control, he could not recapture it.

---

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves asking about the **unintended consequences** of Gorbachev's reforms. Glasnost + perestroika were designed to *save* the Soviet Union, not destroy it. The argument that "reforms intended to preserve a system instead accelerated its collapse" is a high-value thesis for CCOT and causation essays.
