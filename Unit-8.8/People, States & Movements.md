# People, States & Movements

*AMSCO pp. 608–614*

---

## Key Figures

**Mikhail Gorbachev**
Soviet General Secretary (1985–1991) and the central figure of the Cold War's end. Gorbachev rose through Communist Party ranks as a pragmatic reformer convinced that the Soviet system needed updating rather than replacing. His two landmark policies — [[Concept Notes/NATO\|glasnost]] and perestroika — were designed to modernize communism, not dismantle it. He negotiated the INF Treaty with Reagan (1987) and, critically, refused to send Soviet troops to suppress the 1989 Eastern European revolutions — a stark break from the Brezhnev Doctrine. A coup by hardliners in August 1991 failed but destroyed his political authority. He resigned on December 25, 1991, as the Soviet Union formally dissolved. Gorbachev is the subject of ongoing historiographical debate: did his reforms cause the collapse, or did he simply manage an inevitable process?

> [!tip]
> For the AP exam, know **two things Gorbachev did** that mattered most: (1) introduced glasnost + perestroika, (2) refused to use military force in Eastern Europe. The second was arguably more decisive — without Soviet tanks, the communist governments had no enforcement.

**Ronald Reagan**
US President (1981–1989) whose strategy combined confrontation and negotiation. Reagan dramatically increased US military spending, announced SDI, and backed anti-communist movements worldwide (Reagan Doctrine — supporting mujahideen in Afghanistan, Contras in Nicaragua). He framed the Cold War in moral terms ("evil empire," "tear down this wall"). Yet Reagan also engaged diplomatically: he and Gorbachev developed genuine personal rapport and met multiple times. Together they signed the INF Treaty (1987). Reagan's legacy in ending the Cold War is contested: some credit his military pressure for bankrupting the USSR; others argue Soviet internal failures were primary.

**Pope John Paul II (Karol Wojtyła)**
Polish-born pope (1978–2005) who played an important ideological role in Eastern Europe's liberation. His election as the first non-Italian pope in 455 years was itself a symbolic blow to communist authority in Poland. He visited Poland in 1979, drawing massive crowds that defied state control and bolstered the Solidarity movement. John Paul II provided moral and spiritual legitimacy to the anti-communist resistance at a time when open political opposition was dangerous. The AP exam occasionally uses him to illustrate how the Catholic Church functioned as a civil society institution that communist states could not fully suppress.

**Lech Wałęsa**
Polish electrician and leader of the **Solidarity** trade union movement — the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc. Founded in 1980 at the Gdańsk shipyards, Solidarity grew to 10 million members and became a vehicle for political opposition. After years of suppression under martial law, Solidarity was legalized in 1989 and won Poland's first free elections. Wałęsa became Poland's president (1990–1995). He illustrates how a mass civil society movement, not just government diplomacy, drove the Cold War's end from below.

**Richard Nixon / Leonid Brezhnev**
The architects of détente. Nixon's 1972 visits to both China and the Soviet Union were diplomatic triumphs that used great-power triangulation to reduce tensions. Nixon and Brezhnev signed SALT I, the first major nuclear arms control agreement. The Brezhnev Doctrine — that the Soviet Union would intervene militarily to prevent communist governments from falling — was the policy Gorbachev ultimately abandoned in 1989, making his break with it historically significant.

**Boris Yeltsin**
Russian politician who rose as a democratic reformer within the Soviet system. When hardline Communist Party members launched a coup against Gorbachev in August 1991, Yeltsin climbed on a tank outside the Russian parliament and rallied resistance, becoming a symbol of democratic defiance. As Russia's first popularly elected president, Yeltsin oversaw the formal dissolution of the Soviet Union and the transition to a market economy — a chaotic process accompanied by enormous economic disruption and the rise of oligarchs.

## Key States & Blocs

**Soviet Union (USSR) — 1917–1991**
At its peak, the USSR was a nuclear superpower controlling a vast empire of satellite states and client regimes across Eastern Europe, Central Asia, and beyond. By the 1980s, it faced compounding crises: stagnant command economy, costly Afghan war, restive satellite states, and a reform process (glasnost/perestroika) that it could not control. The USSR formally dissolved on December 25, 1991, into 15 independent states. Russia emerged as the primary successor state.

**Eastern European Satellite States**
Countries like Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, East Germany, Romania, and Bulgaria were governed by Communist parties that owed their existence to Soviet military backing. When Gorbachev signaled in 1988–89 that Soviet troops would no longer intervene, these states erupted. The revolutions of 1989 were largely peaceful (except Romania), moving with extraordinary speed. The **Berlin Wall** fell November 9, 1989; Germany reunified October 3, 1990. These revolutions illustrated that communist governments had been surviving on coercion alone, not popular legitimacy.

**Solidarity Movement (Poland)**
Founded 1980 at Gdańsk, Solidarity was the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc — and quickly became a mass opposition movement with 10 million members. Despite being suppressed under martial law (1981), Solidarity survived underground and with Church support. When the Soviet guarantee of repression was lifted, it emerged to win Poland's first free elections in 1989. Solidarity's success became a model showing that organized civil society could outlast authoritarian suppression.
