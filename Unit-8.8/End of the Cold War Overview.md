# End of the Cold War

**AMSCO pp. 608–614 | AP Period 4 | Themes: GOV, ECN, CDI**

## The Big Picture

The Cold War did not end with a bang — it ended with a collapse. After nearly five decades of superpower rivalry, the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, leaving the United States as the world's sole superpower. The causes were interconnected: a fundamentally broken command economy, a draining military overextension in Afghanistan, Mikhail Gorbachev's reform policies that unleashed forces he could not control, and sustained US pressure — ideological, economic, and military — under Reagan and his predecessors. Understanding *why* the Soviet Union fell matters as much as knowing *that* it fell.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about **causes** of the Cold War's end. Know at least three distinct factors: (1) Soviet economic failure and overextension (Afghanistan), (2) Gorbachev's reforms (glasnost, perestroika) that backfired, and (3) US pressure via arms buildup and SDI. Avoid the trap of attributing the collapse to any single cause.

## Phase 1: Détente (1969–1979)

After the dangerous crises of the 1960s (Bay of Pigs, Cuban Missile Crisis), both superpowers sought a more stable relationship. **Détente** — a French term for a relaxation of tensions — characterized US-Soviet relations in the early 1970s. President Nixon's 1972 visit to the Soviet Union produced the [[Concept Notes/NATO\|SALT I]] arms limitation treaty, capping the nuclear arsenals of both powers. Nixon also made a landmark visit to communist China the same year, exploiting the Sino-Soviet split to pressure Moscow.

The Soviet Union had its own reasons for détente. Its economy was stagnating under centralized control. Eastern European satellite states were restless — the Soviet military had crushed the **Prague Spring** (1968) in Czechoslovakia, but the underlying pressures remained. Détente offered breathing room. Both sides traded: the US sold grain to a food-scarce USSR; the Soviets accepted limits on missile growth.

Détente collapsed in 1979 when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan to prop up a failing communist government. President Carter halted grain shipments in protest, and the era of superpower cooperation gave way to a new freeze.

## Phase 2: The Soviet-Afghan War and Reagan's Pressure (1979–1985)

The Soviet-Afghan War (1979–1989) became the USSR's Vietnam. The Soviet army could not defeat guerrilla fighters in Afghanistan's mountainous terrain. Afghan civilian casualties numbered in the hundreds of thousands to millions; millions more became refugees. The war drained Soviet resources, demoralized the military, and delegitimized the Soviet government at home and abroad.

President Reagan (1981–1989) intensified pressure. He referred to the USSR as the "evil empire," massively increased US military spending, and sent weapons to Afghan mujahideen fighters through the CIA. Most provocatively, Reagan announced the **Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)** in 1983 — a proposed space-based missile shield that, if built, would render Soviet nuclear weapons useless. The Soviets could not match it economically. The arms race was being pushed into a domain where the USSR could not compete.

> [!warning]
> **SDI was never built** — it was a proposal, not a functioning system. But its significance was *psychological and economic*: it forced Soviet planners to consider how to respond to a threat they lacked the resources to counter. This contributed to internal pressure for reform.

## Phase 3: Gorbachev's Reforms and the Thaw (1985–1989)

Mikhail Gorbachev came to power in 1985 as a reformer who believed the Soviet system could be saved — if modernized. He introduced two landmark policies:

- **Perestroika** ("restructuring"): economic reforms allowing limited private enterprise and decentralizing decision-making. In practice, the reforms were too partial to fix the economy but enough to destabilize the existing system.
- **Glasnost** ("openness"): political transparency and freedom of expression. Once citizens could criticize the government, the dam broke — suppressed grievances about Soviet rule across the empire poured out.

Reagan and Gorbachev met multiple times, developing a working relationship despite ideological differences. In 1987 they signed the **Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty (INF)** — the first arms control agreement to *eliminate* an entire class of nuclear weapons, not just cap them. It signaled both sides wanted to step back from the brink.

Gorbachev also announced that Soviet troops would no longer intervene to suppress reform movements in Eastern Europe — the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine. This was the signal Eastern Europeans had waited decades for.

## Phase 4: The Collapse (1989–1991)

In 1989, democratic revolutions swept through Eastern Europe with startling speed. In Poland, the **Solidarity** movement (backed in part by Pope John Paul II and the Catholic Church) had been building for years; Lech Wałęsa became Poland's first freely elected president in 1990. Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria all shed communist governments. Most symbolically, the **Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989** — and East and West Germany reunited in October 1990.

Within the Soviet Union itself, Baltic states (Lithuania, Estonia, Latvia) and Caucasus republics declared independence. The Warsaw Pact dissolved. A hardline coup attempt against Gorbachev in August 1991 failed, but it fatally weakened him. Boris Yeltsin emerged as Russia's leader. On December 25, 1991, the Soviet Union formally dissolved into 15 independent states. The Cold War was over.

> [!tip]
> The **domino effect ran in reverse**: just as US policymakers feared communism would spread (domino theory), the collapse of one communist government gave others permission to fall. Gorbachev's refusal to use military force was the key variable — without that threat, Eastern European governments had no enforcement mechanism.

## Post–Cold War World

The end of the Cold War reshaped global politics. The US became the sole superpower ("unipolar moment"). The 1991 **Gulf War** — a US-led coalition expelling Iraq from Kuwait — demonstrated American military dominance in the new world order. Economic globalization accelerated as former communist states opened to market economies. But new challenges emerged: ethnic conflict and genocide (Yugoslavia, Rwanda), terrorism, global inequality, and environmental degradation. The Cold War's end resolved one set of tensions while creating new instabilities.

> [!exam-tip]
> **CCOT framing for essays:** Before 1991 — bipolar world, proxy wars, nuclear deterrence, containment. After 1991 — unipolar US dominance, new ethnic conflicts, economic globalization, terrorism as the primary security threat. The continuity is great-power competition for influence; the change is the collapse of ideological bipolarity.
