# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 9.2 — Technological Advancements & Limitations: Disease | Printed pp. 641–648**

---

**Epidemics**
Widespread outbreaks of infectious disease affecting large populations across regions or globally. The 20th and 21st centuries saw major epidemics including the 1918 influenza pandemic, HIV/AIDS, and Ebola; epidemics disproportionately devastated regions lacking medical infrastructure, making them a lens for AP analysis of global inequality.

**pandemic**
A disease epidemic affecting a very wide area — a whole country, continent, or the world. The 1918 influenza pandemic infected one-fifth of the world's population; HIV/AIDS became a slow-burning global pandemic killing tens of millions. Modern pandemics spread along the same globalized trade and travel networks that drive economic integration.

**smallpox**
A highly contagious viral disease that had devastated populations throughout human history — including indigenous populations of the Americas after 1492, where it killed an estimated 50–90% of those with no prior exposure. The WHO's global vaccination campaign declared smallpox eradicated in 1979, making it the only human disease ever eliminated. Eradication required coordinated international effort across every country — a landmark of global health cooperation.

**malaria**
A parasitic disease spread by mosquitoes in tropical regions, killing over 600,000 people annually in the early 21st century. Malaria's persistence despite modern medicine illustrates how poverty and geography limit access to healthcare — a core AP theme connecting disease to economic development and inequality. Most victims were young children in sub-Saharan Africa.

**[[Concept Notes/Doctors Without Borders\|Doctors Without Borders]]**
An international humanitarian organization (Médecins Sans Frontières, founded 1971) that provides emergency medical care in conflict zones, disaster areas, and regions with endemic disease. Represents the post-1945 growth of NGOs as non-state actors in global health and humanitarianism. Treated about 1.7 million malaria patients annually — exemplifying how private organizations fill gaps left by governments.

**tuberculosis**
An airborne bacterial infection affecting the lungs, spread through coughs and sneezes; closely associated with poverty and overcrowding. A cure using antibiotics became available in 1946, but drug-resistant TB strains emerged in the early 21st century. Demonstrates how technological advances can create new vulnerabilities (antibiotic resistance) even while solving old problems.

**cholera**
A bacterial disease spread through contaminated water, causing severe dehydration and roughly 95,000 deaths per year globally. Preventable with boiling/chlorinating water and hand-washing. Like TB and malaria, cholera disproportionately affects poor populations in developing nations — a persistent marker of global inequality in access to clean water and sanitation.

**polio**
A highly contagious viral disease transmitted through contaminated water that can cause paralysis; historically infected 100,000 new people per year. The development of Salk's injectable vaccine (1955) and Sabin's oral vaccine (1961) enabled a global eradication campaign begun in 1988, eliminating polio from nearly every country within 30 years. Where it persisted, war and political distrust blocked vaccination — illustrating how political instability undermines public health.

**Jonas Salk**
American medical researcher who announced an effective injectable polio vaccine on April 12, 1955 — one of the landmark medical achievements of the 20th century. Salk's vaccine launched global public health campaigns that nearly eradicated polio, exemplifying how scientific advances combined with international cooperation can defeat infectious disease.

**Albert Sabin**
American virologist who developed an oral polio vaccine (available 1961) complementing Salk's injectable version. The oral format was easier to administer in developing nations, accelerating global polio eradication and demonstrating how vaccine accessibility shapes public health outcomes. The oral vaccine's wider usability made mass immunization campaigns feasible in low-income countries.

**Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS)**
A life-threatening condition caused by HIV that destroys the immune system, leaving patients vulnerable to opportunistic infections. AIDS emerged in the early 1980s and killed over 25 million people globally by 2014. It hit sub-Saharan Africa hardest, erasing decades of development gains. Early stigma (association with gay men and drug users) slowed funding and research, delaying effective treatment.

**Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)**
The virus that causes AIDS, transmitted through blood, sexual contact, and breast milk. HIV/AIDS spread globally after the 1980s, with sub-Saharan Africa bearing the heaviest burden. The development of antiretroviral drugs in the mid-1990s transformed HIV from a death sentence into a manageable chronic condition — for those who could access and afford treatment.

**Ebola**
A deadly hemorrhagic fever caused by a virus infecting bats, primates, and humans; first identified in the Congo in 1976. A major 2014 West Africa outbreak caused global alarm but was contained through coordinated international public health efforts led by the WHO. Ebola exemplifies how modern global mobility accelerates disease spread while international cooperation can contain it.

**antiretroviral drugs**
Medications that suppress HIV replication, allowing those infected to live long, relatively healthy lives rather than progressing to AIDS. Developed by the mid-1990s but initially very expensive. Brazil's 1996 policy of providing free antiretroviral drugs to anyone who needed them became a global model — dramatically reducing deaths and hospitalizations while saving government money by preventing costly hospitalizations. Access remains unequally distributed globally.

**heart disease**
Conditions affecting the heart's structure and function (coronary artery disease, heart failure); became the leading cause of death in wealthy industrialized nations by the late 20th century as infectious disease declined. Its rise illustrates the **epidemiological transition**: as societies grow wealthier and eliminate infectious diseases, chronic, lifestyle-related conditions replace them as primary killers.

**heart transplant**
A surgical procedure replacing a diseased heart with a healthy donor heart, first successfully performed by Christiaan Barnard in South Africa in 1967. Represents the broader 20th-century revolution in surgical medicine. Less invasive procedures — valve replacement, stents, bypass surgery, cholesterol-lowering drugs — further extended survival in subsequent decades.

**Christiaan Barnard**
South African cardiac surgeon who performed the world's first successful human heart transplant on December 3, 1967, at Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town. Barnard's achievement demonstrated that landmark medical innovation was not exclusively a Western or American phenomenon — an important counterpoint to Eurocentric narratives of technological progress.

**Robert Jarvik**
American scientist who led the team that designed the Jarvik-7 artificial heart, used as a temporary bridge device for patients awaiting a compatible donor heart. His work represents the convergence of biomedical engineering and medicine that characterized late-20th-century technological advances — extending the frontier of medicine beyond purely biological solutions.

**artificial heart**
A mechanical device designed to temporarily (or potentially permanently) replace the heart's pumping function; developed by Robert Jarvik's team and used clinically from the 1980s. Exemplifies how advances in materials science and biomedical engineering extended the possibilities of medicine beyond biological transplantation.

**Alzheimer's disease**
A progressive neurological disorder causing memory loss and cognitive decline, primarily affecting the elderly; became an increasing concern as rising life expectancy produced aging populations. Illustrates the new health challenges created by demographic success — longer lives generate new pressures on healthcare systems, families, and economies. No cure had been found by the early 21st century.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to identify the **cause of disease persistence** in an MCQ. The answer for malaria, TB, and cholera is almost always **poverty and inequality**, not the absence of a cure. A cure or treatment exists for all three; the problem is access. Frame your answers around inequality, not just medical technology.
