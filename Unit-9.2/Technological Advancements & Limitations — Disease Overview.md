# Technological Advancements & Limitations — Disease

**AP Period 4 | Unit 9: Globalization | Printed pp. 641–648**

**Essential Question:** How has globalization affected the spread of disease and the development of medical technology?

**Learning Objective:** Explain the causes and effects of disease in the context of increased global interaction after 1900.

**AP Themes:** ENV (Environment), TEC (Technology), SIO (Social Interactions & Organization)

---

## The Big Picture

The 20th and 21st centuries brought a paradox in global health: medicine advanced dramatically while new diseases emerged and old ones persisted. Progress in vaccines, antibiotics, and surgery reduced deaths from many diseases that had plagued humanity for centuries. But globalization — the same interconnected world that enabled trade and cultural exchange — also accelerated the spread of new epidemics. HIV/AIDS, Ebola, and antibiotic-resistant tuberculosis all spread along the same pathways as goods and people.

The AP exam tests two patterns here. First, the **epidemiological transition**: as wealthy nations defeated infectious disease, chronic conditions (heart disease, Alzheimer's) became leading killers — a byproduct of longer lives. Second, the **inequality of access**: vaccines and antiretroviral drugs existed, but only those in wealthy countries or with bold government policies (like Brazil's free drug program) could routinely access them. Disease in this topic is never just a science story — it is always also a story about inequality, governance, and globalization.

The key causal chain: global interaction → faster disease spread → international public health response → uneven outcomes by wealth. That chain drives almost every AP question on this topic.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to connect Topic 9.2 to [[Unit-4.3/Columbian Exchange Overview|Topic 4.3]] (Columbian Exchange). The pattern is the same: increased global interaction spreads disease; populations without prior exposure suffer disproportionately. The difference is scale and intentionality — colonial-era spread was partly deliberate; modern epidemics spread through commerce and travel.

## Disease and Poverty: Persistent Killers

Despite modern medicine, three diseases remained devastating in the 21st century precisely because their spread was tied to the conditions of poverty rather than to the absence of a cure.

**Malaria** is a parasitic disease spread by mosquitoes in tropical regions. It killed over 600,000 people annually in the early 21st century, the vast majority of them young children in sub-Saharan Africa. [[Concept Notes/Doctors Without Borders\|Doctors Without Borders]] (Médecins Sans Frontières) treated about 1.7 million malaria patients per year. Mosquito nets treated with insecticide provided protection during sleep, though waking-hours exposure continued. A vaccine was in development for decades; by 2019, the WHO certified Algeria and Argentina as malaria-free — but also warned that some mosquitoes were developing insecticide resistance.

**Tuberculosis** (TB) is an airborne bacterial infection affecting the lungs, associated with crowded, poor living conditions. Before 1946, no effective drug treatment existed. Antibiotics provided a cure, but by the early 21st century, drug-resistant TB strains emerged — particularly in prisons. The WHO launched a worldwide TB campaign in the 2010s.

**Cholera** is a bacterial disease spread through contaminated water, causing dehydration and roughly 95,000 deaths annually. Boiling water, chlorination, and hand-washing prevent spread; vaccines exist but do not replace preventive hygiene. Like TB and malaria, cholera disproportionately struck poor populations in developing countries.

> [!tip]
> Malaria, TB, and cholera form a trio the AP exam treats as evidence of **persistent global inequality** — diseases with known causes and partial treatments that continue killing because poverty makes prevention and treatment inaccessible.

## Vaccines and Global Eradication Campaigns

The greatest triumph of 20th-century medicine was the eradication of [[Concept Notes/Smallpox\|Smallpox]]. The WHO ran a global vaccination campaign that declared success in 1979 — the only human disease ever eradicated.

**Polio** came next. A virus that caused paralysis and sometimes death in 100,000 new people per year, polio was targeted by a landmark 1988 joint campaign involving governments, private organizations, and UN agencies. The key breakthroughs were **Jonas Salk**'s injectable vaccine (1955) and **Albert Sabin**'s oral vaccine (1961). Within 30 years, polio was eliminated from nearly every country. Where it persisted — Pakistan, Afghanistan — war and religious mistrust of outsiders blocked vaccine administration. The polio campaign demonstrated that coordinated global effort could defeat epidemic disease.

> [!exam-tip]
> The polio eradication campaign is a model AP example for **international cooperation** (GOV theme) and **technology reducing disease** (TEC theme). Contrast it with malaria, where a vaccine remains incomplete, to show the limits of technology when political and logistical barriers exist.

## Emerging Epidemics: Globalization as Disease Vector

The same global connectivity that enabled trade, migration, and cultural exchange also created rapid pathways for new diseases.

**The 1918 Influenza Pandemic** killed an estimated 20 million people worldwide — more than World War I itself. One-quarter of Americans and one-fifth of the global population were infected. Victims were often young adults (ages 20–40, unusual for flu). The disease spread along trade routes and with military troop movements, demonstrating how war and global mobility amplify epidemic spread. Life expectancy in the United States fell by 10 years.

**HIV/AIDS** was identified in 1981 and became the defining epidemic of the late 20th century. The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) weakens the immune system by attacking CD4 cells, leaving patients vulnerable to opportunistic infections; without treatment, HIV progresses to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS). The disease spread through unprotected sex, blood transfusions, and shared needles. AIDS killed more than 25 million people between 1981 and 2014. Sub-Saharan Africa bore the heaviest toll — erasing decades of development gains. Early stigma around homosexual men and drug users slowed funding and research. By the mid-1990s, antiretroviral drugs could suppress HIV and allow patients to live for many years — but they were expensive. Brazil's pioneering 1996 policy of providing free antiretroviral drugs to anyone who needed them dramatically cut deaths and hospitalizations. By 2018, about 40 million people globally were living with HIV, most in developing countries.

**Ebola** was discovered in the Congo in 1976. A hemorrhagic fever caused by a virus infecting bats, humans, and other primates, Ebola causes bleeding, organ failure, and death in most cases. A massive 2014 outbreak in West Africa triggered global alarm. A coordinated public health effort led by the WHO contained it, demonstrating again that international cooperation could manage epidemic disease.

> [!warning]
> Students often confuse HIV and AIDS. HIV is the virus; AIDS is the advanced stage of the disease. A person can be HIV-positive for years (especially with antiretroviral treatment) without having AIDS. The AP exam expects precision on this distinction.

## Diseases of Longevity: Chronic Conditions in an Aging World

As vaccines and antibiotics defeated infectious disease, populations in wealthy nations lived longer — and encountered a new set of chronic conditions.

**Heart disease** became the leading cause of death in industrialized nations. The landmark advance was the first successful **heart transplant**, performed by South African surgeon Christiaan Barnard in 1967. American scientist Robert Jarvik then designed an artificial heart as a temporary bridge device for patients awaiting a donor heart. Less invasive procedures — valve replacement, artery stents, bypass surgery, cholesterol medications — further extended survival.

**Alzheimer's disease**, a progressive neurological disorder causing dementia, became increasingly prevalent as populations aged. Researchers continued to search for a cure throughout the late 20th and early 21st centuries.

The rise of chronic disease illustrates the **epidemiological transition**: as societies grow wealthier and infectious disease declines, chronic, age-related conditions replace it at the top of mortality charts.

> [!tip]
> Christiaan Barnard's nationality matters for the AP exam — the first heart transplant was performed in South Africa, not the United States or Europe. This is an example of medical innovation emerging outside the traditional "West," useful as a counterargument to Eurocentric narratives about where technological advances originate.

## Global Health Governance

The World Health Organization ([[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]] agency) coordinated the global response to epidemic disease — from the smallpox eradication campaign to the Ebola road map. [[Concept Notes/Doctors Without Borders\|Doctors Without Borders]] (an NGO) provided frontline medical care in conflict zones and disease hotspots. These institutions exemplify the post-1945 proliferation of international organizations managing problems that no single state could solve alone.

> [!exam-tip]
> For SAQs and LEQs connecting to Unit 8: the same international institutions built to manage Cold War politics (the UN system, WHO) became the backbone of global public health governance. Unit 9.2's disease stories are also stories about the institutional legacy of 1945.
