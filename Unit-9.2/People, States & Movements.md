# People, States & Movements

**Topic 9.2 — Technological Advancements & Limitations: Disease | Printed pp. 641–648**

---

## Key Figures

**Jonas Salk (1914–1995)**
American medical researcher who announced an effective injectable polio vaccine on April 12, 1955 — one of the most celebrated medical achievements of the 20th century. Salk's vaccine was the product of years of painstaking trials and immediately launched global vaccination campaigns. Notably, Salk refused to patent the vaccine, keeping it affordable and available — a decision that accelerated mass immunization. His work set the template for publicly funded, internationally distributed vaccines as a tool of global public health.

**Albert Sabin (1906–1993)**
American virologist born in Russia who developed an oral polio vaccine, available from 1961. The oral format (a few drops on a sugar cube) was far easier to administer than Salk's injectable version, making mass immunization feasible in low-income and conflict-affected countries. Sabin's vaccine became the primary tool of the WHO's global polio eradication campaign begun in 1988.

**Christiaan Barnard (1922–2001)**
South African cardiac surgeon who performed the world's first successful human-to-human heart transplant on December 3, 1967, at Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town. His patient lived for 18 days — a brief but revolutionary proof of concept. Barnard's achievement is notable on the AP exam for two reasons: it came from South Africa (not the U.S. or Europe), and it opened the field of organ transplant surgery that extended countless lives thereafter.

**Robert Jarvik (b. 1946)**
American biomedical engineer and physician who led the team that designed the Jarvik-7 artificial heart, first implanted in a human patient in 1982. The device served as a bridge while patients waited for a compatible donor heart. Jarvik's work represents the convergence of engineering and medicine — a broader 20th-century trend in which technology extended medical possibilities beyond what biology alone could offer.

**Nathan Wolfe (b. 1970)**
American virologist and global health expert whose work focuses on tracking and preventing pandemics at their source (often in wildlife populations before they jump to humans). The AMSCO chapter opens with his observation: "We live in a world fraught with risk from new pandemics. Fortunately, we also now live in an era with the tools to build a global immune system." His framing — risk AND tools — captures the central tension of Topic 9.2.

## Key Organizations & States

**World Health Organization (WHO)**
The UN's specialized agency for international public health, founded in 1948. The WHO coordinated the campaigns that eradicated smallpox (declared 1979) and nearly eradicated polio (campaign began 1988). It issued the global emergency warning and "road map" that contained the 2014 West African Ebola outbreak. The WHO also launched worldwide campaigns against tuberculosis in the 2010s and certified countries as malaria-free. The WHO exemplifies how the post-1945 international order applied multilateral cooperation to shared health challenges. See [[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]].

> [!tip]
> The WHO appears in multiple AP topics: Unit 8 (founding, Cold War context) and Unit 9 (disease campaigns). When writing SAQs or LEQs about international organizations, the WHO is always a strong example of an organization that produced concrete, measurable results (smallpox eradication) — not just documents and resolutions.

**[[Concept Notes/Doctors Without Borders\|Doctors Without Borders]] (Médecins Sans Frontières / MSF)**
A French-origin international NGO (founded 1971, Nobel Peace Prize 1999) that delivers emergency medical care in conflict zones, epidemic areas, and regions underserved by state health systems. In the context of Topic 9.2, MSF treated about 1.7 million malaria patients annually and was a frontline responder during Ebola outbreaks. MSF represents a broader post-1945 trend: non-state organizations filling humanitarian gaps that governments and intergovernmental agencies cannot reach.

**Brazil**
Notable on the AP exam for its 1996 policy of providing free antiretroviral drugs to every person with HIV who needed them — the first country to do so at national scale. The policy dramatically reduced AIDS-related deaths and hospitalizations and actually saved the government money by preventing expensive medical crises. Brazil's approach became a global model for equitable access to life-saving drugs and demonstrates how state policy choices, not just medical technology, determine health outcomes.

**Sub-Saharan Africa**
The region most devastated by HIV/AIDS in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, accounting for the majority of global HIV infections and deaths. AIDS erased decades of development gains — killing working-age adults, orphaning millions of children, and straining health systems already weakened by poverty. The epidemic in sub-Saharan Africa illustrates the AP theme of uneven globalization: the same interconnected world that spread ideas and trade also spread a fatal virus most rapidly through the poorest and least medically equipped communities.

**Pakistan and Afghanistan**
The two countries where polio was NOT eradicated by the early 21st century. War, political instability, and religious fundamentalism created environments where vaccine campaigns could not be implemented safely and where populations distrusted outsiders promoting vaccination. These cases illustrate the limits of technology: the vaccine exists, but political conditions prevented its delivery — a pattern the AP exam uses to test understanding of how non-medical factors shape health outcomes.

## Key Movements & Campaigns

**Global [[Concept Notes/Smallpox\|Smallpox]] Eradication Campaign (WHO, 1967–1979)**
A coordinated international campaign that vaccinated populations worldwide and tracked and isolated every case of smallpox. Declared successful in 1979 — the only time humanity has ever eradicated a human disease. Required participation from both Cold War superpowers (the U.S. and Soviet Union cooperated on this campaign even during the height of Cold War tensions), demonstrating that shared interests could override political rivalry.

**Global Polio Eradication Initiative (1988–present)**
A joint campaign by governments, Rotary International, the WHO, UNICEF, and (later) the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, begun in 1988. Reduced polio cases by over 99.9%. The campaign illustrates the power of public-private partnerships in global health and the role of sustained, coordinated international effort over decades.

**Brazil's Free ARV Policy (1996)**
Brazil's decision to manufacture generic antiretroviral drugs and provide them freely to HIV patients challenged pharmaceutical industry pricing models. It showed that developing-world governments could take assertive action on drug access, and it set a precedent that influenced international negotiations on intellectual property and public health (the 2001 Doha Declaration on TRIPS).

> [!exam-tip]
> If an SAQ asks you to "identify ONE example of a state that implemented policies to address a global health challenge," Brazil's free ARV program is one of the strongest possible examples — it is specific, consequential, and involves a non-Western state, which AP readers appreciate as evidence of global complexity.
