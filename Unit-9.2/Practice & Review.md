## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to a map showing New Cases of Tuberculosis per 100,000 People, 2015. The map shows highest rates concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South/Southeast Asia; lowest rates in North America, Western Europe, and Australia.*

**Question 1.** Which of the following best explains the overall trend of tuberculosis cases shown on the map?

(A) The role of trade routes in spreading disease
(B) The lack of a global agency to coordinate public health campaigns
(C) The spread of diseases in countries with rising birth rates
(D) The persistence of diseases associated with poverty

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> TB is closely associated with poverty — poor housing, overcrowding, malnutrition, and lack of access to healthcare sustain transmission even where effective treatments exist. (A) is plausible but wrong: TB doesn't spread via trade routes the way cholera or plague did. (B) is wrong: the WHO actively campaigns against TB. (C) is wrong: rising birth rates don't directly cause TB; poverty does. The map's pattern correlates with income level, not population growth.

**Question 2.** Which of the following is the most reasonable inference about countries with the lowest rates of tuberculosis infection?

(A) Climate conditions kill off the tuberculosis bacteria.
(B) Medical workers control the spread of tuberculosis with antibiotics.
(C) Scientific advances have ended the threat of new epidemic diseases.
(D) These countries receive the lowest numbers of immigrants.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Low-infection countries have the health infrastructure to detect, treat, and isolate TB cases using antibiotics, preventing community spread. (A) is wrong: TB bacteria survive in a wide range of climates. (C) is wrong: antibiotic resistance shows scientific advances haven't ended epidemic threats. (D) is wrong and irrelevant: immigration patterns don't determine TB rates — access to treatment does.

**Question 3.** Which differences shown on the map most directly reflected which more general development in the late 20th century?

(A) Longer life expectancy
(B) Deforestation and desertification
(C) Neocolonialism
(D) The uneven expansion of Internet access

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The TB map shows the same geographic pattern as the uneven expansion of global technology and wealth — high-income regions gained access to effective treatments and health infrastructure; low-income regions did not. "Uneven" is the operative word. (A) doesn't explain the geographic disparity. (B) is environmental but doesn't explain the TB pattern. (C) is tempting but overly specific — the map's pattern is better explained by uneven development broadly than by neocolonialism specifically.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Primary Source Analysis

*Passage (Phillip Stevens, Director of Health Projects, Diseases of Poverty and the 10/90 Gap, 2004):*
> "The health problems faced by the world's poorest populations are not caused by a lack of drugs specifically related to their problems and diseases. The real problem is ensuring that these populations can actually access vital medicines. Many governments fail their populations in this respect by imposing punitive tariffs and taxes on medicines, and by skewing their spending priorities in favour of defense over health... Emerging health threats, ranging from drug-resistant strains of AIDS and tuberculosis to avian flu, remind us of the importance of ensuring that the pharmaceutical industry continues to discover and develop new drugs. Innovation is a fragile process, and it can be weakened or thwarted by poor public policies."

**(A) Identify ONE action governments take, in Stevens's view, that keep people in need from accessing medications.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> According to Stevens, governments keep people from accessing medications by imposing high tariffs and taxes on medicines, and by directing spending toward defense rather than health. This makes drugs unaffordable even when they exist and are effective.

**(B) Explain ONE historical example that supports Stevens's argument.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Antiretroviral drugs for HIV/AIDS were developed in the mid-1990s and could suppress the virus, allowing infected individuals to live long, healthy lives. However, because of high prices — driven by pharmaceutical company patents and lack of generic alternatives — most patients in sub-Saharan Africa could not access them. Brazil's 1996 decision to manufacture generic antiretroviral drugs and distribute them freely to all who needed them demonstrated that government policy choices, not just drug availability, determined who survived. Most governments in affected regions made no such choice, leaving millions to die of a treatable condition — exactly the failure Stevens describes.

**(C) Explain ONE way in which environments have shaped societies between 1900 and the present.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The tropical environment of sub-Saharan Africa, where the Anopheles mosquito thrives, created conditions for malaria to persist as a major cause of death even in the 21st century. The geographic concentration of malaria in tropical low-income regions meant that the disease disproportionately struck populations already vulnerable due to poverty, shaping these societies by killing over 600,000 people annually — the vast majority of them young children — and thereby limiting economic productivity, educational development, and population growth in affected countries.

### SAQ 2 — Free Response

**(A) Identify ONE example of a medical development that affected populations around the world between 1900 and the present.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Jonas Salk's development of an effective injectable polio vaccine in 1955 affected populations worldwide. The vaccine launched a global immunization campaign that, combined with Albert Sabin's oral vaccine (1961), reduced polio cases by over 99.9% and eliminated the disease from nearly every country by the early 21st century.

**(B) Identify ONE example of a disease that affected populations around the world between 1900 and the present.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> HIV/AIDS, identified in 1981, affected populations worldwide by killing more than 25 million people between 1981 and 2014. The epidemic spread globally through sexual contact, blood transfusions, and shared needles, disproportionately devastating sub-Saharan Africa while also affecting North America, Europe, and South and Southeast Asia.

**(C) Explain ONE example of a scientific development that affected populations around the world between 1900 and the present.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The development of antiretroviral drugs in the mid-1990s transformed HIV/AIDS from a nearly universal death sentence into a manageable chronic condition. By suppressing HIV replication, antiretroviral drugs allowed infected individuals to live for decades rather than dying within years of infection. However, the drugs were initially expensive and inaccessible to most patients in developing nations. Brazil's 1996 policy of providing free antiretrovirals demonstrated that access — not scientific capability — was the limiting factor. This scientific development thus affected global populations unevenly, dramatically extending life expectancy for wealthy patients while leaving those in low-income countries to die of a condition that was technically treatable.

## Key Comparisons for LEQ/DBQ

| Disease | Primary cause of persistence | Geographic concentration | Response type |
|---------|-----|------|------|
| Malaria | Mosquito environment + poverty | Sub-Saharan Africa, tropical regions | NGOs (MSF), nets, vaccine development |
| Tuberculosis | Airborne + poverty + crowding | Low-income countries globally | WHO campaign; antibiotic treatment |
| Cholera | Contaminated water + poverty | Low-income countries globally | Sanitation, water treatment, vaccines |
| Polio | Contaminated water | Near-eradicated; persists in conflict zones | WHO/UNICEF global campaign (1988–) |
| HIV/AIDS | Sexual contact, blood, needles | Sub-Saharan Africa disproportionately | Antiretrovirals; Brazil free drug model |
| Ebola | Zoonotic (bats → humans) | Central/West Africa | WHO emergency response |
| Heart disease | Lifestyle + genetics + longevity | Wealthy industrialized nations | Transplants, surgery, medications |

> [!exam-tip]
> For an LEQ asking about technology and its limits: structure your response around the contrast between what was *technically possible* and what was *actually accessible*. Vaccines and antiretrovirals existed — but billions couldn't access them. That gap is the argument.
