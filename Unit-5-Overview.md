# Unit 5 Overview: Revolutions (c. 1750–c. 1900)

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

> [!exam-tip]
> Unit 5 covers AP World History **Period 3 (c. 1750–1900)**, which accounts for roughly **17–20% of the MCQ section** — one of the most heavily weighted periods on the exam. Expect stimulus-based MCQs on the Enlightenment's political impact, the causes and effects of the Industrial Revolution, and reactions to industrial capitalism. SAQs and DBQs frequently ask you to analyze causation, continuity, and change in political revolutions or industrialization patterns.

---

## Unit Summary

Between 1750 and 1900, two interlocking revolutions — one political, one economic — remade the world. The **Enlightenment** provided a new intellectual toolkit: reason, natural rights, popular sovereignty, and the social contract replaced divine authority as the basis for legitimate government. These ideas ignited a chain of **Atlantic Revolutions** — American (1776), French (1789), Haitian (1791–1804), and Latin American (1810s–1820s) — that toppled monarchies, abolished slavery in some places, and created new nations built on nationalist ideologies. At almost the same moment, Britain launched the **Industrial Revolution**, replacing human and animal muscle with machine power fueled by coal and steam. Industrialization spread unevenly — to Western Europe, North America, Russia, and Japan by 1900 — generating unprecedented wealth while also producing brutal working conditions, child labor, urban squalor, and sharp class divisions. These inequalities spurred new ideological responses: socialism, communism, labor unionism, and early feminism. By 1900, industrialized nations wielded economic and military power sufficient to dominate the globe, setting the stage for formal imperialism in Unit 6.

## The Big Picture: Unit 5 in One Paragraph

Unit 5 marks the twin birth of the modern political and economic orders. The Enlightenment dismantled the intellectual foundations of absolute monarchy and hereditary aristocracy, replacing them with ideals of individual rights, representative government, and national self-determination. These ideas were explosive: the American and French revolutions inspired independence movements across the Atlantic world, and the Haitian Revolution — the only successful slave rebellion in history to produce an independent state — took Enlightenment ideals further than their authors intended. Simultaneously, Britain's Industrial Revolution replaced agrarian and artisanal economies with factory production, railroad networks, and coal-powered machinery, generating a new industrial capitalism whose logic spread across Europe, North America, Russia, and Japan. The wealth generated was immense but unevenly distributed: a new industrial bourgeoisie accumulated capital while a new urban proletariat endured dangerous conditions. Their conflict generated modern ideologies — liberalism, conservatism, socialism, nationalism, feminism — that structured political life through the 20th century. By 1900, the combination of industrial power and nationalist ideology gave a handful of states the capacity to dominate the rest of the world, making Unit 5 the hinge on which modern global inequality turns.

## AP Thematic Synthesis Across Unit 5

### Governance
The Enlightenment challenged the divine right of kings and hereditary aristocracy, providing the intellectual arsenal for the Atlantic Revolutions. New governments experimented with republics, constitutional monarchies, and popular sovereignty — often with contradictions: the American republic enshrined slavery; the French Revolution lurched from republic to terror to empire. Nationalism became the dominant political principle by the mid-19th century, justifying both liberation movements (Italian and German unification) and the suppression of minority nationalities within multiethnic empires (Ottoman, Habsburg). Industrialization also transformed governance: industrial powers adopted constitutions, expanded suffrage (slowly, unevenly), and built bureaucratic states capable of managing railroads, armies, and public schools.

> [!exam-tip]
> The **Haitian Revolution** is a perennial AP exam topic because it tests whether students understand the *limits* of Enlightenment universalism — the revolution's founders applied principles of natural rights that white revolutionaries had denied to enslaved people.

### Economic Systems
The Industrial Revolution fundamentally reorganized economic life. The **factory system** replaced cottage industries and artisanal workshops, concentrating production and workers in urban centers. **Capitalism** evolved from mercantilism toward industrial and then corporate forms, with new institutions — investment banks, limited liability corporations, stock markets — mobilizing capital at unprecedented scale. **Free trade ideology** (championed by Adam Smith and David Ricardo) displaced mercantilist protectionism in Britain after the 1840s, but other industrializing nations (Germany, USA) used protective tariffs to build their own industries. Global commodity markets integrated raw material–producing regions (cotton from Egypt and India, rubber from Congo, nitrates from Chile) into an industrial supply chain controlled from London, Manchester, and New York.

### Cultural Developments and Religion
The Enlightenment secularized political thought without eliminating religion — most revolutionaries were deists, not atheists, and religious institutions retained enormous social influence. Romantic nationalism in the 19th century fused ethnic, linguistic, and cultural identity into political projects of unification and independence. Industrialization produced new cultural movements: **Romanticism** reacted against industrial rationalism with emotion, nature, and the sublime; **Realism** depicted the grim conditions of industrial workers and urban poverty. Scientific positivism — the belief that rational inquiry could solve all human problems — dominated intellectual life, clashing with traditional religious authority in debates over evolution (Darwin, 1859), biblical criticism, and the role of the Church in public education.

### Social Structures
Industrialization dismantled the old agrarian social order and replaced it with a new class structure. The **industrial bourgeoisie** (factory owners, merchants, bankers) and the **urban proletariat** (wage laborers in factories, mines, and railways) were the defining social classes of the age. A new **middle class** of managers, professionals, clerks, and small businesspeople emerged between them. Gender roles were restructured around the **"separate spheres" ideology** — middle-class women confined to domestic virtue; working-class women (and children) compelled to labor in harsh factory conditions. The persistence of slavery in the Americas (abolished in the British Empire 1833, USA 1865, Brazil 1888) and the expansion of indentured labor to replace it complicated any simple narrative of progress.

### Technology and Environment
This period produced the most dramatic acceleration of technological change in world history to that point. The **steam engine** (Watt, 1760s–80s), **railroad** (Stephenson, 1820s), **telegraph** (Morse, 1830s–40s), **Bessemer steel process** (1856), **electricity** (Edison, Tesla, 1870s–90s), and **internal combustion engine** (1880s) collectively transformed communication, transportation, production, and warfare. Environmental consequences began to accumulate: coal-burning cities produced toxic air; factory effluent poisoned rivers; deforestation accelerated. The **ecological footprint** of industrial civilization grew exponentially, establishing patterns that would define the environmental crisis of the 20th–21st centuries.

### Human Migration
Industrialization produced both internal and international migration on massive scales. Millions of rural workers migrated to industrial cities — Manchester, Birmingham, Chicago, Pittsburgh, Essen — creating explosive urban growth without adequate housing, sanitation, or social services. International emigration grew dramatically: ~55 million Europeans emigrated to the Americas, Australia, and South Africa between 1820 and 1920, driven by agricultural disruption (Irish Famine, 1845–52) and the pull of industrial wages. The Chinese diaspora expanded into Southeast Asia and the Americas. The abolition of slavery in the British Empire (1833) spurred a new **indentured labor** system drawing workers from India, China, and Africa to Caribbean and Pacific plantations.

> [!exam-tip]
> For **CCOT essays**, a strong argument for Unit 5 distinguishes between continuities (capitalism and global trade networks existed before industrialization, just at different scales) and genuine changes (the *pace, scale, and energy source* of production and the *new social classes* it created are genuinely unprecedented).

## AP Exam: Unit 5 High-Frequency Topics

These topics appear disproportionately on AP exams — know them cold:

1. **Enlightenment thinkers** — Locke (natural rights, consent), Rousseau (general will), Montesquieu (separation of powers), Wollstonecraft (women's rights), Smith (free market)
2. **Haitian Revolution** — Only successful slave revolt producing independent state; tests Enlightenment universalism; 1791–1804
3. **Causes of Britain's Industrial Revolution** — coal and iron deposits, capital, empire, enclosure movement, stable government, colonial markets; AP loves causation questions here
4. **Railroad as diffusion mechanism** — railroads spread industrialization, enable nation-building, facilitate imperialism; also environmental and social impact
5. **Marxism** — historical materialism, class struggle, bourgeoisie vs. proletariat; why Marx thought capitalism would collapse; *Communist Manifesto* (1848)
6. **Meiji Restoration** — Japan's state-led, top-down industrialization; selective borrowing from the West; contrast with China's failure to industrialize
7. **Separate spheres ideology** — gender and industrialization; middle-class domestic ideal vs. working-class reality; connects to early feminism
8. **Labor unions and reform** — Factory Acts (Britain), unions as collective bargaining, contrast with Marxist revolution vs. reform debate
9. **Atlantic Revolutions comparison** — What did each borrow from Enlightenment? What were the limits? (slavery in USA; women excluded from French citizenship)
10. **Continuity and change** — Industrial capitalism's connection to earlier commercial capitalism; what is genuinely *new* about industrialization?

> [!warning]
> **Common AP exam trap:** Do not treat industrialization as automatic or universal. The AP exam rewards answers that explain *why Britain first* (specific conditions), *why different countries industrialized differently* (government role, resource endowments, colonial connections), and *why some regions did not industrialize* (colonial extraction, deliberate underdevelopment).
