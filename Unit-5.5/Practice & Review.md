# Topic 5.5 – Practice and Review

**Unit 5: Revolutions | Period 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900)**

## Key Facts to Know Cold

- Steam engine (Watt, **1765**) → factories, then trains
- SS Savannah: first steam-powered transatlantic crossing (**1819**)
- Bessemer Process (**1856**): mass steel production — blast molten iron with air
- First commercial oil wells: **mid-1800s**; kerosene extracted **1847**
- First public power station: **London, 1882**
- Telephone patent: **Alexander Graham Bell, 1876**
- First transatlantic radio signal: **Guglielmo Marconi, 1901**
- Transcontinental Railroad (U.S.) completed: **1869**
- **Coaling stations** = strategically vital for steam-powered imperial navies

## Multiple Choice Practice

**1.** The development of the Bessemer Process (1856) most directly contributed to industrialization by

- A) making steam-powered ships faster and more reliable
- B) enabling the mass production of affordable steel for construction and railways
- C) replacing coal with petroleum as the primary industrial energy source
- D) allowing factories to be built without access to rivers or coal deposits

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — the Bessemer Process had no direct effect on steam engine speed or reliability; those improvements came from separate engineering advances.
> **B is correct** — The Bessemer Process drastically reduced the cost and time to produce steel, making it cheap enough to use at massive scale for railroads, bridges, and buildings. This was the structural backbone of the second industrial revolution.
> C is wrong — coal remained the dominant industrial energy source through the 19th century; petroleum gained importance gradually in the early 20th century.
> D is wrong — factory location was still heavily constrained by access to coal and rail networks; the Bessemer Process changed *what* could be built, not *where* factories could operate.

---

**2.** The construction of coaling stations across the Pacific and Africa in the late 19th century was most directly connected to

- A) the development of the internal combustion engine
- B) the spread of the telegraph network
- C) the replacement of sailing ships with steam-powered vessels
- D) the growth of the railroad industry in Europe

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> A is wrong — the internal combustion engine ran on petroleum, not coal; it became significant in the early 20th century, not the period when coaling stations were being built.
> B is wrong — telegraph lines were laid separately from coaling infrastructure; the two networks served different purposes.
> **C is correct** — Steam-powered ships needed coal to operate and could not carry enough for long ocean voyages. Coaling stations at strategic points worldwide allowed navies and merchant vessels to refuel, enabling both global trade and imperial expansion.
> D is wrong — European railroad growth was a domestic matter and did not require coaling stations in the Pacific or Africa.

---

**3.** Which of the following best describes the significance of the telegraph in the late 19th century?

- A) It replaced the railroad as the primary means of transporting goods across continents
- B) It enabled near-instant communication across long distances, linking global markets
- C) It allowed factory owners to coordinate production with steam engines more efficiently
- D) It provided the energy source for the second industrial revolution

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — the telegraph transmitted information, not goods; railroads remained the primary freight network throughout the century.
> **B is correct** — The telegraph allowed businesspeople, governments, and military commanders to communicate almost instantly across continents and oceans, fundamentally transforming commerce, imperial administration, and coordination of industrial supply chains.
> C is wrong — the telegraph's impact was on *communication across distance*, not on internal factory management or steam engine operation.
> D is wrong — the telegraph ran on electricity but was not an energy source for industry; coal and later petroleum powered industrial production.

## Short Answer Question (SAQ) Practice

**Question**: Explain ONE way that new technologies of the second industrial revolution (c. 1850–1900) changed economic production or global trade.

**Model Response**: The development of the Bessemer Process (1856) transformed economic production by making mass steel manufacturing affordable for the first time. Steel, stronger and more versatile than iron, became the structural backbone of industrial civilization — used for railroads, bridges, ships, and buildings. This drove a new wave of railroad construction globally, including the U.S. Transcontinental Railroad (completed 1869) and Russia's Trans-Siberian Railroad, which in turn opened vast interior regions to resource extraction and linked distant markets to the global economy.

## Document-Based and Long Essay Connections

**For DBQs**: Topic 5.5 commonly appears in documents about:
- Accounts of industrial workers and the social conditions of factory life
- Arguments for and against economic free trade in the age of steam and steel
- Imperial justifications citing the "civilizing" role of railroads and telegraphs in colonies
- Descriptions of technological exhibitions (like the Crystal Palace, 1851) celebrating industrial progress

**For LEQs**: Common prompts connected to 5.5:
- "Evaluate the extent to which technological change drove economic and social change in the period 1750–1900."
- "Compare the impact of the first and second industrial revolutions on global trade and economic systems."
- "Evaluate the extent to which industrialization changed the relationship between Europe and the rest of the world in the period 1750–1900."

> [!exam-tip]
> When writing about industrial technology and its effects, always connect *specific technologies* to *specific consequences*. Don't just say "the railroad connected markets" — say the **Trans-Siberian Railroad** allowed Russia to extract Siberian resources and project military power eastward, while the **railroads in India** allowed Britain to move troops and extract cotton efficiently. The more precise your evidence, the stronger your argument.

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning]
> **The second industrial revolution was NOT just "more of the same."** Steel, electricity, oil, and mass communications represented genuinely new technologies with fundamentally different applications — not simply improved versions of steam and iron. Examiners expect you to distinguish *first* (steam, coal, textiles, iron) from *second* (steel, electricity, oil, chemicals, telegraphs) industrial revolution technologies.

> [!warning]
> **Railroads didn't just help industrialized nations.** In colonized regions, railroads were tools of **imperial extraction and control**. Britain, France, and Belgium built railroads in Africa and Asia primarily to move raw materials to ports for export — not to develop local economies or connect local communities.
