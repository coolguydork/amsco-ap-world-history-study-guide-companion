# Key Terms & Concepts

**AMSCO pp. 310–316**

---

**steam engine**
Machine that converts steam pressure into mechanical energy. James Watt's improved version (1765) was far more efficient than earlier designs, making coal-powered energy practical for factories, mines, ships, and locomotives. The steam engine is the foundational technology of the First Industrial Revolution.

**coal**
Fossil fuel that became the primary energy source of industrialization. Coal powered steam engines, heated iron furnaces, and fueled ships and locomotives. Its importance made access to coal deposits a major economic and geopolitical factor in the 19th century.

**coaling stations**
Refueling depots established at strategic points along major ocean trade routes — including Cape Colony in South Africa and various Pacific islands — where coal-burning steamships could take on fuel. Control of coaling stations was a key strategic goal of imperial powers seeking to protect long-distance trade and naval power.

**Transcontinental Railroad**
Completed in 1869 in Promontory Summit, Utah, this railroad connected the Atlantic and Pacific coasts of the United States. It was built with massive public subsidies and relied heavily on immigrant labor (especially Chinese workers). It opened the American interior to resource extraction, settlement, and industrial growth.

**Bessemer Process**
Introduced in 1856 by Henry Bessemer, this process mass-produced steel by blasting molten iron with air to burn off impurities. It dramatically reduced the cost and time required to make steel, enabling the construction of skyscrapers, long bridges, ocean steamships, and rail infrastructure on an industrial scale.

**steel**
An alloy of iron and carbon made practical for mass production by the Bessemer Process. Stronger and more versatile than iron, steel became the structural material of the Second Industrial Revolution — used in railroads, bridges, buildings, ships, and machinery.

**oil**
Fossil fuel drilled commercially beginning in the mid-1800s. Initially refined into kerosene for lighting and heating; later refined into gasoline to fuel internal combustion engines. Oil became the defining energy resource of the late 19th and 20th centuries, driving both industrial expansion and imperial competition over resource-rich territories.

**capital**
Money or financial assets available for investment in business ventures. The pursuit of capital drove industrialized nations to seek colonies for cheap raw materials and new markets, accelerating imperial expansion. Capital investment also funded railroads, factories, and communication infrastructure.

**Second Industrial Revolution**
The second major wave of industrialization, occurring roughly 1870–1914, centered on steel, oil, chemicals, electricity, and precision machinery. Led by the United States, Germany, and Britain, it produced radically new industries and forms of communication, extending industrial production beyond Britain to multiple continents.

**telegraph**
Communication technology that transmitted electrical signals over wires, enabling near-instantaneous long-distance messages. Combined with railroads and steamships, the telegraph linked global markets and coordinated industrial activity across vast distances for the first time.

**telephone**
Patented by Alexander Graham Bell in 1876, the telephone transmitted voice over electrical wires. Thomas Edison's 1886 improvements made it commercially viable. It accelerated business communication and, over time, transformed social interaction.

**radio**
Wireless communication technology developed by Guglielmo Marconi, who transmitted a transatlantic signal in 1901. Radio eventually became the first form of mass media broadcasting, with broad cultural and political implications in the 20th century.

**internal combustion engine**
Engine that burns fuel inside the cylinder to generate mechanical power. Developed in the late 19th century, it powered automobiles and eventually airplanes. Its reliance on gasoline transformed oil into a strategic global resource.

**James Watt**
Scottish engineer who improved the steam engine in 1765 to make it far more efficient and cost-effective. His design made steam power practical for industrial use, effectively launching the era of coal-powered industrialization.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP essays on industrialization often ask about the *relationship* between technologies — how steam enabled railroads, how railroads enabled resource extraction, how resource extraction enabled imperialism. Practice tracing these causal chains rather than memorizing isolated facts.

> [!warning]
> Common confusion: the **Bessemer Process** produces **steel**, not iron. Iron came first (First Industrial Revolution); steel mass production is a marker of the **Second** Industrial Revolution.
