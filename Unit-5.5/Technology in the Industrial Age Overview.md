# Topic 5.5 — Technology in the Industrial Age: Overview

**AP Unit:** 5 — Revolutions | **Period:** 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900) | **AMSCO:** pp. 311–313

**Essential Question:** How did technology shape economic production during the period 1750–1900?

---

## The Two Industrial Revolutions

Historians often distinguish two waves of industrialization:

| | First Industrial Revolution | Second Industrial Revolution |
|--|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Era** | c. 1760–1850 | c. 1850–1914 |
| **Key technologies** | Textiles, steam power, iron | Steel, chemicals, electricity, precision machinery |
| **Energy source** | Coal → steam | Coal + petroleum + electricity |
| **Leading countries** | Britain | Britain, United States, Germany |
| **Key inventions** | Spinning jenny, water frame, steam engine | Bessemer steel, telephone, radio, internal combustion engine |

---

## The Coal and Steam Revolution

The **steam engine** (improved by James Watt, 1765) was the first industrial revolution's most transformative technology. Burning coal produced steam → steam drove machinery → energy became **mobile and scalable**.

Before steam, factories had to be built next to rivers (waterpower was fixed to one location). Steam engines could be installed anywhere coal could be transported. Within 50 years of Watt's design, steam was powering trains.

### Steamships
Steam transformed ocean travel. Ships were no longer dependent on wind — they could travel on schedule, in any direction, at reliable speed. On rivers, steam-powered vessels could travel **upstream at up to 5 mph** (previously impossible without being towed by animals along the shore). Ocean-going sailing ships were gradually replaced by steam-powered vessels.

As steamships spread, **coaling stations** became strategically vital — ports where ships could refuel. Key locations: Cape Colony (South Africa), various Pacific islands. Control of coaling stations shaped imperial competition.

- **SS Savannah** (1819): first steam-powered ship to cross the Atlantic Ocean

### Iron Production
Coal also made **mass production of iron** possible. Key developments:
- **Coke** (refined coal): enabled larger iron-producing furnaces
- **Wrought iron** (Henry Cort, 1794): less strong than cast iron but far more workable for shaping → valuable for construction and machinery
- **Cast iron**: strong but brittle — useful for some purposes, limited for others

## The Second Industrial Revolution

### Steel
The **Bessemer Process** (1856) made mass production of steel economical. By blasting molten metal with air, impurities were removed and the metal stayed workable. Steel — an alloy of iron and carbon — was **stronger and more versatile than iron**, becoming the backbone of industrial infrastructure: railroads, bridges, buildings, ships.

### Oil
- First commercial oil wells drilled in the **mid-1800s**
- Petroleum is a **fossil fuel** (from ancient plant and animal remains), like coal
- Initial key product: **kerosene** (extracted 1847) — used for lighting and heating
- Later: **internal combustion engine** → automobiles, airplanes
- By early 1900s, **gasoline** overtook kerosene as petroleum's most important product

### Electricity
- Effective electrical generator developed in the late 19th century
- **First public power station**: London, 1882
- Results: electric street lighting (1890s), electric street trams, and eventually electrification of factories

### Communications
- **Telephone**: patent issued to Alexander Graham Bell, 1876; Thomas Edison's refined voice transmitter (1886) made it practical
- **Radio**: Guglielmo Marconi's experiments → first transatlantic radio signal, 1901 → mass media

## Railroads and Global Trade

Railroads, steamships, and the telegraph together **linked the global economy** for the first time in history — connecting farmers, miners, manufacturers, customers, and investors across continents.

- **Transcontinental Railroad** (U.S., completed 1869): connected Atlantic and Pacific coasts; enabled U.S. industrial expansion; heavily subsidized by public funds
- **Trans-Siberian Railroad** (Russia): Moscow to the Pacific; enabled Russian trade with East Asia
- Railroads opened **interior regions** (previously inaccessible) to resource extraction and trade
- The **telegraph** enabled near-instant communication across vast distances

> [!exam-tip]
> Railroads, steamships, and the telegraph together made a truly **global economy** possible. Industrial nations used these technologies to access raw materials from colonized regions and ship finished goods worldwide. This is the technological foundation of New Imperialism — see [[Unit-6.1/Overview]].
