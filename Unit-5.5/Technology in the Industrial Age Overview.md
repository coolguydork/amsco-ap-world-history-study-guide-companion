# Technology in the Industrial Age

**AMSCO pp. 310–316 | AP Themes: TEC, ECN, ENV, MIG | AP Period 3 (1750–1900)**

**Essential Question:** How did technology shape economic production during the period from 1750 to 1900?

## The Coal Revolution and Steam Power

The Industrial Revolution's defining energy source was coal. James Watt's improved steam engine (1765) made coal power cheap and mobile — unlike water-powered mills locked to rivers, steam engines could be built anywhere. Within fifty years, steam was driving textile factories, mines, ships, and railways alike.

**Steamships** replaced sail as coal made ocean power reliable and controllable. Ships could now travel upstream on rivers at up to five miles per hour, and ocean voyages ran on predictable schedules instead of at the mercy of the wind. **Coaling stations** — at Cape Colony, Pacific islands, and other strategic points — became the logistical backbone of global trade, as ships required fuel stops along long-haul routes.

**Railroads** transformed land transport. The first steam-powered railway opened in England in 1825. The American **Transcontinental Railroad**, completed in 1869 in Utah, connected the Atlantic and Pacific coasts, unlocking the continent's interior resources — timber, coal, iron, and oil — for industrial use. Like canals before them, railroads were heavily subsidized by public funds.

> [!exam-tip]
> Railroads and steamships are the signature technologies of 5.5. Know that they (1) enabled resource extraction from interior regions, (2) accelerated imperial expansion, and (3) drove migration by opening previously inaccessible territories.

## The Second Industrial Revolution

By the late 19th century a new wave of innovation — the **Second Industrial Revolution** — transformed the United States, Great Britain, and Germany. Where the first revolution had centered on textiles, steam, and iron, the second centered on **steel, chemicals, electricity, and precision machinery**.

**Steel** became the backbone of industrial society after the **Bessemer Process** (1856) made mass production possible by blasting molten iron with air to remove impurities. Steel was stronger and more versatile than wrought iron, enabling the construction of skyscrapers, bridges, and ocean-going vessels.

**Oil** emerged as a critical resource. The first commercial oil wells in the mid-1800s produced kerosene for lighting and heat. As the internal combustion engine developed, gasoline displaced kerosene as oil's most important product, leading directly to automobile and aviation technologies in the early 1900s. London's first public electric power station opened in 1882, enabling electric street lighting and electric trams by the 1880s.

## Communication Technologies

The same industrial energy that moved goods now moved information. **Alexander Graham Bell** received a patent for the telephone in 1876, and Thomas Edison's 1886 refinements made it practical for widespread use. **Guglielmo Marconi** transmitted a radio signal across the Atlantic in 1901, creating the foundation for mass media broadcasting.

Earlier, the **telegraph** had already revolutionized communication by enabling instantaneous, long-distance messages — a capability entirely without precedent. Combined with railroads and steamships, the telegraph linked farmers, miners, manufacturers, customers, and investors globally for the first time.

> [!tip]
> Think of 5.5 technologies in two clusters: **movement of goods** (steam, railroads, steamships) and **movement of information** (telegraph, telephone, radio). Both clusters accelerated economic globalization and imperial expansion.

## Global Trade, Migration, and Empire

Industrial technologies did more than change production — they reorganized the world. Railroads and steamships opened interior regions of every continent to exploration, resource extraction, and settlement. Migration followed infrastructure: workers moved to mines, rail lines, and factory towns wherever industry pushed.

The desire for **capital** — money available to invest in business — drove industrialized nations outward. Britain, Germany, and the United States sought to secure raw materials and markets by establishing colonies, intensifying the imperial scramble that would define the late 19th century. Technologies of the Industrial Age thus directly enabled the New Imperialism covered in later topics.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate the First and Second Industrial Revolutions. First = textiles, steam, iron (Britain-led, c. 1760–1840). Second = steel, oil, electricity, chemicals (US/Germany dominant, c. 1870–1914). AP essays sometimes ask you to distinguish them.
