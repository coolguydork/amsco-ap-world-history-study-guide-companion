# Topic 5.5 — The Two Industrial Revolutions

**AP Unit:** 5 — Revolutions | **Period:** 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900) | **AMSCO:** pp. 311–313

> [!tip]
> Think of this topic as the "engine room" of AP World History's Period 3. Every major development — New Imperialism, migration, urban growth, social reform — runs on the technologies covered here. Learn the two waves, their key inventions, and how they made a global economy possible.

---

## Why Two Revolutions?

Historians divide industrialization into two waves because the technologies, energy sources, leading nations, and economic consequences were meaningfully different. The **First Industrial Revolution** (c. 1760–1850) was centered in Britain and ran on coal, steam, and iron. The **Second Industrial Revolution** (c. 1850–1914) spread to the United States, Germany, and France and ran on steel, petroleum, and electricity.

Both revolutions shared a common logic: **replacing human and animal muscle with machine power** — and each time, the scale expanded and the pace of change accelerated.

---

## First Industrial Revolution: Coal, Steam & Iron (c. 1760–1850)

### The Steam Engine — The Revolution's Core Technology

[[Concept Notes/James Watt|James Watt]]'s improved steam engine (1765) was the pivot point. Before it, factories had to be built next to rivers because waterpower was **geographically fixed** — the machine had to come to the energy source. Steam reversed that logic: **coal could be transported anywhere**, so factories could be built wherever labor and markets were.

Within 50 years of Watt's design, steam was powering trains. The implications compounded across decades:

- Textile mills → steam-powered, no longer river-dependent
- Mines → steam-driven pumps could drain deeper shafts, unlocking more coal
- Ships → see below
- Railroads → see below

### Iron: The Structural Material of the First Revolution

Coal also made **mass production of iron** possible. Key milestones:

- **Coke** (refined coal): replaced charcoal in furnaces, enabling much larger iron-smelting operations
- **Cast iron**: extremely strong under compression, but brittle — good for pillars and cannon, not for bending
- **Wrought iron** (Henry Cort, 1794): less strong than cast iron but far more workable — could be rolled, bent, and shaped → crucial for machinery, rails, bridges

> [!warning]
> Students often confuse iron and steel. Iron comes first and is the backbone of the *first* industrial revolution. Steel — stronger AND more workable than either kind of iron — only becomes cheap with the Bessemer Process in 1856, making it the key material of the *second* revolution.

### Steamships: Ending Wind Dependency

Before steam, ocean trade was hostage to winds, currents, and seasons. Steam changed three things:

1. **Schedules became reliable** — ships departed and arrived on time
2. **Rivers became two-way highways** — before steam, going upstream meant being towed by animals or people along the shore; steam-powered riverboats could travel **up to 5 mph upstream** independently
3. **Coaling stations became strategic assets** — steam ships needed coal at regular intervals, so whoever controlled refueling points controlled sea lanes

The **SS Savannah** (1819) became the first steam-powered ship to cross the Atlantic — a landmark demonstration of the technology's potential.

[[Concept Notes/Coaling stations|Coaling stations]] at chokepoints — [[Concept Notes/Cape Colony|Cape Colony]] (South Africa), Pacific islands, [[Concept Notes/Aden|Aden]] — became prizes of imperial competition. Control of coal = control of the sea.

## Second Industrial Revolution: Steel, Oil & Electricity (c. 1850–1914)

### Steel: Better Than Iron in Every Way

The **Bessemer Process** (1856) was the breakthrough. By blasting molten iron with air, impurities burned off and the metal remained workable while becoming enormously stronger. Steel is an alloy of iron and carbon — it combines iron's strength with workability that cast iron lacked.

Results of cheap steel:
- **Railroads** — steel rails lasted far longer than iron rails and could support heavier loads
- **Skyscrapers** — steel frames allowed buildings to rise many stories
- **Battleships** — steel-hulled warships replaced wooden and iron-hulled ones
- **Bridges** — steel enabled longer, stronger spans (Brooklyn Bridge, 1883)

Steel production became a measure of national industrial power. By 1900, **Germany and the United States had both surpassed Britain** in steel output — a signal of shifting global dominance.

### Oil: A New Fossil Fuel

Petroleum joined coal as a fossil fuel powering the industrial world:

- First commercial oil wells: **mid-1800s**
- **Kerosene** (extracted 1847): the first major petroleum product — used for lamps and heaters, it provided cheap lighting to households and factories
- The **internal combustion engine** followed from kerosene refining techniques → led directly to automobiles and airplanes
- By the early 1900s, **gasoline** overtook kerosene as petroleum's most important product — the automobile age had begun

> [!tip]
> On the AP exam, the transition from kerosene → gasoline maps neatly onto a CCOT argument: *what continues* (fossil fuel dependence, industrial capitalism) vs. *what changes* (from steam/coal to petroleum/electricity, from trains to cars).

### Electricity: The Invisible Infrastructure

Electricity transformed both production and daily life:

- **Effective electrical generator** developed in the late 19th century
- **First public power station**: London, 1882
- **Electric street lighting** and **electric trams**: 1890s
- Factories could now run independently of both waterways (old constraint) and coal furnaces (first-revolution constraint) — electricity arrived through wires

### Communications: Collapsing Distance

The second industrial revolution made **communication nearly instantaneous** across any distance for the first time in history:

| Technology | Date | Who | Significance |
|-----------|------|-----|-------------|
| **Telegraph** | Mid-1800s | Samuel Morse (and others) | Coded messages across continents via electrical wire; synchronized global markets |
| **Telephone** | Patent 1876 | [[Concept Notes/Alexander Graham Bell|Alexander Graham Bell]] | Voice transmission; Thomas Edison's refined transmitter (1886) made it practical |
| **Radio** | 1901 transatlantic signal | [[Concept Notes/Guglielmo Marconi|Guglielmo Marconi]] | Wireless communication; became mass media — the first technology to reach entire populations simultaneously |

The telegraph was the **nervous system** of the global economy: stock prices, military orders, commodity quotes, and shipping schedules could all move in near-real time across oceans.

## Railroads & the Global Economy

Railroads were the physical backbone tying everything together. They did for land what steamships did for sea:

- **Opened interior regions**: Before railroads, only coastal and riverine areas were economically accessible. Railroads unlocked continents — resources in the American West, Russian Siberia, South African interior, and Indian subcontinent.
- **Lowered transport costs dramatically**: Moving a ton of goods overland dropped from enormously expensive (horse-drawn wagon) to cheap (railroad freight)
- **Required massive capital**: Railroads were the first truly capital-intensive industry — governments subsidized them heavily

### Case Studies

**Transcontinental Railroad (U.S., completed 1869)**
Connected the Atlantic and Pacific coasts. Relied heavily on immigrant labor ([[Chinese immigration|Chinese laborers]] on the western half). Heavily subsidized by federal land grants. Opened the American interior to extraction and settlement, accelerating industrial growth.

**Trans-Siberian Railroad (Russia)**
Stretched from Moscow to the Pacific Ocean. Enabled Russia to trade with East Asia (China, Japan) and to assert strategic control over Siberia. Russia's industrialization clustered around this rail corridor in the 1890s.

> [!exam-tip]
> Railroads appear constantly in AP World History essays. When you use a railroad as evidence, explain the **mechanism**: it lowered transport costs → made interior resources accessible → enabled industrial nations to extract raw materials from distant regions → linked to New Imperialism. A strong LEQ never just names the technology; it explains **how** it caused change.

## How the Two Revolutions Made a Global Economy

The combination of railroads + steamships + telegraph created something genuinely new: a **global economy** in which distant producers, consumers, and investors were linked in near-real time.

Before this:
- Trade was slow, expensive, and limited largely to coastal zones and navigable rivers
- Communication took weeks or months across long distances
- Markets were regional, not global

After:
- A cotton farmer in Egypt, a textile mill in Manchester, a banker in London, and a consumer in Boston were all connected economically
- Price signals moved globally via telegraph — commodity prices became global, not local
- Industrial nations could extract resources from distant colonies efficiently, then sell finished goods back — the technological foundation of [[Concept Notes/New Imperialism|New Imperialism]]

> [!summary]
> The two industrial revolutions did not just change how things were made. They changed who had power, where resources flowed, which nations rose, and which declined. The technologies of Topic 5.5 are the direct cause of the imperial competition in Unit 6.
