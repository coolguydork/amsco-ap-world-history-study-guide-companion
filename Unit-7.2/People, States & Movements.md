# People, States & Movements

Topic 7.2 — Causes of World War I | Printed pp. 461–468

---

## Key Individuals

**Gavrilo Princip** (1894–1918)
Serbian nationalist and member of the [[Concept Notes/Black Hand|Black Hand]] secret society. On June 28, 1914, he assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie in Sarajevo, triggering the alliance cascade that became WWI. Born in Bosnia to a poor Serb family, Princip was motivated by intense hatred of Austro-Hungarian occupation of Slavic lands. His act illustrates how individual political violence, in the right structural conditions, can detonate a world war. He died in prison in 1918 from tuberculosis.

**Archduke Franz Ferdinand** (1863–1914)
Heir presumptive to the Austro-Hungarian throne and nephew of Emperor Franz Joseph. His assassination in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, served as the immediate trigger of WWI. Ironically, Franz Ferdinand was reportedly more conciliatory toward Slavic minorities than many Austrian officials — his death removed a potential moderate. The Austro-Hungarian government used his assassination as a pretext for an ultimatum that, when rejected, began the war.

**Kaiser Wilhelm II** (1859–1941)
German emperor whose aggressive, erratic foreign policy — naval buildup, colonial competition, blank-check support for Austria-Hungary in 1914 — contributed substantially to the war's outbreak. His ambition to make Germany a world power created anxiety among Britain, France, and Russia. He abdicated in November 1918 as Germany collapsed and went into exile in the Netherlands. Often cited as a key figure in debates about German war guilt.

**Otto von Bismarck** (1815–1898)
Prussian statesman who unified Germany (1871) and built the original alliance system to keep France isolated. His famous prophecy — "The next great European war will come out of some damned foolish thing in the Balkans" — opens the AMSCO chapter on WWI. After Bismarck's dismissal in 1890, Wilhelm II abandoned his careful balancing act, setting Europe on the path to war. Bismarck appears on the AP as a contrast: the diplomatic skill that *prevented* war vs. the recklessness that caused it.

**Woodrow Wilson** (1856–1924)
U.S. president who kept America neutral until 1917, then declared war after Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram. His **14 Points** (1918) proposed a just peace based on self-determination, freedom of the seas, arms reduction, and a League of Nations. Though Wilson championed self-determination, the Paris Peace Conference implemented it selectively — and the U.S. Senate rejected the League, undermining Wilson's peace vision.

## Key States & Empires

**Austria-Hungary**
Multinational empire that fired the first shots of WWI with its declaration of war on Serbia (July 28, 1914). Governed a patchwork of Slavic, Germanic, Magyar, and other ethnic groups whose nationalist movements posed an existential threat. Its harsh ultimatum to Serbia — designed to be rejected — reflects how its leadership saw war as a solution to the empire's nationalist instability. The empire collapsed entirely by 1918.

**Germany**
The most powerful Central Power, whose military buildup, colonial competition with Britain and France, and blank-check support for Austria-Hungary made it the central villain in Allied propaganda and at Versailles. Germany's Schlieffen Plan (invasion of France through Belgium) brought Britain into the war. Forced by the Treaty of Versailles to accept sole war guilt, pay massive reparations, and surrender territory — creating the resentment that fueled the rise of Hitler.

**Ottoman Empire**
Joined the Central Powers in 1914, seeking to recover territory lost in the Balkan Wars and check Russian expansion. Its entry opened a new front in the Middle East. Nationalist uprisings within the empire — Arab Revolt (1916), Armenian Genocide (1915–16) — reflected its internal fragility. The empire collapsed at the war's end; former provinces Iraq, Palestine, Syria, and Lebanon became British or French mandates. Connects to [[Unit-7.1/Shifting Power Overview\|Shifting Power Overview]].

**Serbia**
Small Slavic kingdom that became the flashpoint of WWI. Serbian nationalists wanted to unite all South Slavs in a "Greater Serbia" and end Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman presence in the Balkans. The Black Hand, with links to Serbian military intelligence, organized Princip's assassination. Serbia survived the war and became the core of the new Kingdom of Yugoslavia.

## Key Movements

**Serbian [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]]**
The driving force behind Princip's assassination and the Black Hand's activities. Serbian nationalists saw themselves as liberators of Slavic peoples from Austro-Hungarian domination. Russia supported Serbia as a fellow Slavic Orthodox nation, making Serbian nationalism the tripwire that activated the Russian-Austrian-German-French-British cascade. On the AP, Serbian nationalism is the clearest example of how nationalism was a *cause* of WWI.

**Pan-Slavism**
Broader movement asserting the cultural and political unity of all Slavic peoples. Russia positioned itself as the protector of Slavic peoples against Germanic and Ottoman domination — the reason it mobilized for Serbia in 1914. Pan-Slavism contributed to the Austro-Hungarian fear that its own Slavic minorities would defect and helps explain why the empire was so aggressive toward Serbia.

> [!exam-tip]
> For SAQ/LEQ compare-contrast: Serbia's nationalism in 1914 connects to **Arab nationalism** within the Ottoman Empire (also causes of WWI's extension) and both connect forward to **self-determination movements** in the colonies that accelerated after WWI weakened European empires.
