## What This Vault Is

This is a structured, interconnected Obsidian study system for AP World History: Modern. It is not a simple summary of a textbook. Every topic is built around the College Board's learning objectives, AP themes, and historical reasoning skills. Notes are designed to answer three questions that drive every AP exam prompt:

- **What happened, and why?** (causation)
- **What changed and what stayed the same?** (continuity and change over time)
- **How does this region or period compare to others?** (comparison)

Each of the 67 AP topics gets its own folder with up to seven note files: an overview, key terms and concepts, people and states, themes and historical reasoning, a timeline, practice and review questions, and a connections note. Cross-topic concepts — the Silk Roads, the Mongols, Buddhism, the Columbian Exchange — get dedicated hub notes in the `Concept Notes/` folder. All notes link to each other using Obsidian wikilinks, so studying one topic automatically surfaces related topics, people, empires, and ideas.

## AP Themes and Historical Reasoning Skills

### The Seven AP World History Themes

The College Board organizes all of AP World History around seven themes. Every note in this vault is tagged with the relevant themes, so you can filter by topic in Obsidian or quickly identify which lens to apply when writing an essay.

| Code | Full Name | What It Covers |
|------|-----------|----------------|
| `GOV` | Governance | State-building, empires, political structures, administration, legitimacy, law, power |
| `ECN` | Economic Systems | Trade, production, labor systems, exchange networks, capitalism, industrialization |
| `CDI` | Cultural Developments and Interactions | Religion, philosophy, science, arts, cultural exchange, syncretism, diffusion |
| `SIO` | Social Interactions and Organization | Social hierarchies, gender roles, family structures, race, ethnicity, class, slavery |
| `TEC` | Technology and Innovation | Technological change, agricultural innovations, tools, weapons, transportation |
| `ENV` | Humans and the Environment | Climate, disease, ecology, environmental change, geography's effect on history |
| `MIG` | Human Migration and Settlement | Migration patterns, diaspora communities, settlement, urbanization, forced and voluntary movement |

### The Five Historical Reasoning Skills

AP exam prompts — MCQs, SAQs, DBQs, and LEQs — always test one or more of these skills. Every `Themes & Historical Reasoning.md` note and every `Practice & Review.md` note is organized around them.

**Causation** — Explaining why something happened and what effects it produced. Look for immediate causes, underlying causes, and intended vs. unintended consequences.

**Comparison** — Identifying similarities and differences between societies, empires, time periods, or regions. Every Unit N.7 / N.8 / N.9 / N.10 topic is explicitly a comparison topic.

**Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)** — Identifying what transformed and what persisted across a time period. CCOT is the core skill of LEQ prompts.

**Contextualization** — Explaining the broader historical context that makes an event or development meaningful. Required for full credit on DBQ and LEQ.

**Argumentation** — Constructing a defensible, historically grounded argument with a thesis and supporting evidence. Required for LEQ and DBQ.

## Note Types — What Each File Contains

Every topic folder holds up to seven notes. Use the one that matches what you are trying to do right now.

### `[Region] Overview.md`
The primary study note. Explains what happened, why it mattered, and how to use this topic as evidence on the AP exam. Includes `[!exam-tip]` callouts highlighting frequently tested content, comparisons to related topics, and links to connected notes throughout the vault. **Best for:** initial reading, big-picture review before a test, understanding how a topic fits into AP World History as a whole.

### `Key Terms & Concepts.md`
An alphabetized glossary of every important term, person, empire, religion, and institution for the topic. Each entry is a bolded term followed by a definition and its exam significance. **Best for:** vocabulary review, checking definitions, pre-exam flashcard-style self-testing.

### `People, States & Movements.md`
Profiles of the key states, empires, rulers, religious movements, and historical figures for the topic, organized by region or role. Every entry includes a significance statement explaining *why* it matters for the exam. **Best for:** understanding who the major actors are, building your evidence base for SAQ and LEQ writing.

### `Themes & Historical Reasoning.md`
An analysis of the topic through the AP themes and historical reasoning skills. Includes historiographical debates (how historians interpret the topic differently), AP skill exercises modeled on the "Think As a Historian" sections, and exam-strategy callouts. **Best for:** DBQ contextualization, building LEQ arguments, understanding what historians argue about.

### `Timeline & Chronology.md`
A chronological table of every key date and event for the topic, with brief explanations of significance. **Best for:** sequencing events, tracing causation chains, quick review before a test.

### `Practice & Review.md`
AP-style multiple-choice questions, short-answer questions, and long-essay prompts. MCQ answers appear as `[!tip] Answer: **[Letter]**` callouts with full explanations. SAQ model responses appear as `[!tip] Model Response` callouts written at the 5-level. **Best for:** timed practice, self-assessment, understanding what the exam actually tests for this topic.

### `Connections.md`
A dedicated note mapping this topic's connections to the rest of AP World History — backward (what caused it), forward (what it caused), and lateral (what it compares to). **Best for:** finding contextualization for DBQs, building cross-topic LEQ arguments, exploring the vault's linked graph.

### Using the Graph View

Press `Ctrl+G` (or `Cmd+G` on Mac) to open Obsidian's Graph View. Every note appears as a dot, and every wikilink appears as a line connecting them. Topics that are central to AP World History — the Mongols, the Silk Roads, the Industrial Revolution — appear as larger hubs with many connections. Use the graph to see how topics relate, which is exactly the thinking that earns points on LEQ and DBQ prompts.

### Using Tags to Filter

Open the Tags pane in Obsidian's right sidebar to filter notes by theme or period:

- `period-1` — all Period 1 (c. 1200–c. 1450) notes
- `period-2` — all Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750) notes
- `trade-networks` — all notes touching trade systems
- `east-asia` — all notes covering East Asian history
- `concept-note` — all cross-topic concept hub notes

### Exam Tip Callouts

Throughout the vault, look for `[!exam-tip]` callouts. These highlight content that has historically appeared on AP exams, common essay topics, frequently tested vocabulary, and traps to avoid. If you are short on time before the exam, read these callouts first.

### Following Wikilinks

Every bolded person, empire, religion, trade network, or idea in the notes is wikilinked to either another topic note or a Concept Note. Click any link to navigate directly to more information. The more you follow links, the more connections you build — and making connections across topics is the single highest-value skill on the AP exam.

## For Instructors — Using This Vault in Your Class

### Sharing with students

Share the entire vault folder with your students by zipping it or putting it in a shared drive. Students open it as an Obsidian vault. No account or internet connection is required after initial setup.

### Using notes as lesson scaffolds

Each `[Region] Overview.md` is structured like a lesson outline. The `[!exam-tip]` callouts can be used to focus class discussion on what the College Board actually tests. The `Themes & Historical Reasoning.md` notes provide ready-made historiographical discussion starters.

### Using Practice & Review notes for in-class practice

The `Practice & Review.md` notes contain AP-style MCQ sets and SAQ prompts with model responses. These can be assigned as homework, used for in-class timed practice, or projected for group discussion. Model SAQ responses are written at the 5-level and can be used for annotation exercises.

### Using Connections notes for essay preparation

The `Connections.md` notes map out the cross-topic relationships students need to write strong LEQ and DBQ responses. Assign the Connections note for a topic before students write an LEQ to help them find contextualization and comparative evidence.

### Using the Concept Notes for thematic review

The `Concept Notes/` folder is ideal for thematic review sessions. Each hub note synthesizes content from across multiple AP topics. Assign a Concept Note as a reading before a thematic discussion or before a unit that builds on prior knowledge (e.g., assign the Silk Roads Concept Note before starting Unit 4's transoceanic trade topics).

### Adapting notes for your class

All notes are plain Markdown files. You can edit them directly in Obsidian to add your own examples, adjust emphasis, insert primary source links, or add class-specific annotations. Obsidian's graph view will update automatically to reflect any new links you add.

## Exam Strategy and AP Test Tips

### The AP World History: Modern Exam Format

The exam is 3 hours and 15 minutes total:

- **Section I, Part A:** 55 Multiple Choice Questions — 55 minutes
- **Section I, Part B:** 3 Short Answer Questions — 40 minutes
- **Section II, Part A:** 1 Document-Based Question (DBQ) — 60 minutes recommended
- **Section II, Part B:** 1 Long Essay Question (LEQ) — 40 minutes recommended

### What separates a 3 from a 5

MCQ performance matters, but free-response scores separate 3s from 5s. The DBQ and LEQ rubrics reward three things above all: a defensible thesis with a line of reasoning, specific and accurate evidence, and historical reasoning that goes beyond description. Every `Practice & Review.md` and `Themes & Historical Reasoning.md` note in this vault is written to develop exactly those skills.

### Historical reasoning skills for free response

**SAQs:** Each part (A, B, C) asks for causation, comparison, or CCOT. Use the `Themes & Historical Reasoning.md` note for the relevant topic to find pre-organized arguments. Use the `Connections.md` note for context.

**DBQs:** You need to: (1) contextualize before your thesis, (2) use all or all-but-one documents, (3) bring in outside evidence beyond the documents, and (4) analyze sourcing using HAPP (Historical situation, Audience, Purpose, Point of view). The `Themes & Historical Reasoning.md` notes provide sourcing language and historiographical context.

**LEQs:** Your thesis must make a historically defensible claim with a line of reasoning — not just restate the prompt. The `Connections.md` notes provide ready-made comparative and CCOT arguments that can anchor an LEQ thesis.

### High-frequency AP exam topics

Based on College Board released exams, the following appear most often in prompts and are worth extra study time:

- The Silk Roads and Indian Ocean trade networks (Units 2.1, 2.3)
- The Mongol Empire (2.2)
- The Columbian Exchange (4.3)
- The Atlantic slave trade and coerced labor (4.4, 4.5)
- The Industrial Revolution (5.3–5.5)
- New Imperialism (6.1–6.3)
- The causes and effects of World War I (7.2, 7.3)
- Decolonization and newly independent states (8.5, 8.6)

All of these topics have fully built note sets in this vault.
