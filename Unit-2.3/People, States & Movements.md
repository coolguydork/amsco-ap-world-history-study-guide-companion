# People, States & Movements — Exchange in the Indian Ocean

---

## Key People

**Zheng He (1371–1433)**
Chinese Muslim admiral who commanded seven monumental voyages across the Indian Ocean on behalf of Ming Emperor Yongle (1405–1433). His fleet reached Indonesia, Ceylon (Sri Lanka), Arabia, and the East African coast — at its height comprising over 300 ships carrying 28,000 people, the largest wooden vessels ever built. The voyages' primary purpose was tribute collection: displaying Ming power and receiving tokens of submission from Indian Ocean rulers. The voyages incidentally spread Chinese cultural influence and inspired emigration to Southeast Asian ports. Criticized by Confucian scholars as wasteful and culturally threatening, the voyages were ended by Yongle's successor Emperor Zhu Gaozhi, who banned multi-masted ship construction. Compare with [[Concept Notes/Tribute System|Tribute System]] (AMSCO p. 99).

> [!exam-tip]
> Zheng He is a frequent AP free-response subject. Key points: (1) voyages were **tribute missions**, not commercial exploration; (2) China **withdrew** from the Indian Ocean voluntarily — a contrast with European expansion in [[Unit-4.1/Technological Innovations Overview|Topic 4.1]]; (3) withdrawal had effects (piracy resumed in China Sea).

**Ibn Battuta (1304–1368/1369)**
Moroccan Muslim scholar and traveler who visited the Swahili coast, India, and Southeast Asia via Indian Ocean routes in the 1330s. His accounts (*Rihla*) are among the most valuable primary sources for the Indian Ocean trade world. His travels are referenced in [[Unit-2.5/Cultural Consequences Overview|Topic 2.5]] as evidence of Indian Ocean connectivity. See [[Unit-1.5/People, States & Movements|People, States & Movements]] for his African travels.

**Emperor Yongle (r. 1402–1424)**
Third emperor of the [[Concept Notes/Ming Dynasty\|Ming Dynasty]]; the patron of Zheng He's voyages. Yongle's sponsorship of the treasure fleets reflected an expansive, outward-looking Ming foreign policy. His death marked the turning point: successors dismantled the naval program and turned inward.

**Zhu Gaozhi (Emperor Hongxi, r. 1424–1425)**
Yongle's son and successor. Ended Zheng He's voyages, prohibited Chinese from sailing beyond Chinese waters, and made building a ship with more than two masts a punishable offense. His policies reflect the Confucian scholarly establishment's preference for agrarian stability over maritime commerce. His brief reign had outsized consequences for Chinese engagement with the Indian Ocean world (AMSCO p. 99).

**Chang Han**
Ming official (15th century) whose writings reflect the Confucian tension over trade and foreign commerce. He acknowledged trade's utility — "to use what one has to exchange for what one does not have is what trade is all about" — but dismissed merchants as overconfident and criticized foreigners as "recalcitrant" and greedy. His views capture the intellectual context behind the end of Zheng He's voyages (AMSCO p. 101).

**Arab and Persian merchants**
The dominant seafarers of the Indian Ocean for centuries before and during the Postclassical Era. Following the 7th-century expansion of Islam, Arab and Persian merchants became the primary connective tissue of the Indian Ocean network — settling in diasporic communities from East Africa to China. They brought Islam with them through intermarriage and cultural exchange, not conquest. See [[Concept Notes/Indian Ocean Trade Network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]].

## Key States & Polities

**Malacca (Melaka) Sultanate (c. 1400–1511)**
Muslim city-state at the strategic Strait of Malacca, controlling maritime traffic between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Built its wealth through tolls and fees rather than agriculture or manufacturing. The sultan extended Malacca's reach into Sumatra and the southern Malay Peninsula before the Portuguese conquest in 1511 ended its independent reign. The conquest scattered Malacca's merchants to other ports, diversifying — but not ending — Indian Ocean trade (AMSCO p. 97).

**Swahili City-States (c. 1200–1500)**
A string of Muslim-influenced port cities along East Africa's coast — Kilwa, Mombasa, Sofala, Mogadishu, Zanzibar — collectively known as the Swahili city-states. Their prosperity depended on Indian Ocean trade: exporting ivory, gold, enslaved people, and exotic goods; importing Chinese porcelain, Indian cotton, and ironwork. Architectural remains in Kilwa — stone and coral mosques and merchant houses — testify to peak-era wealth. See [[Unit-1.5/Africa Overview|Africa Overview]] for earlier East African context.

**Gujarat (Rajput Kingdom)**
Northwestern Indian kingdom that served as the great go-between for East-West Indian Ocean trade. Customs revenues from Gujarat's ports reportedly dwarfed the total wealth of some European states. Gujarat's merchants formed a significant diaspora across the Indian Ocean Basin (AMSCO p. 98).

**Ming China (1368–1644)**
The Chinese dynasty that sponsored Zheng He's voyages. Ming China was initially outward-looking under Yongle, then deliberately withdrew from the Indian Ocean after 1424. The decision not to sustain naval engagement had profound long-term consequences: it left the ocean open for Portuguese and later European intrusion. See [[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]] for the ideological context behind the withdrawal.

**Majapahit Empire (Java, c. 1293–1527)**
Hindu-Buddhist empire centered in Java (modern Indonesia) that controlled much of maritime Southeast Asia during the peak of Indian Ocean trade. A major commercial and cultural hub in the island world connecting the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea.

**Calicut (Kozhikode)**
Not a state but a powerful trading city on India's Malabar Coast, ruled by the Zamorin. Calicut was the premier Indian meeting point for Arab merchants from the west and Chinese merchants from the east. The Zamorin deliberately welcomed foreign merchants to maximize trade revenue. Later the site of Vasco da Gama's first Indian landfall (1498).

## Key Movements & Processes

**Spread of Islam via commerce**
The Indian Ocean trade was the primary vehicle by which Islam reached South and Southeast Asia. Arab and Persian merchants settled in port cities, married locally, and raised Muslim children. This "bottom-up" conversion through trade stands in contrast to conversion by conquest or missionary activity. By 1400, Islam had become the dominant religion of Malaysia, Indonesia, and the Swahili coast.

**Diasporic community formation**
Monsoon winds forced merchants to wait months in foreign ports, accelerating cultural exchange and intermarriage. Muslim, Chinese, Sogdian, Jewish, and Malay merchant diasporas formed across the basin, each occupying commercial niches while blending with local cultures. See [[Unit-2.3/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts]] for the diaspora table.

**Indian Ocean slave trade**
A long-running forced migration from East Africa to the Middle East, India, and North Africa — predating the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] by many centuries and peaking in the 18th–19th centuries. See [[Unit-1.5/People, States & Movements|People, States & Movements]] for earlier East African context.
