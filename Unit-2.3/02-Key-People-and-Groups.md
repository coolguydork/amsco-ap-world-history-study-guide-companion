# Unit 2.3: Key People and Groups — Indian Ocean Exchange

> [!exam-tip]
> For Indian Ocean trade, the AP exam focuses on **mechanisms** and **patterns** more than individual biographies. Know the roles of Arab merchants, the Swahili city-states, and Zheng He — these appear most frequently as stimulus contexts.

---

## Arab and Persian Merchants
**Time Period:** 7th–15th centuries
**Region:** Arabian Peninsula, Persian Gulf → Indian Ocean broadly

- Arab and Persian merchants dominated Indian Ocean trade from the rise of Islam onward, using their geographic position at the hub of the network to operate as middlemen between East Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Mediterranean
- They spread Islam wherever they traded, establishing diasporic communities that became nuclei of religious conversion — in East Africa (Swahili coast), South Asia (Malabar Coast, Gujarat), and Southeast Asia (Malacca)
- Their commercial practices — credit systems, bills of exchange, use of Islamic commercial law across different legal jurisdictions — made long-distance trust-based trade possible without state enforcement

## Swahili City-State Rulers and Merchants
**Time Period:** c. 1000–1500
**Region:** East African coast (Kilwa, Mombasa, Zanzibar, Malindi, Sofala)

- The Swahili city-states were governed by rulers who derived their wealth and power from taxing trade; they created a hybrid culture blending Bantu African traditions, Arabic commercial practices, and Islamic religious identity
- Kilwa, the most powerful city-state, controlled the southern gold trade from Great Zimbabwe; its prosperity was visible in its stone architecture (the Great Mosque of Kilwa) and the Chinese porcelain found in its archaeological remains
- Ibn Battuta visited Kilwa in 1331 and described the sultan as generous and the city as beautiful — his account is the primary written source for Swahili prosperity at its peak

## Zheng He (1371–1433)
**Time Period:** Early 15th century
**Region:** China → Southeast Asia → Indian Ocean → East Africa
*(Primary coverage: [[Unit-1.1/02-Key-People-and-Groups]]; covered here for his role in Indian Ocean exchange)*

- Chinese Muslim admiral who commanded seven massive naval expeditions (1405–1433) on behalf of the Yongle Emperor of the Ming Dynasty, visiting ports across Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf, and East Africa
- His fleet — hundreds of ships, including massive "treasure ships" — was the largest naval force in the world at the time; it demonstrated Chinese technological superiority and political ambition. The treasure ships' exact dimensions are debated: traditional accounts describe them as extraordinarily large, but modern historians' estimates range from around 200 to 400+ feet — even the lower estimates dwarf contemporary European vessels
- The voyages' primary purpose was diplomatic (collecting tribute, asserting Ming prestige) rather than commercial; after the Yongle Emperor died, a faction of Confucian bureaucrats opposed to the expense reversed the policy and restricted maritime expeditions. The claim that fleet records were deliberately burned is a traditional narrative, though the historical evidence for a deliberate, systematic burning is less clear-cut than often presented — the end result was that records and momentum were lost, and Chinese state maritime activity ceased

## Indian Ocean Merchants (Hindu and Jain Traders of Gujarat and the Malabar Coast)
**Time Period:** c. 1200–1450 and beyond
**Region:** Northwestern and southwestern India

- Hindu Bania merchants from Gujarat and Jain merchants from the same region were major commercial operators in the Indian Ocean, specializing in textile trade (Indian cotton cloth was a primary export) and using sophisticated credit and banking systems
- Unlike Muslim merchants, they did not spread their religion along trade routes — their commercial networks were ethno-religious in a different way, based on caste and family ties rather than shared faith
- Their presence in East African ports (they were established in Mombasa long before the Portuguese arrived) illustrates the multi-religious character of Indian Ocean trade

## Chinese Merchants and the Ming State
**Time Period:** c. 1200–1450
**Region:** China → Southeast Asia, Indian Ocean

- Private Chinese merchants operated throughout Southeast Asia, establishing diasporic communities in port cities that spread Chinese commercial practices, cultural influences, and goods (silk, porcelain) throughout the region
- The Ming Dynasty's relationship with maritime trade was deeply ambivalent: Zheng He's voyages (1405–1433) represented state-sponsored engagement, while the *haijin* (maritime prohibition) policies implemented from the 1370s and reimposed later attempted to restrict private maritime commerce
- The contrast between Zheng He's voyages and the subsequent Ming withdrawal is one of the AP exam's favorite "what if" comparisons — Chinese maritime power, had it continued, might have transformed Indian Ocean trade before European arrival

## Malay and Javanese Merchants and Rulers
**Time Period:** c. 1000–1500
**Region:** Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java

- Southeast Asian rulers and merchants controlled the critical Strait of Malacca chokepoint and the spice-producing islands of the Maluku archipelago
- The rulers of Malacca converted to Islam (c. 1400) and leveraged their position to become the dominant commercial hub of the region; their Islamic identity connected them to the broader Muslim merchant network and attracted Arab traders
- The Majapahit Empire of Java (c. 1293–1527) was the dominant Hindu-Buddhist maritime power of Southeast Asia before Malacca's rise, representing an earlier form of the region's engagement with Indian Ocean trade

## East African Enslaved People
**Time Period:** c. 1000–1500 (and long before and after)
**Region:** East Africa → Persian Gulf, Arabian Peninsula, India

- An often-overlooked group: enslaved East Africans were a significant commodity in Indian Ocean trade, transported northward to the Persian Gulf and South Asia by Arab merchants
- The Zanj Rebellion (869–883 CE) in the Abbasid Caliphate, led by enslaved East Africans working in salt marshes near Basra, is evidence of the scale of this trade in earlier centuries — it continued through the period 1200–1450
- Their presence in the Islamic world influenced culture and eventually produced communities (such as the Siddi community in South Asia) that are a direct legacy of Indian Ocean slave trade
