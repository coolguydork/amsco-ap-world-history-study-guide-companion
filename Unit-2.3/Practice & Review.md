# Practice & Review — Exchange in the Indian Ocean

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the Indian Ocean wind pattern map (AMSCO p. 100)*

**Question 1.** The map of winter wind patterns in the Indian Ocean helps explain why South Asian merchants preferred to travel —

(A) to Africa in the winter months
(B) to Southeast Asia in the winter months
(C) to China in the summer months
(D) to the Middle East in the summer months

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Winter monsoon winds blow from the northeast, pushing ships from South Asia toward East Africa. Option B is wrong — winter winds blow toward Africa, not Southeast Asia. Options C and D are reversed: summer winds blow southwest, carrying merchants from the Middle East toward South Asia, not the other way.

---

**Question 2.** Since merchants were often delayed in foreign ports for lengthy periods of time, they frequently —

(A) became involved in local political affairs
(B) married wives who often converted to their religion
(C) lost their connection to the culture of their homeland
(D) settled in the foreign port and converted to the local faith

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> AMSCO directly states that Arab and Persian merchants who stayed in Indian port cities married local women and raised Muslim children — spreading Islam through intermarriage, not conquest. Option A is a distractor (merchants occasionally influenced politics but this wasn't the primary effect). Option C is wrong: diasporic communities maintained their cultural identity. Option D reverses the dynamic — merchants generally preserved their own culture and converted locals, not the reverse.

---

**Question 3.** The coastal cities of Mombasa, Sofala, and Mogadishu often exported —

(A) silks and porcelain
(B) cotton and steel
(C) horses, figs, and dates
(D) enslaved people, ivory, and gold

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> These are Swahili city-states on East Africa's coast. AMSCO explicitly lists enslaved people, ivory, and gold (plus exotic goods like tortoise shells and rhinoceros horns) as their exports. Option A (silks/porcelain) = China. Option B (cotton/steel) = India. Option C (horses, figs, dates) = Southwest Asia.

## Short-Answer Questions

**SAQ 1.** Use the passage from Chang Han, Ming official (15th century), to answer all parts.

*"Merchants boast that their wisdom and ability are such as to give them a free hand in affairs… Foreigners are recalcitrant and their greed knows no bounds."*

**(A)** Identify ONE way the ideas of Chang Han provided the political context for Zhu Gaozhi's decision on Zheng He's voyages.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Chang Han reflects the Confucian suspicion of merchants and foreign engagement that pervaded the Ming scholarly establishment. His claim that merchants overestimate their own importance echoes broader elite distaste for commercial activity as a basis for foreign policy. Zhu Gaozhi, whose court was dominated by Confucian scholars hostile to Yongle's outward-looking policies, could cite such views to justify canceling voyages that critics saw as elevating merchants' interests over those of the agrarian state.

**(B)** Explain ONE way the ideas of Chang Han provided the economic context for Zhu Gaozhi's decision on Zheng He's voyages.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Chang Han acknowledges that trade between China and foreign nations has mutual economic benefit — "to use what one has to exchange for what one does not have is what trade is all about" — but frames foreign merchants as untrustworthy and greedy. This perspective supported the argument that the enormous cost of Zheng He's treasure fleets (over 300 ships, 28,000 men) was not economically justified, since the tribute goods brought back did not offset the expense. Zhu Gaozhi's decision to cancel the voyages reflected this calculation: the economic cost outweighed the benefit.

**(C)** Explain ONE way the ideas of Chang Han reflect a traditional Chinese view of China's relationship with other cultures.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison/Context
> Chang Han's dismissal of foreigners as "recalcitrant" and his suspicion of merchants reflect the Sinocentric worldview embedded in Confucian thought: China was the cultural center of the universe (*tianxia*, "all under heaven"), and other peoples were culturally inferior. This view supported the tribute system — foreign rulers should come to China bearing gifts, not the reverse. Zheng He's voyages were, in part, consistent with this worldview (they were tribute-collection missions), but the scale of Chinese engagement with foreign ports made Confucian scholars uncomfortable.

**SAQ 2.** Answer all parts.

**(A)** Identify ONE cause of expanded trade in the Indian Ocean Basin in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> One cause was the spread of Islam, which connected more port cities than ever before into a unified commercial network. Muslim merchants from Dar al-Islam established trading relationships from East Africa to Southeast Asia, creating demand for goods across a wider geographic zone and providing a shared legal and cultural framework (Islamic commercial law, the Arabic language) that reduced transaction costs.

**(B)** Explain ONE effect of expanded trade in the Indian Ocean Basin in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> One effect was the formation of diasporic merchant communities. As monsoon timing required merchants to wait months in foreign ports, Arab, Persian, and Chinese merchants settled permanently in distant cities, married locally, and spread their cultural and religious practices. In particular, Islam spread to South and Southeast Asia through these merchant diasporas rather than through conquest, fundamentally reshaping the religious geography of the Indian Ocean world.

**(C)** Explain the relationship between the expansion of maritime trade in the Indian Ocean Basin 1200–1450 and the expansion of trade over the land routes of the Silk Roads.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> The Indian Ocean maritime trade and the Silk Roads were complementary, not competing, systems. Both expanded together in the 1200–1450 period: many of the same goods (spices, silk, porcelain) moved through both networks, and diasporic Muslim merchants participated in both. The Mongol unification of Central Asia ([[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Topic 2.2]]) temporarily boosted Silk Road security and volume, which also fed demand for Indian Ocean goods. The key difference was environmental: maritime merchants depended on monsoon wind timing, making Indian Ocean routes seasonal, while Silk Road caravans operated year-round (weather permitting). The two networks reinforced each other by connecting different ends of the Eurasian-African exchange system.

## Historical Reasoning Practice (Think As a Historian)

The following connections use the three AP historical reasoning skills. Write your own version before reading the models.

**Comparison:** The expansion of sea routes for trade is like the expansion of the land-based Silk Roads because both extended global interconnections and depended on new technologies. They differ in mode of transport, dominant merchant groups, and specific commodity mixes.

**Causation:** China's withdrawal from Indian Ocean sailing after 1424 caused a return of piracy in the China Sea. The armed merchant fleet Zheng He commanded had suppressed pirates; without that naval presence, piracy resumed.

**Continuity and Change:** Indian Ocean trade flourished before and after the spread of Islam. The spread of Islam represents a change in the *scale* and *ideological framework* of the network — Muslim merchants now connected more ports through a shared commercial culture — but the fundamental monsoon-driven, commodity-exchange structure was continuous.

## Essential Question Response

**In 2–3 paragraphs, explain the causes and effects of the growth of networks of exchange after 1200 and the way environmental knowledge supported that expansion.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Synthesis
> The growth of Indian Ocean trade networks after 1200 had multiple interlocking causes. The spread of Islam connected Muslim merchants across East Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia into a shared commercial network, dramatically expanding the number of cities integrated into long-distance trade. Simultaneously, rising demand for specialized goods — spices from the Maluku Islands, textiles from India, gold and ivory from East Africa, porcelain from China — gave merchants strong economic incentives to make difficult ocean crossings. Environmental knowledge, particularly expertise in monsoon wind patterns, transformed these incentives into reliable reality: merchants who understood that winter monsoons carried ships toward Africa and summer monsoons reversed the flow could plan systematic, repeatable voyages rather than gambling on unpredictable weather.
>
> Maritime technology amplified these causes. Lateen sails, stern rudders, astrolabes, and the dhow design collectively reduced the physical difficulty and cost of ocean travel, enabling more participants to enter the network. States like Malacca institutionalized trade revenue through toll systems, further reducing risk for merchants by providing naval protection and stable commercial infrastructure.
>
> The effects were equally sweeping. Diasporic merchant communities formed wherever monsoon timing stranded merchants for months — and through intermarriage, these communities spread Islam to South and Southeast Asia more effectively than conquest ever had. Swahili city-states on East Africa's coast grew wealthy from the trade, transforming their architecture and culture. Zheng He's voyages briefly brought Chinese state power into the ocean at unprecedented scale before Confucian opposition ended them. By c. 1450, the Indian Ocean Basin was the world's most sophisticated commercial system — which is precisely why the Portuguese worked so hard to enter it after 1498.

## Quick Review: Causes vs. Effects

| Causes of Expansion | Effects of Expansion |
|---|---|
| Spread of Islam → more connected ports | Diasporic communities → Islam spreads |
| Demand for specialized goods | Swahili city-states flourish |
| Monsoon knowledge (environmental) | Gujarat and Malacca grow wealthy |
| Maritime tech (lateen, rudder, astrolabe) | Cultural transfers (language, religion, goods) |
| State growth (Malacca, Gujarat) | Indian Ocean slave trade expands |

> [!exam-tip]
> Notice that some **causes** (spread of Islam, state growth) also appear as **effects** — this is intentional. AMSCO notes that "some of the very factors that caused expansion also became effects." The AP loves to test whether students understand this feedback loop.
