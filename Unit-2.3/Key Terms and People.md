# Topic 2.3 — Key Terms and People

## Key People

**Zheng He (1371–1433)** — Chinese Muslim admiral commissioned by Ming emperor Yongle to lead seven great voyages across the Indian Ocean (1405–1433). His fleet of 300+ ships and 28,000 people visited Indonesia, India, Arabia, and East Africa. The voyages were tribute-collecting displays of Ming power, not commercial expeditions. Ended by Confucian opposition after Yongle's death. His story illustrates both the peak of Chinese maritime capability and the political factors that limited it.

## Key Places

**Calicut (Kozhikode)** — Major port city on India's southwest (Malabar) coast. Known as the "City of Spices." Meeting point for Arab and Chinese merchants. Described admiringly by Zheng He's crew and later by the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama (who arrived in 1498). (AMSCO p. 95–96)

**Malacca (Melaka)** — Muslim city-state on the Strait of Malacca in modern Malaysia. Grew wealthy by taxing all ships passing through the strait — the crucial bottleneck between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Expanded into Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. Conquered by the Portuguese in 1511.

**Spice Islands** — Modern Indonesia and Malaysia (especially the Maluku Islands/Moluccas). Source of nutmeg, cinnamon, cloves, and cardamom — among the most valuable commodities in medieval and early modern trade.

**Swahili Coast** — The East African coast (modern Kenya, Tanzania, Mozambique) lined with wealthy trading city-states including Kilwa, Mombasa, and Zanzibar. Named for the Swahili language — a Bantu-Arabic creole that reflects centuries of Indian Ocean cultural mixing.

**Gujarat** — Kingdom on India's northwest coast; a crucial intermediary in Indian Ocean trade. Customs revenue was many times greater than entire European states.

## Environment & Technology

**Monsoon winds** — Seasonal wind system of the Indian Ocean: winds blow from the northeast in winter (driving ships toward East Africa and Southeast Asia), then reverse from the southwest in summer (driving ships back). Merchants had to time voyages carefully, often waiting months in port cities — which is exactly how diasporic communities formed.

**Dhow** — Small wooden sailing vessel used by Arab and Indian sailors across the Indian Ocean. Known for its triangular lateen sail. The most common ship on Indian Ocean trade routes during the Postclassical Era.

**Lateen sail** — Triangular sail that could catch winds from multiple directions; gave sailors flexibility in the variable wind conditions of the Indian Ocean. Associated with Arab sailors; possibly adopted from South Asian or Mediterranean origins.

**Stern rudder** — Device attached to the rear of a ship to control direction; Chinese innovation that gave ships better maneuverability than earlier steering-oar systems.

**Astrolabe** — Navigational instrument improved by Muslim astronomers in the 12th century; allowed sailors to measure the angle of the sun or stars and determine their latitude. Critical for open-ocean navigation away from coastlines.

**Magnetic compass** — Chinese navigational invention; spread via the Indian Ocean and Silk Roads networks; revolutionized ability to navigate in cloudy or nighttime conditions.

## Economy & Society

**Indian Ocean Basin** — The broader geographic system encompassing the Indian Ocean and its surrounding coastlines — East Africa, Arabian Peninsula, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and southern China. The largest maritime trade zone in the premodern world.

**Diasporic community** — A settlement of people living away from their original homeland who maintain cultural, religious, and economic ties to that homeland. Indian Ocean trade generated diasporic communities of Arab, Persian, Chinese, Jewish, and Malay merchants at ports throughout the region.

**Indian Ocean slave trade** — Long-running trade in enslaved people primarily from East Africa, sold to buyers in the Middle East, North Africa, and India. Distinct from the later Atlantic slave trade in scale, type of labor, and the presence of some legal protections for enslaved people under Islamic law.

**Swahili city-states** — Wealthy East African commercial cities along the coast (Kilwa, Mombasa, Zanzibar, Mogadishu, Sofala). Their prosperity was based entirely on trade — ivory, gold, slaves, and exotic goods in exchange for Chinese porcelain, Indian cotton, and iron goods. Built of stone and coral at peak prosperity.

## Key Connections to Themes

> [!exam-tip]
> On comparison questions, the Indian Ocean trade network is often paired with either the Silk Roads (overland vs. maritime) or with the Atlantic trade (c. 1500–) to illustrate how trade systems evolve. Know the similarities and differences cold.

**How Islam spread in the Indian Ocean world vs. by conquest**:
- Indian Ocean: *peacefully*, through merchant diasporas and intermarriage
- Compare to: Conquest of Persia, Central Asia (forced/political conversion)
- This distinction matters for AP themes of cultural diffusion

**Swahili language as evidence of cultural mixing**:
- Swahili = blend of Bantu (African) + Arabic; still spoken by 200 million people today
- Physical evidence of Indian Ocean trade's lasting cultural legacy
