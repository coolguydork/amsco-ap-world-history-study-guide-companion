# Unit 4.7: Changing Social Hierarchies from 1450 to 1750

#apwh #unit-4 #period-4

## AP Exam Period
**Period 4: 1450–1750**

## Summary

The period 1450–1750 produced dramatic transformations in social hierarchies across the globe, driven primarily by the encounter between European, Indigenous American, and African peoples. In Spanish and Portuguese colonial Americas, new race-based social categories emerged — the casta system — ranking people by their degree of European, Indigenous, and African ancestry and translating these categories into legal rights, occupational access, and social status. The Atlantic slave trade created the African diaspora and embedded racial chattel slavery at the foundation of Atlantic economies. Gender roles were also transformed: colonial encounters created new vulnerabilities for Indigenous and African women while also, paradoxically, creating spaces for some women (like Queen Nzinga) to exercise unusual political authority. Beyond the Americas, social hierarchies in Asia and Africa were also reshaped by commercial contact with Europeans, though less dramatically. The period is defined by the emergence of race as a defining social category in the Atlantic world — a development with consequences extending to the present day.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain how the casta system functioned in Spanish colonial society.
- Analyze how the Atlantic slave trade reshaped social structures in the Americas and Africa.
- Evaluate how gender roles were transformed by colonial encounters.
- Connect social hierarchy changes to the economic systems of the period.

## AP Key Concepts

- **KC-4.2.II** — The Atlantic slave trade led to the development of new forms of racial hierarchy.
- **KC-4.2.III** — New social and economic relationships were forged in colonial societies.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the **casta system** and the **racialization of slavery** as connected phenomena. Be ready to explain both and to distinguish the social structures of colonial Latin America from those of North American British colonies.
