# Changing Social Hierarchies

## Essential Question
How were social categories, roles, and practices maintained or changed from 1450 to 1750?

## Overview

Between 1450 and 1750, the expansion of gunpowder empires, European colonialism, and global trade networks dramatically reshaped social hierarchies across the world. Societies did not simply inherit their old class structures — they adapted them, imposed new ones, or constructed entirely new systems of categorization based on race, religion, ethnicity, and military power.

The period's key theme is **transformation under pressure**: rulers used social hierarchies to maintain control (granting land, enforcing religious conformity, assigning caste), while marginalized groups — enslaved Africans, indigenous peoples, religious minorities, serfs — struggled within systems designed to limit their mobility.

## Key Regional Patterns

**Gunpowder Empires (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal)**
All three Islamic gunpowder empires balanced a warrior aristocracy with scholars (ulama) and bureaucrats. The Ottoman Empire extended relative tolerance to Jews and Christians, who lived under the *millet* system as protected minorities paying the *jizya* tax. The Mughal emperor Akbar the Great (r. 1556–1605) went further, abolishing the jizya and integrating Hindu elites into his administration — a deliberate strategy to hold together a fractious, multi-ethnic empire. Women could wield indirect political power through *harem politics*, as demonstrated by Roxelana, an enslaved woman who became Suleiman the Magnificent's wife and a significant political actor. (AMSCO pp. 251–253)

**Qing China**
When the Manchu established the Qing Dynasty (1644–1912), they ruled as an ethnic minority over a Han Chinese majority — a situation directly parallel to Mongol rule some 300 years earlier. The Qing enforced cultural submission by requiring all men to wear their hair in queues (Manchu braids), executing those who refused. They used Han Chinese defectors — Li Chengdong and Liu Liangzuo — to carry out massacres of Han who resisted. At the same time, the Qing maintained the civil service examination system, a continuity that helped legitimize their rule. (AMSCO pp. 253–254)

**Europe**
European social hierarchies centered on royalty and a landed nobility. In England and the Netherlands, nobles actively participated in government; in France, Louis XIV deliberately neutralized the nobility by keeping them at Versailles, dependent on royal patronage. The emerging middle class of merchants and skilled workers challenged the old order from below. Jews, expelled from Spain in 1492, found varying degrees of acceptance across Europe — welcomed in the Ottoman Empire and the Netherlands, but marginalized in much of the continent. Those from Spain became known as *Sephardic Jews*; those from central/eastern Europe, *Ashkenazi Jews*. (AMSCO pp. 254–255)

**Russia**
Russian society preserved the Kievan Rus social order: boyars (noble landowners) at the top, merchants in the middle, and peasants at the bottom who gradually fell into serfdom. Serfs were bound to the land and transferred with it — not technically enslaved, but severely restricted in freedom. Ivan IV ("Ivan the Terrible") crushed boyar power in Novgorod, confiscating lands and forcing nobles to Moscow where he could surveil them. (AMSCO p. 255)

**Americas: The Casta System**
The most radically new social hierarchy of the period emerged in Spanish and Portuguese colonial Latin America. The *casta* system formally ranked people by racial ancestry:

| Level | Group | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (top) | Peninsulares | Born in Iberian Peninsula |
| 2 | Criollos | European ancestry, born in Americas |
| 3 | Mestizos | Mixed European + indigenous |
| 4 | Mulattos | Mixed European + African |
| 5 | Zambos | Mixed indigenous + African |
| 6 (bottom) | Indígenas / Enslaved Africans | — |

Racial assignment happened at baptism; people could not legally move up except through intermarriage. Lower tiers paid higher taxes and tributes. (AMSCO pp. 255–256)

## Big-Picture Significance

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves comparing social hierarchies across regions. Be ready to compare the casta system (Americas) with the Qing ethnic hierarchy (China), Ottoman millet system (Middle East), and European nobility (Europe) — all created during the same period and all reflecting how rulers used social categories to maintain control.

> [!tip]
> Key continuity: coercive labor (serfdom in Russia, slavery in the Americas, debt labor) persisted across the period even as new forms emerged. Key change: race replaced religion and class as the primary marker of status in the colonial Americas — a genuinely new development.
