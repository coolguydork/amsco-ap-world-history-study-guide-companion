# Topic 4.7 – Changing Social Hierarchies

**Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections | Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)**

## Learning Objectives

- Explain how social categories, roles, and practices were maintained or changed from 1450 to 1750.
- Identify the social hierarchies that developed in different regions during this period.
- Analyze how race, ethnicity, religion, and class shaped social structures in colonial and non-colonial contexts.
- Compare social hierarchies across the Ottoman Empire, Qing China, European states, Russia, and the Americas.

## The Big Picture

In every part of the world connected by the Columbian Exchange and maritime empire, **social hierarchies were being created, reinforced, or challenged**. The critical insight for AP World History is that social hierarchy in this period was not static — it was actively constructed through law, violence, religious authority, and economic power. And the primary new factor in this construction was **race**, which emerged as a formal social organizing principle in the Americas in ways it had not before.

## Ottoman Social Hierarchy

### The Warrior Aristocracy and Janissaries

The Ottoman social system was built around a warrior aristocracy that competed for positions in the imperial bureaucracy with the **ulama** — scholars and experts in Islamic law. Within the military, the **Janissaries** (elite infantry troops, originally recruited as children from Christian families) gained enormous power and prestige — ultimately attempting coups against ineffective sultans.

As sultans became increasingly weak, strong advisors called **viziers** spoke for the sultan and wielded great practical power. The sultan retained the power of **timar** — granting land or tax revenues to reward soldiers and loyal followers — as a key tool for maintaining loyalty.

### Treatment of Religious Minorities

One reason for Ottoman success was relative religious **tolerance** toward Jews and Christians, in contrast to contemporary European persecution. After Spain expelled Jews in 1492, **Sultan Mehmed II** invited them to settle in Istanbul. Many did, contributing as court physicians, diplomats, and literary figures.

But Ottoman tolerance was not equality:
- Non-Muslims were restricted to specified areas of cities
- They paid the **jizya** tax (required of all non-Muslims in the empire)
- They could not hold top imperial positions

### Women in the Ottoman Empire: Roxelana

Women played political roles at court through the **harem** — the residence of a powerful man's wives and concubines. "Harem politics" — wives and concubines promoting their sons as heirs — was a constant feature of Ottoman court life.

**Roxelana**: A remarkable exception to typical women's social limitations. Kidnapped from Eastern Europe as a child, sold into slavery in the Ottoman Empire, converted to Islam, and placed in Suleiman the Magnificent's harem. Suleiman eventually **married** her — extraordinarily unusual. She became politically powerful, commissioning public works projects. Her son succeeded Suleiman. Viziers complained about a "sultanate of the women," evidence of how unusual (and threatening to men) her power was.

> [!tip]
> Roxelana's story shows that social mobility was possible — but rare, and usually achieved through extraordinary personal circumstances. She went from enslaved woman to commissioning public buildings. Use her as an example of women's political influence *within* a system that generally excluded them.

## Qing Dynasty: Manchu over Han

China's **Qing Dynasty (1644–1912)** was founded when the Manchu people from Manchuria conquered the Han Chinese. Like the Mongols 400 years earlier, the Manchu were ethnically and culturally distinct from those they ruled.

**Qing methods of control and integration**:
- Put Manchu people in top government positions (like the Mongols)
- Maintained Chinese civil service exams and bureaucracy (unlike the Mongols, who had initially abolished them)
- Required all men to wear their hair in **queues** (braided Manchu pigtail style) — a test of loyalty; refusing could mean execution
- Some Han Chinese defectors carried out massacres of those who resisted Qing authority

**Qing-Han tensions**: The queue policy was not just about hair — it was a **humiliating symbol** of Qing authority challenging traditional Confucian values. The Qing used some Han Chinese defectors to enforce their rule through violence, creating a pattern of collaborators and resisters that defined Qing-era social relations.

Over time, many (but not all) Han Chinese came to accept Qing legitimacy — particularly because the Qing maintained key Chinese institutions like the exam system.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Qing are a useful comparison to the Mongols (Yuan Dynasty). Both were foreign dynasties ruling China. The Mongols maintained more cultural distinctiveness; the Qing adapted more Chinese institutions. But both imposed some practices of their own culture (queues for the Qing, different administrative systems for the Mongols). This comparison appears on AP exams regularly.

## European Social Hierarchies

### The Nobility's Changing Position

In Europe, the social pyramid ran: royalty → nobility/aristocracy → merchants/middle class → peasants/serfs → enslaved people. Nobles were usually wealthy landowners who inherited their status and land through primogeniture.

**Where nobles were strong (Netherlands, England)**: Dutch landowners provided stable provincial governance. In England, large landowners controlled Parliament — but they also had to contend with radical religious sects and a growing merchant middle class.

**Where nobles faced pressure (France)**: Louis XIV (after the failed Fronde rebellion) carefully kept nobles away from real power. He is famous for saying "I am the state." He used court culture at Versailles to control noble behavior, keeping them distracted with ceremonial life rather than political power.

**Gunpowder equalizing technology**: Cannons and firearms allowed rulers to destroy nobles' fortresses and seize lands, undercutting the military advantage that had traditionally given nobles their power. This technological shift supported the rise of **absolute monarchs**.

**Growing acceptance of Jews**: Starting in the 17th century, under the influence of the Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment, prejudices against Jews declined somewhat in parts of Europe. Jews became important in banking and commerce. The Netherlands was particularly tolerant. Jews expelled from Spain in 1492 became known as **Sephardic Jews** (Sepharad = Hebrew for Spain); those from central and eastern Europe were **Ashkenazi Jews**.

## Russian Social Hierarchy

Russia's social structure built on that of Kievan Rus:
- **Boyars** (noble landowning class) at the top
- **Merchants** below them
- **Peasants/serfs** at the bottom

**Ivan IV ("Ivan the Terrible")**: Opposed by boyars of Novgorod who resisted his expansionist policies. After defeating Novgorod, Ivan confiscated boyar lands and forced opponents to Moscow where he could surveil them. His murder of his own son is emblematic of his volatile, brutal rule.

**The serf-boyar-tsar triangle**: Boyars constantly struggled with tsars for power, while serfs (bound to the land by the 1649 law) struggled to survive and occasionally rebelled (see Pugachev Rebellion, [[Unit-4.6/Challenges Overview]]).

## Social Hierarchies in the Americas: The Casta System

The encounter of Europeans, Africans, and indigenous Americans in the colonial Americas created entirely new social structures. **Race** became the primary organizing principle — a formal, legal system with profound consequences.

### The Casta Hierarchy (Latin America)

From top to bottom:

1. **Peninsulares**: Born on the Iberian peninsula (Spain or Portugal); held top positions; most powerful
2. **Criollos/Creoles**: Of European ancestry, born in the Americas; politically dominant locally, but below peninsulares
3. **Castas**: People of mixed-race ancestry, in descending status:
   - **Mestizos**: Mixed European and indigenous ancestry
   - **Mulattoes**: Mixed European and African ancestry
   - **Zambos**: Mixed indigenous and African ancestry
4. **Indigenous peoples** and **Enslaved Africans**: At the bottom

People were assigned to their casta level **at baptism** and generally could not move up except through intermarriage. Higher taxes fell on lower casta groups, who could often least afford them.

> [!exam-tip]
> The casta system is a prime example of how European colonizers used **race** as a social organizing tool. Before 1492, race in the modern sense barely existed as a legal category. The casta system created it — turning biological ancestry into legal status. It profoundly shaped Latin American social structures for centuries and remains relevant today.

The Spanish actually commissioned **casta paintings** — artistic depictions showing the different mixed-race groups of colonial society — to reinforce the hierarchy visually and socially.

**Skin color as power**: Across European colonies worldwide, skin color became a signifier of power and status. White Europeans or Americans of European descent held the majority of wealth and political power. This was not accidental — it was a deliberate legal construction.

### Creoles and Independence

By 1750, creoles had achieved substantial local political power in New Spain. Their growing ambitions — frustrated by the glass ceiling of peninsulares' dominance — would eventually fuel the independence movements of the early 19th century. See [[Unit-5.1/Atlantic Revolutions Overview]].

## AP Themes

**Social Structures**: This topic is entirely about social structure. The key argument: social hierarchies in this period were *constructed* through law, violence, and ideology — they were not natural or inevitable. Race was invented as a legal category in the colonial Americas.

**Governance**: States actively created and enforced social hierarchies — through the casta system (Spain), the queue requirement (Qing), the jizya (Ottoman), the serf laws (Russia). Social control and political control were deeply intertwined.

**Cultural Developments**: Religious identity — Muslim vs. Christian, Catholic vs. Protestant, Confucian vs. other — shaped social hierarchies everywhere. The Ottoman tolerance of religious minorities was unusual; Spanish Catholicism's role in the casta system was extreme.
