# People, States & Movements

## Key Individuals

**Mehmed II (r. 1451–1481)**
Ottoman sultan who consolidated the empire after the conquest of Constantinople (1453). Crucially for Topic 4.7: after Ferdinand and Isabella expelled Jews from Spain in 1492, Mehmed II's invitation to Jewish refugees to settle in Istanbul reflected Ottoman religious pragmatism — skilled Jews contributed as physicians, diplomats, and trade intermediaries. His policies illustrate the Ottoman millet system's relative tolerance. (AMSCO p. 252)

**Akbar the Great (r. 1556–1605)**
Greatest Mughal emperor. Akbar pursued systematic religious tolerance as deliberate imperial policy: he abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims, funded a Catholic church, gave grants to Hindus and Muslims alike, and supported Sikhism. He also created the Din-i-Ilahi ("Divine Faith"), a syncretic spiritual movement that drew on multiple religions. His rule is the clearest example of a [[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|gunpowder empire]] using religious inclusion rather than coercion to maintain social order. See [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]] for broader context on Mughal imperial development. (AMSCO p. 252)

**Roxelana (c. 1500–1558)**
Originally enslaved in Eastern Europe and sold into the Ottoman harem, Roxelana rose to become the official wife of Suleiman the Magnificent — a stunning social reversal. Her son succeeded Suleiman. She commissioned public works projects and wielded political influence that led critics to decry a "sultanate of the women." Her story is the AP exam's go-to example of social mobility through harem politics and the exceptional — but real — paths available to women in this era. (AMSCO p. 252)

**Suleiman the Magnificent (r. 1520–1566)**
Ottoman sultan at the height of the empire's power. His marriage to Roxelana was remarkable precisely because it violated convention. His reign illustrates both the height of Ottoman power and the emerging tension around harem influence. (AMSCO p. 252)

**Louis XIV of France (r. 1643–1715)**
The "Sun King" who famously declared "I am the state." Louis centralized royal power by keeping the French nobility at the Palace of Versailles, where they were financially dependent on royal patronage and politically neutralized. This strategy is the European parallel to how other rulers used social hierarchy — redefining the nobility's role from independent power-holders to court ornaments. (AMSCO p. 254)

**Ivan IV ("Ivan the Terrible," r. 1547–1584)**
First tsar of Russia. Ivan's suppression of the Novgorod boyars — confiscating their lands and forcing them to Moscow — represents the Russian tsarist strategy of subordinating the noble class to centralized royal authority. His violence (including murdering his own son) earned his sobriquet. (AMSCO p. 255)

**Li Chengdong**
Han Chinese defector general who served the Qing Dynasty. Li orchestrated three massacres in the city of Jiading within a single month as the Qing enforced cultural submission. His actions illustrate how the Qing used Han collaborators to suppress Han resistance. (AMSCO p. 254)

**Liu Liangzuo**
Another Han Chinese defector who massacred the entire population of Jiangyin, killing between 70,000 and 100,000 people in 1645. These massacres were the Qing's most violent tool for enforcing the queue requirement. (AMSCO p. 254)

## Key States & Empires

**Ottoman Empire**
The most religiously tolerant of the gunpowder empires in practice. The Ottoman millet system allowed Christians and Jews to govern their own communities under their own laws while paying the jizya. The Janissaries (elite military corps) rose in power and frequently challenged sultans, illustrating the tension between military elites and centralized rule. Located primarily in Anatolia, the Middle East, and southeastern Europe.

**Mughal Empire (India)**
Founded 1526 by Babur; peaked under Akbar. The Mughal case is central to the AP exam because Akbar's tolerance is the clearest example of a ruler *deliberately reshaping* social hierarchy to sustain imperial rule over a diverse population. The Mughal Empire ultimately failed to maintain Akbar's tolerance — later emperors like Aurangzeb reversed course, reimposing the jizya and persecuting Hindus, contributing to the empire's eventual fragmentation. See [[Unit-4.6/Internal & External Challenges to State Power Overview|Internal & External Challenges to State Power Overview]].

**Qing Dynasty (China, 1644–1912)**
Manchu rulers over a Han Chinese majority. The Qing maintained the civil service examination system (continuity) while enforcing cultural submission through the queue requirement (change). The contrast between Qing ethnic domination and Ottoman/Mughal tolerance is a productive AP comparison.

**Spanish Colonial Empire (Americas)**
Created the casta system — the most systematized racial hierarchy of the period. Administered through viceroyalties (New Spain and Peru), the empire formalized racial categories in law, art (*casta paintings*), and the Church. Peninsulares and criollos monopolized political office; enslaved Africans and indigenous peoples bore the heaviest tax and labor burdens.

## Key Movements & Patterns

**Harem Politics (Ottoman)**
The informal political competition among women in the sultan's harem to influence succession. While not an organized movement, harem politics represented a real avenue of female political influence in an otherwise male-dominated imperial system.

**Jewish Diaspora after 1492**
Ferdinand and Isabella's Edict of Expulsion (1492) displaced the largest Jewish community in Europe. Jews dispersed across the Mediterranean, becoming Sephardic Jews. The Ottoman Empire's welcome of Jewish refugees stands in sharp contrast to European persecution. Under Enlightenment influence, European attitudes began to soften somewhat by the 17th–18th centuries.

**Qing Conquest Resistance**
Han Chinese resistance to Qing rule — including refusal to wear the queue — resulted in massive massacres. The Qing conquest is a textbook example of a new ethnic minority imposing cultural assimilation on a conquered majority.

> [!exam-tip]
> For SAQ and LEQ comparisons, Roxelana (Ottoman) vs. female rulers generally is a productive comparison for gender and power. Akbar (Mughal tolerance) vs. Ferdinand and Isabella (Spanish expulsion of Jews) is a high-frequency comparison for religious policy and social hierarchy.

> [!tip]
> **Continuity thread:** Rulers across every region in this period used social hierarchy as a tool of political control — granting status to allies (Ottoman timar, Mughal land grants, Russian boyar system) and stripping it from rivals (Ivan IV's boyar confiscations, Qing queue enforcement, Louis XIV's Versailles strategy).
