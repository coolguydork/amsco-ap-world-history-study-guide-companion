# Topic 2.7 — Comparison of Economic Exchange

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 2.7 is the **synthesis and comparison** topic for Unit 2. The AP exam loves it for LEQs and DBQs. Master the three-column comparison (Silk Roads / Indian Ocean / Trans-Saharan) cold: what was similar, what was different, and *why*.

## The Big Picture

Between c. 1200 and c. 1450, three major interregional trade networks stitched Africa, Asia, and Europe together: the [[Unit-2.1/Silk Roads Overview|Silk Roads]], the [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Overview|Indian Ocean]] trade routes, and the [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview|Trans-Saharan Trade Routes]]. Topic 2.7 asks you to zoom out and compare all three simultaneously — what made them alike, what made them different, and what those differences meant for the people who lived along them.

As the traveler Abd al-Razzaq wrote in his *Description of Calicut* (1442), wealthy merchants could "unload and unhesitatingly send [their goods] into the markets without thinking in the meantime of any security" — a detail that captures the interconnected, trust-based commercial world these networks created. (AMSCO p. 127)

---

## Similarities Among the Networks

All three networks shared the same fundamental origins, purpose, and effects. (AMSCO pp. 128–129)

**Origins** — Each network built on routes traced by earlier agrarian societies and expanding empires. Crucially, all three depended on the existence of **stable polities** — kingdoms, caliphates, city-states, or empires — that guaranteed security along the routes, protected merchants, and invested in commercial infrastructure. The [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]] (1206–1368) is the clearest example: the [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]] unified the overland Silk Roads under a single protective umbrella, causing trade to surge.

**Purpose** — All three networks served primarily economic ends: exchanging what one region could produce for what it lacked. But merchants, diplomats, and missionaries all traveled the same roads, so cultural and religious exchange was an inevitable byproduct. None of the three networks was purely commercial.

**Effects — Trading Cities** — Every network gave rise to "knots" — trading cities located at key junctions where goods changed hands, currencies were exchanged, and merchants rested. These cities were the engines of economic centralization:

| Route | Major Trading Cities |
|---|---|
| Silk Roads | Chang'an, Samarkand, Aleppo, Mosul |
| Indian Ocean | Malacca, Calicut, Hormuz, Mombasa, Alexandria |
| Trans-Saharan | Gao, Timbuktu, Marrakesh, Cairo |

**Effects — Centralization** — Trading cities used their commercial wealth to fund navies and police forces, deterring piracy and banditry. Malacca, for example, levied fees on ships passing through the Strait of Malacca and used the revenue to build a powerful navy. This dynamic pushed all three networks toward greater political centralization. (AMSCO p. 129)

**Effects — Standardized Currency** — All three networks drove demand for widely accepted currencies to speed transactions. Over time, states shifted from commodity currencies (silk as currency on the Silk Roads; cowrie shells in West Africa; tin ingots in Southeast Asia) toward metal coin economies. China went furthest, inventing "flying cash" (fei-qian) — the precursor to paper money — and developing early credit institutions. (AMSCO p. 131)

**Effects — Disease Spread** — The same connectivity that moved goods moved pathogens. The [[Concept Notes/Black Death|Black Death]] spread along all three networks simultaneously in the mid-14th century. China suffered massive outbreaks in the 1330s and 1350s; Europe lost at least a third of its population; great trading cities like Constantinople declined. (AMSCO p. 132)

## Differences Among the Networks

Despite their similarities, the three networks differed significantly in the goods they carried, the transportation technologies they required, and the religions they spread. (AMSCO pp. 130–131)

| Feature | Silk Roads | Indian Ocean | Trans-Saharan |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary goods** | Luxury goods: silk, tea, spices, porcelain, paper, gunpowder (east → west); horses, fruit, textiles (west → east) | Bulky goods: gold, ivory, textiles, peppers, pearls, citrus, dates | Salt (north → south); gold, ivory, enslaved people, cloth (south → north) |
| **Transportation** | Horses and camels on overland caravan routes; caravanserais for rest | Dhows and junks powered by monsoon winds | Camel caravans across the desert; people often walked |
| **Key technologies** | Saddles, caravanserais | Stern rudder, lateen sail, astrolabe, magnetic compass | Saddles for heavier loads |
| **Religions spread** | Buddhism (South Asia → East/Southeast Asia); Neo-Confucianism (China → Korea/Japan/Vietnam); Islam (SW Asia → South Asia) | Buddhism; Neo-Confucianism; Islam (South Asia → Southeast Asia); Christianity (Mediterranean) | Islam (SW Asia and North Africa → Sub-Saharan Africa) |

> [!tip]
> The **Indian Ocean route** carried the heaviest and most voluminous goods because ships could handle bulk cargo that overland camels could not. As maritime volume grew, Indian Ocean trade eventually surpassed the overland Silk Roads — a key CCOT point for the period after 1450.

**Currencies also differed.** Silk functioned as currency on the Silk Roads at various times. Cowrie shells served as money in West Africa. Tin ingots were used in parts of Southeast Asia. These differences reflect how regional environments shaped commercial practices.

## Social Implications

The rising demand for luxury goods pushed production to new levels. China underwent **proto-industrialization** — a dramatic scaling-up of iron, steel, and porcelain output to meet export demand. New **business practices** emerged, including partnerships that spread investment risk across multiple merchants. (AMSCO p. 131)

**Labor** — Growing production demanded more workers. Labor forms from earlier periods persisted: free peasant farmers, craft workers in cottage industries, debt laborers, and enslaved people. Trade in enslaved people was particularly common on the Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan routes. Large-scale projects — irrigation canals, fortifications, grand buildings — mobilized thousands of organized laborers, often coordinated through kinship networks. (AMSCO pp. 131–132)

**Gender and Social Structures** — Most societies between 1200 and 1450 remained patriarchies defined by class or caste. Trade created some exceptional spaces for women, however. In Southeast Asia, women were recognized as skilled market operators, controlling trade on behalf of powerful families — Zhou Daguan observed in Cambodia (1296–97) that "the local people who know how to trade are all women." Mongol women had greater freedom than their counterparts elsewhere in Afro-Eurasia. In Europe, women worked as farmers and artisans and had their own guilds. Yet these remained exceptions: in most regions, women experienced sharply fewer opportunities than men. (AMSCO pp. 132–133)

## Environmental Consequences

Trade intensified human impact on the environment. Population growth driven by commercial prosperity spurred deforestation and overgrazing; soil erosion from deforestation forced growing populations to migrate. Political instability and agricultural expansion compounded these pressures. See [[Unit-2.6/Environmental Consequences Overview|Environmental Consequences Overview]] for full treatment. (AMSCO p. 132)
