# Unit 2.7: Key People and Groups — Comparison of Economic Exchange

## Individuals and Groups

### Ibn Battuta
**Period:** 1304–c. 1368 | **Network(s):** Indian Ocean, Trans-Saharan, Silk Roads

- A Moroccan Muslim scholar who traveled over 75,000 miles across the Islamic world over three decades, visiting the Swahili Coast, Mali, the Delhi Sultanate, Southeast Asia, and China
- His *Rihla* (travel account) is the best primary source for understanding how all three trade networks functioned and intersected in the 14th century; he traveled on Indian Ocean dhows, Trans-Saharan camel caravans, and Silk Road routes
- His travels were made possible by the shared infrastructure of the Islamic world — shared language (Arabic), shared law (sharia), and shared hospitality networks (caravanserai, Muslim merchants)

> [!exam-tip]
> Ibn Battuta is the AP exam's go-to primary source for Unit 2. When you see him as a stimulus, think: **evidence of Islamic connectivity**, **cross-network travel**, and **the cosmopolitan nature of the Indian Ocean world**.

---

### Marco Polo
**Period:** 1254–1324 | **Network:** Silk Roads (primarily)

- A Venetian merchant who traveled to the court of Kublai Khan in China during the Pax Mongolica, spending 17 years in the Yuan Dynasty before returning to Europe
- His account, *The Travels of Marco Polo*, introduced European audiences to the wealth of East Asian commerce and the breadth of the Mongol Empire; it directly inspired later European interest in finding sea routes to Asia
- His journey was only possible because of the Pax Mongolica — the Mongol political framework that guaranteed safe passage for merchants along the Silk Roads

---

### Arab and Persian Merchant Communities
**Period:** c. 800–1450 | **Network:** Indian Ocean (primary), Trans-Saharan (secondary)

- Arab and Persian Muslim merchants dominated the western Indian Ocean from the Persian Gulf and Red Sea to the East African coast and Indian ports; they were the primary agents of Islam's spread throughout the network
- Used shared Islamic commercial law, Arabic as a lingua franca, and diasporic community ties to maintain trust and enforce contracts across vast distances without state authority
- Distinguished from Silk Road merchants by their commercial rather than tribute-based orientation: they were private actors, not state agents

---

### Chinese Merchant Communities and the Tribute System
**Period:** c. 1200–1450 | **Network:** Indian Ocean (eastern), Silk Roads

- Chinese merchants participated in Southeast Asian and Indian Ocean trade but operated partly within the Ming tribute framework — foreign traders came to China as "tributaries," not as equal commercial partners
- The contrast between Chinese merchants (who often operated despite official disapproval) and Arab/Indian merchants (who operated within a commercially celebrated framework) illustrates how Confucian and Islamic attitudes toward trade differed
- Zheng He's voyages (1405–1433) represent the Ming state's brief attempt to extend the tribute system throughout the Indian Ocean — a fundamentally different model from the decentralized Arab commercial network

---

### Tuareg and Berber Camel Traders
**Period:** c. 800–1600 | **Network:** Trans-Saharan

- Indigenous North African and Saharan peoples who controlled the desert crossings, acting as guides, escorts, and traders between West African kingdoms and the Mediterranean coast
- Converted to Islam early and served as vectors of Islamic spread southward into the Sahel and West Africa; their knowledge of Saharan geography was irreplaceable
- Distinguished from Indian Ocean merchants by the physical danger and geographic specificity of their route — the Saharan crossing limited participation to those with specialized knowledge

---

### Mansa Musa and the Mali Empire
**Period:** r. 1312–c. 1337 | **Network:** Trans-Saharan

- The emperor of Mali whose legendary hajj to Mecca in 1324–1325 — during which he distributed so much gold that he depressed Cairo's gold market for a decade — demonstrated the wealth generated by the Trans-Saharan gold trade
- Mali's state-centered model of trade (controlling gold sources and taxing trans-Saharan commerce) contrasts with the decentralized, merchant-driven Indian Ocean model
- His hajj also illustrates how the Trans-Saharan network connected West Africa to the wider Islamic world — Mansa Musa returned with architects, scholars, and artisans who transformed Timbuktu

---

### Swahili City-State Merchants
**Period:** c. 900–1500 | **Network:** Indian Ocean

- The Swahili city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa, Zanzibar, Malindi) on the East African coast were both producers (gold, ivory, enslaved people) and intermediaries in the Indian Ocean network
- Swahili culture emerged from the synthesis of Bantu African traditions with Arab and Persian Islamic influence — a direct product of Indian Ocean commercial connectivity
- Distinguished from Trans-Saharan traders by their maritime orientation and their role as the African node of the Indian Ocean rather than the overland Saharan network
