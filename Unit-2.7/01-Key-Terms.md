# Unit 2.7: Key Terms — Comparison of Economic Exchange

## Vocabulary

**Silk Roads** — The network of overland trade routes connecting China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean; named for silk but also carrying porcelain, spices, paper, and ideas. (Significance: the world's longest overland trade corridor; transmitted Buddhism, Islam, plague, and technology across Eurasia. See [[Unit-2.1/00-Overview]].)

**Indian Ocean network** — The maritime system connecting East Africa, Arabia, Persia, India, Southeast Asia, and China, powered by seasonal monsoon winds. (Significance: the world's most commercially active maritime zone in this period; primary vehicle for Islam's spread to East Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. See [[Unit-2.3/00-Overview]].)

**Trans-Saharan trade** — Overland exchange across the Sahara Desert connecting West African kingdoms to North Africa and the Mediterranean, primarily trading gold and salt. (Significance: Islam spread along these routes to Mali and other West African states; gold from the Sahel financed Mediterranean commerce. See [[Unit-2.4/00-Overview]].)

**Pax Mongolica** — "Mongol Peace" — the period of relative political stability across Eurasia under Mongol rule (c. 1260–1350) that enabled safe travel and trade along the Silk Roads. (Significance: made possible the journeys of Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta; also enabled plague transmission — showing that connectivity has both costs and benefits. See [[Unit-2.2/00-Overview]].)

**Monsoon winds** — Seasonal wind patterns over the Indian Ocean blowing northeast in winter and southwest in summer, enabling predictable maritime travel. (Significance: the physical mechanism underlying all Indian Ocean trade; merchants planned commercial cycles around the monsoons, making the network both reliable and limiting.)

**Caravanserai** — Roadside inns spaced roughly a day's journey apart along Silk Road routes, providing food, shelter, water, and security for merchants and their animals. (Significance: state-funded infrastructure that made long-distance overland trade viable; demonstrates how states could facilitate commerce without directly controlling it.)

**Dhow** — A traditional Arab sailing vessel with a lateen (triangular) sail, designed to exploit monsoon winds; the workhorses of Indian Ocean trade. (Significance: the primary commercial vessel of the Indian Ocean network; their design allowed sailing against the wind and enabled reliable trans-oceanic navigation.)

**Camel caravan** — A trading convoy using camels as pack animals, capable of crossing the Sahara or Central Asian steppes; the backbone of both Trans-Saharan and Silk Road overland trade. (Significance: the dromedary camel's ability to go days without water made the Saharan crossing possible; without camels, Trans-Saharan trade as practiced in this period would have been impossible.)

**Diasporic merchant community** — A group of merchants from a shared ethnic or religious background living permanently in a foreign port city, maintaining trade networks through family and community ties. (Significance: the Indian Ocean network was sustained by Arab, Indian, Chinese, and Swahili diasporic communities rather than by state control — a fundamentally different commercial model from the state-sponsored Silk Roads.)

**Entrepôt** — A trading post or port city where goods are collected, stored, and redistributed between different trade networks; a commercial hub. (Significance: cities like Malacca, Kilwa, Calicut, and Hormuz served as entrepôts connecting the Indian Ocean network to regional economies; they demonstrate how geography enables commercial power.)

**Bulk goods vs. luxury goods** — Bulk goods (grain, timber, salt) are high-weight/low-value items moved over short distances; luxury goods (silk, spices, porcelain, gold) are high-value/low-weight items that justify long-distance transport costs. (Significance: all three major networks primarily carried luxury goods — only the Indian Ocean at its commercial peak began incorporating bulk goods. This distinction explains who participated in each network and who profited.)

**Bills of exchange** — Commercial instruments allowing merchants to transfer money across distances without physically moving coins; developed in the Islamic world and Italian banking. (Significance: financial innovation that reduced the risk and cost of long-distance trade; shows how commercial needs drive institutional innovation.)

**Islamic commercial law** — A body of legal principles derived from sharia governing contracts, partnerships, interest, and dispute resolution. (Significance: provided a shared legal framework across the Indian Ocean network, allowing merchants from different regions to trade with enforceable rules — a substitute for the political authority that underpinned the Silk Roads.)

**Tribute system** — A system in which subordinate states or peoples offer regular payments of goods or services to a more powerful state in exchange for recognition, protection, or trade access. (Significance: China's Ming tribute system organized East Asian trade very differently from the commercial logic of the Indian Ocean or the trans-Saharan networks — an important comparison point.)

**Trade diaspora** — A network of merchant communities of the same ethnic or cultural origin living across different cities and regions, linked by shared culture and family ties rather than political authority. (Significance: the Indian Ocean network was organized around trade diasporas — Arab, Indian, Chinese — rather than state structures; compare with the state-centered Mongol Silk Roads.)
