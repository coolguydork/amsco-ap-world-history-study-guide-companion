## Multiple Choice Practice

**Question 1**

The similarities among the Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, and trans-Saharan trade networks between c. 1200 and c. 1450 are best explained by which of the following?

A. All three networks were controlled by a single imperial power that standardized commercial practices.
B. All three networks depended on stable polities to protect merchants and support commercial infrastructure.
C. All three networks exchanged the same luxury goods, including silk, spices, and gold.
D. All three networks were dominated by Muslim merchants who imposed uniform trading rules.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> All three networks required stable kingdoms, caliphates, or city-states to guarantee security, support infrastructure, and issue currencies. (A) is wrong because no single empire controlled all three. (C) is wrong — the goods differed significantly by network. (D) is wrong — Muslim merchants were prominent on all three but did not impose uniform rules.

**Question 2**

The trans-Saharan trade network differed from the Indian Ocean trade network primarily in that the trans-Saharan routes

A. carried a higher volume of goods due to the use of large sailing vessels.
B. were characterized by the exchange of bulk commodities like salt and gold rather than maritime luxury goods.
C. were not influenced by the spread of Islam, which was confined to coastal regions.
D. did not give rise to significant trading cities or commercial centers.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The trans-Saharan network's defining feature was the salt-gold exchange — complementary commodities in high regional demand. (A) is wrong — the Indian Ocean used large ships; trans-Saharan used camels. (C) is wrong — Islam spread deeply into West Africa via trans-Saharan routes. (D) is wrong — Timbuktu and Gao were major commercial and intellectual centers.

**Question 3**

Which of the following best explains why Indian Ocean trade increasingly surpassed the overland Silk Roads in volume by the 15th century?

A. The destruction of trading cities along the Silk Roads by the Black Death reduced their commercial capacity permanently.
B. Ships on the Indian Ocean could carry bulk goods at lower cost than overland camel caravans, and the collapse of the Pax Mongolica removed the Silk Roads' security advantage.
C. Chinese merchants abandoned the Silk Roads after the Ming dynasty banned overland trade with Central Asia.
D. The Indian Ocean network became more profitable after Portuguese explorers established direct trade routes with West Africa.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Two factors drove the maritime shift: maritime transport is cheaper for bulk goods, and the Pax Mongolica's collapse after 1368 eliminated the overland safety advantage. (A) overstates the Black Death's lasting commercial impact. (C) is not accurate — the Ming conducted overland trade. (D) is anachronistic — Portuguese Indian Ocean exploration comes after 1450, outside this period.

**Question 4**

The excerpt below is from Zhou Daguan's *Customs of Cambodia* (1296–97): *"The local people who know how to trade are all women. So, when a Chinese goes to this country, the first thing he must do is take in a woman, partly with a view to profiting from her trading abilities."*

Which of the following best describes what this source illustrates about gender roles in Southeast Asia during this period?

A. Women in Southeast Asia were equal to men in all aspects of political and economic life.
B. Trade networks created specific regional contexts where women's economic participation exceeded the patriarchal norm.
C. Chinese merchants introduced the practice of women trading to Southeast Asian markets.
D. The Indian Ocean trade network universally elevated women's economic status across all participating regions.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Zhou Daguan's account shows women controlling local commercial exchanges in Cambodia — an exception to the general pattern of patriarchy. (A) overstates the case; women's political power remained limited. (C) reverses the dynamic — local women had this expertise before Chinese merchants arrived. (D) is wrong — the Indian Ocean network did not universally improve women's status; Empress Xu's China is a counterexample.

## Short-Answer Question (SAQ)

**Prompt:** Use the following excerpt and your knowledge of world history to answer the questions below.

*"Wealthy merchants bring in big cargoes, which they unload and unhesitatingly send into the markets without thinking in the meantime of any security, or checking the account, or keeping watch over the goods."* — Abd al-Razzaq, *Description of Calicut* (1442)

**Part A.** Briefly explain ONE similarity between the Indian Ocean trading city described by Abd al-Razzaq and trading cities along a different network (Silk Roads or trans-Saharan) during the period c. 1200–1450.

**Part B.** Briefly explain ONE difference between the Indian Ocean trading network and either the Silk Roads or trans-Saharan routes during the same period.

**Part C.** Briefly explain ONE reason why trade networks such as the one described by Abd al-Razzaq grew in importance during the period c. 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A (Similarity)
> Calicut, like Samarkand on the Silk Roads, was a trading city that served as a "knot" in its network — a hub where merchants from multiple regions exchanged goods, paid fees, and rested. Both cities grew wealthy from commercial activity and used that wealth to develop political and administrative structures. Both also became centers of learning and cultural exchange, illustrating that educational institutions tended to emerge wherever trade concentrated wealth.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B (Difference)
> Unlike the Silk Roads, which specialized in high-value, low-weight luxury goods (silk, porcelain, spices) that justified the expense of overland caravan transport, the Indian Ocean network carried heavy bulk commodities — gold, ivory, textiles, timber — that would be too expensive to move by camel. Ships powered by monsoon winds made it economically viable to transport these bulkier goods, so the Indian Ocean carried a greater volume and variety of goods than the overland routes.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C (Growth)
> The Pax Mongolica (c. 1206–1368) dramatically increased the security and connectivity of overland trade, while simultaneously making the Indian Ocean network more valuable as a complementary and eventually rival route. More fundamentally, stable polities across Afro-Eurasia — caliphates, sultanates, city-states, and empires — created the legal and physical infrastructure (harbors, navies, currencies) that allowed merchants to invest in long-distance trade with confidence, as Abd al-Razzaq's account of security in Calicut illustrates.

## DBQ Practice — Unit 2 Review: Women's Roles c. 1200–1450

The Unit 2 Review includes a seven-document DBQ set on the topic of gender roles across different societies c. 1200–1450. (AMSCO pp. 138–140)

**DBQ Prompt (paraphrased):** Evaluate the extent to which women's roles in society changed across different regions during the period c. 1200–1450.

**Document summary:**

| Doc | Source | Key Argument |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Ibn Battuta on Mali (1352) | Matrilineal inheritance; women interact freely with men — unusual by Islamic standards |
| 3 | Empress Xu, *Instructions for the Inner Quarters* (early 15th c.) | Confucian ideal of female submission; women should follow their husbands completely |
| 4 | Drawing of Tomoe Gozen (c. 1157–1247) | Female samurai — rare exception in Japanese military culture |
| 5 | Zhou Daguan on Cambodia (1296–97) | Women dominate local commerce in Southeast Asia |
| 6 | Joan of Arc's letter to the King of England (1429) | European woman claiming divine military authority — exceptional case |
| 7 | Juzjani on Sultan Raziyya (r. 1236–1240) | First female ruler of Delhi Sultanate — acknowledged as capable, but undermined for her gender |

**Thesis model:**

> [!tip] Model Response — DBQ Thesis
> Although patriarchy remained the dominant gender norm across virtually all regions connected by the trade networks of c. 1200–1450, trade activity created specific regional contexts — particularly in Southeast Asia and West Africa — where women exercised unusual economic authority, while political and military crises occasionally allowed exceptional individual women to operate outside their prescribed roles, revealing the conditional and contested nature of gender norms in this period.

**Writing guidance:** The strongest DBQ argument for this prompt acknowledges both the continuity (patriarchy was the norm across all regions) and the meaningful exceptions (Southeast Asia, West Africa, the Mongol Empire), while explaining the *context* that made those exceptions possible — trade exposure, specific inheritance systems, or crisis conditions.

## LEQ Practice

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which the major trade networks of c. 1200–1450 were more similar than different.

**Argument outline:**

- **Thesis:** Although the Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, and trans-Saharan networks shared common origins in stable state power and all gave rise to trading cities as commercial hubs, they differed significantly in the goods they carried and the technologies they required — differences driven primarily by geographic and environmental factors.
- **Similarity point 1:** All three depended on stable polities for security and infrastructure.
- **Similarity point 2:** All three generated trading cities that became centers of commerce, currency, and learning.
- **Difference point 1:** The goods differed by route — luxury goods overland, bulk goods by sea, gold-salt across the Sahara — because geography determined what transport was economical.
- **Difference point 2:** The technologies differed — caravanserais and saddles for overland; lateen sails, stern rudders, and astrolabes for maritime.
- **Complexity/Synthesis:** These differences matter because they help explain the maritime shift: as demand for bulk goods grew, sea transport's economic advantages over overland caravans grew, ultimately making the Indian Ocean dominant and pushing European powers to seek oceanic routes to Asia.
