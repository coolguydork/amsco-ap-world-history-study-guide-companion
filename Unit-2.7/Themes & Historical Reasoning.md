# Themes & Historical Reasoning — Topic 2.7

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 2.7's core AP skill is **Comparison** — the ability to identify and explain similarities and differences across the three networks. Almost every AP exam question on this topic will ask you to compare. A strong comparison always includes both a similarity AND a difference, and explains *why* each exists.

---

## AP Theme 1: Economic Systems (ECN)

**How trade networks functioned.** All three networks emerged from the same basic economic logic: exchange what you can produce for what you cannot. But the *way* each network organized exchange differed significantly. The Silk Roads prioritized luxury goods — silk, porcelain, spices — that were high in value relative to their weight, because the overland journey was expensive and slow. The Indian Ocean carried bulk goods — textiles, grain, timber, gold, spices — because ships could handle heavy cargo that camels could not. The trans-Saharan network was defined by a stark geographic complementarity: West Africa had gold but desperately needed salt; North Africa had salt but needed gold. The desert itself shaped what was traded.

**State role.** All three networks depended on stable polities to function. States built infrastructure (caravanserais, harbors, warehouses), guaranteed legal protection for merchants and their contracts, issued currencies, and suppressed piracy and banditry. The [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]] is the clearest case: Mongol military power made the overland Silk Roads safer than at any time since the Han dynasty, causing commerce to surge. When the Mongol Empire fragmented after 1368, overland trade declined.

**Proto-industrialization.** As demand from the trade networks grew, China dramatically scaled up production of iron, steel, and porcelain — a process sometimes called proto-industrialization. New business practices emerged, including merchant partnerships that distributed investment risk. The money economy deepened, with China inventing flying cash (fei-qian) and early credit instruments. (AMSCO p. 131)

**The maritime shift.** By the late 14th and early 15th centuries, the volume of goods moving by sea on the Indian Ocean began to surpass the overland Silk Roads. This shift accelerated after the Mongol Empire's collapse disrupted overland routes and after Ottoman expansion in the 15th century made the Silk Roads more difficult for European merchants. The stage was set for the European turn to oceanic exploration in the period after 1450 — the opening of Unit 3. (AMSCO curated key dates)

## AP Theme 2: Social Interactions and Organization (SIO)

**Labor systems.** Trade intensified labor demands without fundamentally transforming labor forms. Free peasant farmers, cottage-industry craft workers, debt laborers, and enslaved people all coexisted. The trade in enslaved people was especially significant on the Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan routes, foreshadowing the much larger slave trades of the early modern period.

**Gender structures.** The overwhelming norm across all three networks' societies was patriarchy — hierarchical gender systems in which men controlled political, economic, and domestic life. Trade created a few exceptional spaces for women:

- **Southeast Asia:** Women dominated local markets, operating as commercial intermediaries for powerful families. Zhou Daguan (1296–97) noted that Chinese men trading in Cambodia sought out local women partners specifically for their trading expertise. (AMSCO p. 139)
- **West Africa:** Ibn Battuta (1352) was shocked by Mali's matrilineal inheritance system and women's relative freedom in public space, noting inheritance passed "to the sons of his sister to the exclusion of his own sons." (AMSCO p. 138)
- **Mongol Empire:** Mongol women had greater freedom of movement and refused practices like foot binding and the veil that restricted women elsewhere in Afro-Eurasia.
- **Europe:** Women participated as farmers, artisans, and guild members.

These remain exceptions. Empress Xu's *Instructions for the Inner Quarters* (early 15th c.) and the resistance faced by Sultan Raziyya (r. 1236–1240) both illustrate how deeply patriarchal norms constrained even elite women. (AMSCO pp. 138–140)

> [!warning]
> Don't overstate women's freedom in this period. The exam expects you to acknowledge exceptions (Southeast Asia, West Africa, the Mongol Empire) while recognizing that patriarchy was the dominant system across virtually all regions.

**Class and caste.** Social hierarchies were typically determined by birth — class in most of Afro-Eurasia, caste in South Asia. Trade wealth could sometimes allow merchant families to gain prestige, but it rarely disrupted the fundamental hierarchy.

## AP Theme 3: Cultural Developments and Interactions (CDI)

**Religious diffusion via trade.** Each network became a conduit for religious spread, but the specific religions and destinations differed by route. [[Concept Notes/Buddhism|Buddhism]] spread from South Asia to East and Southeast Asia primarily via the Silk Roads and Indian Ocean. [[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]] spread from China to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam via the same routes. Islam spread via all three networks but in different directions: from Southwest Asia across the Silk Roads into South Asia; across the Indian Ocean into Southeast Asia; and across the Sahara into sub-Saharan West Africa. Christianity spread primarily through the Mediterranean Basin. (AMSCO p. 130)

**Educational centers.** Great trading cities became centers of scholarship. Samarkand, Timbuktu, Cairo, and Venice all developed famous institutions of learning — a pattern found across all three networks. The Ulugh Beg Madrasa built in Samarkand (1417–1422) is a famous example of how trade wealth funded intellectual culture. (AMSCO p. 129)

**Syncretism.** As religions traveled along the trade routes, they adapted to local conditions. West African Islam, for example, blended with existing matrilineal social practices that Ibn Battuta found puzzling. See [[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]] and [[Unit-2.5/Cultural Consequences Overview|Cultural Consequences Overview]].

## Historical Perspectives: How Brutal Was Genghis Khan?

The AMSCO unit review poses this historiographical debate directly. (AMSCO p. 134)

**The Destroyer view** — Military historian Steven R. Ward writes that "overall, the Mongol violence and depredations killed up to three-fourths of the population of the Iranian Plateau." Total Mongol-attributed deaths run into the tens of millions. Stories of massacres and the deliberate use of unarmed civilians as human shields support a picture of calculated brutality.

**The Empire Builder view** — Mongolian historians argue the brutality is exaggerated and that Khan was a sophisticated state-builder: he unified China, revived Eurasian trade through the [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]], operated a meritocracy, established a single writing system, and demonstrated genuine religious tolerance. From this perspective, the long-term gains in connectivity and cultural exchange outweigh the violence.

**The "Man of Energy" synthesis** — A Persian historian's assessment tries to hold both: "Genghis was possessed of great energy, discernment, genius, and understanding, awe-inspiring, a butcher, just, resolute, an over-thrower of enemies, intrepid, sanguinary, and cruel." This both/and framing is actually a reasonable historiographical position — the destruction was real, and so were the long-term commercial and cultural consequences.

> [!tip]
> For LEQs asking you to "evaluate the extent" of the Mongols' impact on trade or culture, the best answers acknowledge complexity: the destruction was massive, and so was the commercial and cultural integration that followed.

## AP Historical Reasoning Skills

**Comparison** — The primary skill for 2.7. Always structure comparisons around a point of similarity AND a point of difference, with evidence for each. The strongest comparisons also explain *why* the similarity or difference exists (often: geographic and environmental factors).

**Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)** — How did the relative importance of each network change between 1200 and 1450? Key CCOT argument: the Pax Mongolica boosted the Silk Roads in the 13th century, but the maritime Indian Ocean routes grew in relative importance throughout the period and eventually surpassed overland trade.

**Causation** — Why did stable states matter so much for trade? Because merchants would not risk capital on dangerous routes. State protection was the precondition for commercial investment.
