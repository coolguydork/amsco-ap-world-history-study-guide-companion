# People, States & Movements — Topic 2.7

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 2.7 is the comparison topic, so many of its key "people" appear in the Unit 2 Review DBQ documents — primary sources that illustrate gender roles, trade practices, and state power across different societies. Know these sources and what argument each one supports.

---

## Primary Source Voices

**Abd al-Razzaq al-Samarqandi** (1413–1482)
Persian scholar and traveler who visited Calicut in 1442 and wrote *Description of Calicut*. His account opens Topic 2.7 with the observation that merchants could leave their goods in the market "without thinking in the meantime of any security" — a testament to the legal and commercial stability that trading cities provided. His memoir is evidence of how state protection enabled Indian Ocean commerce. (AMSCO p. 127)

**Ibn Battuta** (1304–c. 1368/1369)
North African Muslim traveler who visited the court of Mansa Musa, ruler of Mali, in 1352. His account (*Rihla*, "My Travels") describes social customs that differed sharply from Islamic norms — including matrilineal inheritance and women interacting freely with men outside their families. Ibn Battuta's disapproval reveals how West African cultures adapted Islam while maintaining pre-Islamic social practices. Useful DBQ evidence for comparing gender roles across networks. (AMSCO p. 138)

**Zhou Daguan** (c. 1266–c. 1346)
Chinese diplomat who served the Yuan Emperor and traveled to the Khmer Empire (Cambodia) in 1296–97. His *Customs of Cambodia* records that "the local people who know how to trade are all women" — a striking observation about Southeast Asian women's role as market intermediaries. This source is often used on DBQs examining how trade created unusual opportunities for women in specific regions. (AMSCO p. 139)

**Empress Xu** (d. 1407)
Third wife of the Ming Emperor Yongle (r. 1402–1424). Her text *Instructions for the Inner Quarters* defines ideal womanhood as "upright and modest, reserved and quiet" and instructs wives to follow their husbands completely. This source represents Confucian patriarchal ideals at the height of the Ming dynasty — useful contrast to the Southeast Asian and West African sources on women. (AMSCO p. 138)

**Domingo Paes** (active c. 1520–1522)
Portuguese traveler who visited the Vijayanagara Empire in South India. His narrative describes thousands of laborers — "so many people at work that there must have been fifteen or twenty thousand men, looking like ants" — completing a giant reservoir under the direction of the king's captains. Illustrates how kinship ties and state authority organized large-scale labor in this period. (AMSCO p. 132)

**Minhaji Siraj Juzjani** (c. 1193–c. 1260)
Persian historian whose chronicles record the reign of Sultan Raziyya. His account of her acknowledges she had "all the admirable attributes and qualities required of kings" while simultaneously undercutting her by noting that she "did not attain the destiny, in her creation, of being computed among men." A revealing example of how medieval patriarchal societies processed exceptional women in power. (AMSCO p. 140)

## Key Historical Figures

**Sultan Raziyya bint Iltutmish** (r. 1236–1240)
The first woman to rule the Delhi Sultanate in northern India — and one of the very few female rulers in the medieval Islamic world. Recognized for her intelligence, justice, and military skill, she nonetheless faced persistent resistance because of her gender and was eventually deposed. Her reign is a compelling DBQ subject: evidence that patriarchal norms could be temporarily disrupted, but rarely permanently overturned. (AMSCO p. 140)

**Tomoe Gozen** (c. 1157–1247)
Japanese female samurai (*onna-bugeisha*) renowned for her bravery, archery, and horsemanship during the Genpei War. One of the rare documented female warriors in Japanese history. Represents the limited but real spaces that specific political and military crises opened for women to operate outside traditional gender roles.

**Genghis Khan** (r. 1206–1227)
Founder of the Mongol Empire. Topic 2.7 includes a Historical Perspectives reading asking: was Genghis Khan a brutal destroyer or a great empire builder? See [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Mongol Empire Overview]] and [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]] for full treatment. For 2.7, the relevance is his role in creating the [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]] that supercharged [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads\|Silk Roads]] trade. (AMSCO p. 134)

## Key States and Polities

**Malacca** (founded c. 1400)
Muslim sultanate controlling the Strait of Malacca — the narrow waterway connecting the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea. Malacca levied fees on all passing ships and used the revenue to build a powerful navy. The clearest example of how trading cities grew into centralized polities through commercial wealth. (AMSCO p. 129)

**Mali Empire** (c. 1235–1468)
West African empire that dominated the trans-Saharan gold and salt trade. Controlled by a Muslim ruling class, including the famous Mansa Musa, while much of the population maintained older religious practices. Ibn Battuta's 1352 account of Mali is a primary source on this network. See [[Unit-2.4/[[Concept Notes/Trans-Saharan Trade\|Trans-Saharan Trade]] Routes Overview|Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview]].

**Vijayanagara Empire** (1336–1646)
Hindu empire in southern India. Domingo Paes's account of its massive labor mobilization illustrates how state power organized large-scale projects in a trading-zone context. (AMSCO p. 132)

**Delhi Sultanate** (1206–1526)
Islamic sultanate in northern India. Important as the political context for Sultan Raziyya's exceptional reign (1236–1240), and as evidence of how Islamic governance spread via Indian Ocean trade networks.

**Khmer Empire / Angkor** (802–1431)
Dominant power in mainland Southeast Asia. Zhou Daguan's 1296–97 account provides a rare Chinese-eye view of Cambodian society, including women's dominance of local markets.
