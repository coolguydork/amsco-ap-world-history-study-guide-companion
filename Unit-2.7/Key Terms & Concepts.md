# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 2.7

> [!exam-tip]
> Many of these terms appear across multiple Unit 2 topics. For 2.7, the key skill is using them **comparatively** — being able to say which term applies to which network, and why the differences matter.

---

**Interregional trade**
Long-distance commerce connecting different regions across political boundaries. The three major interregional networks of c. 1200–1450 were the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]], the [[Concept Notes/Indian Ocean Trade Network|Indian Ocean Trade Network]], and the [[Concept Notes/Trans-Saharan Trade|Trans-Saharan Trade]] routes. (AMSCO p. 127)

**Trading city (entrepôt)**
A city that served as a commercial hub — a "knot" in the trade network where merchants exchanged goods, paid fees, and rested. All three networks generated trading cities. Examples: Samarkand and Chang'an (Silk Roads); Malacca, Calicut, and Hormuz (Indian Ocean); Timbuktu, Gao, and Cairo (Trans-Saharan). (AMSCO p. 128)

**Calicut**
Port city on the southwest coast of India, known as the "City of Spices." One of the Indian Ocean's premier entrepôts, where merchants traded pepper, cinnamon, and goods from across Asia. Featured prominently in Abd al-Razzaq's 1442 account. (AMSCO p. 127)

**Caravanserai**
Rest stops built at regular intervals along the Silk Roads — essentially roadside inns that provided shelter, food, water, and animal fodder for overland merchant caravans. Critical infrastructure that made long-distance overland travel possible. (AMSCO p. 130)

**Monsoon winds**
Seasonal winds in the Indian Ocean that blow northeast in winter and southwest in summer. Merchants sailing with the monsoons could reliably travel between Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and East Africa and back — the predictable "engine" of Indian Ocean trade. See [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Overview|Indian Ocean Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 93)

**Dhow**
A lateen-rigged sailing vessel used by Arab and Indian Ocean merchants, especially on routes between Arabia, East Africa, and South Asia. Well-suited for sailing with the monsoon winds.

**Junk**
A Chinese flat-bottomed sailing vessel with battened sails, used extensively in the South China Sea and Indian Ocean by Chinese merchants and, most famously, by Zheng He's treasure fleets. Larger than dhows and capable of carrying heavy cargo.

**Lateen sail**
A triangular sail that allowed ships to sail into the wind rather than only with it, dramatically expanding navigational flexibility on the Indian Ocean. One of the key maritime technologies that made the Indian Ocean network thrive. (AMSCO p. 130)

**Magnetic compass**
Navigation instrument that used Earth's magnetic field to determine direction. Invented in China and adopted by Indian Ocean merchants, the compass extended the range and reliability of long-distance sea voyages. (AMSCO p. 128)

**Astrolabe**
Instrument used to measure the altitude of stars and the sun, allowing sailors to determine their latitude at sea. A key technology behind Indian Ocean navigational accuracy.

**Stern rudder**
A rudder mounted at the rear of a ship, allowing much finer steering control than earlier side-mounted oars. A Chinese invention that spread across Indian Ocean trade and improved maritime navigation. (AMSCO p. 130)

**Flying cash (fei-qian)**
An early Chinese financial instrument — essentially a promissory note or letter of credit — that allowed merchants to transfer value without carrying heavy coin. A precursor to paper money and modern banking. Invented to reduce the risk of transporting coins over long distances. (AMSCO p. 131)

**Proto-industrialization**
The large-scale expansion of craft production — especially iron, steel, and porcelain in Song China — to meet the growing demands of long-distance trade. Not yet mechanized industry, but a significant step toward mass production and specialization. (AMSCO p. 131)

**Cowrie shells**
Small shells used as currency across West Africa and parts of South and Southeast Asia. Durable, counterfeit-resistant, and easily portable. Their use illustrates how different regions developed distinct commercial practices within their trade networks.

**Centralization**
The political process by which trading cities accumulated wealth from commerce, then used that wealth to build navies, armies, and administrative structures — concentrating power in fewer hands. Malacca is the clearest example from this period. (AMSCO p. 129)

**[[Concept Notes/Black Death|Black Death]]**
The pandemic caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*, transmitted by fleas on rats and spread rapidly along all three trade networks in the mid-14th century. Killed an estimated one-third or more of Europe's population, tens of millions in China, and devastated trading cities across Afro-Eurasia. A dramatic illustration of how trade connectivity could spread catastrophe. (AMSCO p. 132)

**[[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]]**
"Mongol Peace" — the relative political stability and security for merchants across the Mongol Empire (c. 1206–1368) that allowed overland Silk Roads trade to reach its peak. Under the Mongols, a merchant could travel from China to Eastern Europe with relative safety. Collapsed when the Mongol Empire fragmented. See [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Mongol Empire Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 134)

**Cottage industry**
Small-scale production of goods (textiles, ceramics, metalwork) by craftworkers in their own homes, typically organized by a merchant who supplied raw materials and bought finished goods. A widespread labor form across all three networks.

**Patriarchy**
A social system in which men hold primary power in political, economic, and social institutions. The typical social structure across most regions connected by the trade networks c. 1200–1450, though trade created limited exceptional spaces for women's participation.

**Proselytizing**
Actively seeking to convert others to a religion. Missionaries and merchants traveled the trade routes and spread [[Concept Notes/Buddhism|Buddhism]], [[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]]-infused Islam, and other faiths. Trade and religious conversion were deeply intertwined. See [[Unit-2.5/Cultural Consequences Overview|Cultural Consequences Overview]].
