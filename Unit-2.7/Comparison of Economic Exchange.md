# Topic 2.7 — Comparison of Economic Exchange

> [!summary] Essential Question
> What were the **similarities and differences** among the various networks of exchange in the period from c. 1200 to c. 1450?

## Why This Topic Matters

Topic 2.7 steps back from the individual networks and asks you to think about all three together. This is exactly what the AP exam's comparison questions and LEQs demand: not just knowing *what* each network did, but understanding *how they resembled each other*, *where they differed*, and *what those similarities and differences reveal* about this period of world history.

> [!exam-tip]
> Comparison is one of the four AP Historical Thinking Skills (along with Causation, Continuity and Change, and Contextualization). LEQ prompts about Unit 2 often ask you to compare two of the three trade networks. Mastering Topic 2.7 directly prepares you for those prompts.

---

## The Three Networks

A quick orientation:

1. **The Silk Roads** — overland network through the Gobi Desert and mountain passes; China to Southwest Asia and Europe; specialized in luxury goods (silk, porcelain, spices, paper, gunpowder)

2. **The Indian Ocean network** — maritime network linking East Asia, Southeast Asia, South Asia, Southwest Asia, and East Africa; monsoon-dependent; moved goods too heavy for overland transport (porcelain, cotton, gold, iron)

3. **Trans-Saharan routes** — overland network from North Africa and the Mediterranean Basin across the Sahara to West and East Africa; camel caravan-based; specialized in salt (north to south) and gold/ivory/slaves (south to north)

---

## Similarities Among the Three Networks

### 1. Origins in Ancient Trade

All three networks had roots long before 1200. They built on routes first traced by early traders and conquerors:

- The Silk Roads existed since at least 200 BCE
- Indian Ocean trade predated Islam (active by c. 200 BCE)
- Trans-Saharan routes developed as camels spread to North Africa (~300 CE)

The **boom in c. 1200–c. 1450** was not the creation of new networks but the *intensification and stabilization* of existing ones.

### 2. Dependence on Political Stability

All three networks required stable states to function at their highest level. Merchants needed assurance that:
- Their goods would not be stolen
- Their contracts would be enforced
- Their routes would be protected

This is why **Calicut's** wealthy merchants could walk away from their cargoes with full confidence (as the 1442 observer Abdu Razzak noted). Political stability created commercial trust.

Each network had its patrons:
- Silk Roads: Mongol Empire (Pax Mongolica)
- Indian Ocean: Sultanate of Malacca, Gujarat, Swahili city-states
- Trans-Saharan: Mali Empire, Songhai

### 3. Generation of Trading Cities

The trade networks all created **"knot" cities** — points where goods exchanged hands, merchants rested, and cultures mixed:

| Network | Key Trading Cities |
|---------|-------------------|
| Silk Roads | Samarkand, Kashgar, Aleppo, Mosul |
| Indian Ocean | Calicut, Hormuz, Mombasa, Alexandria, Malacca |
| Trans-Saharan | Timbuktu, Gao, Cairo, Marrakesh |

> [!tip]
> Think of trade networks as a fabric — trading cities are the knots where the threads cross and strengthen. This metaphor (from AMSCO) is actually useful for understanding why cities grow where they do: at the intersections.

### 4. Centralization and State Power

As trading cities grew wealthy, they needed to protect that wealth. This drove **centralization** — the growth of organized government power to:
- Police trade routes and protect merchant shipping
- Standardize currency for faster transactions
- Administer customs (taxes on imported goods)

Malacca built a navy to prevent piracy. Mali taxed all goods entering West Africa. The Mongols built roads and established consistent laws.

### 5. Shared Overall Purpose

All three networks existed primarily for **economic exchange** — trading what one region produced for what it needed. But they also moved much more: diplomats, missionaries, scholars, pilgrims, and refugees all used the trade routes. The result was that the networks were cultural exchange systems as much as commercial ones.

## Differences Among the Three Networks

The networks differed in goods, transportation, technology, and the religions they spread:

### Routes and Transportation

| Network | Primary Transport | Environmental Adaptation |
|---------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| Silk Roads | Camels; horses in northern steppes | Caravanserai every ~100 miles for desert; mountain passes |
| Indian Ocean | Dhows and junks | Monsoon wind timing; lateen sails; astrolabe |
| Trans-Saharan | Camel caravans | Load-bearing saddles; oasis stops across 3.6M sq miles |

### Goods Exchanged

| Network | East/South Goods | West/North Goods |
|---------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Silk Roads | Silk, tea, porcelain, spices, dyes, paper, gunpowder, rice | Horses, horses, saddles, fruit, textiles, honey, domestic animals |
| Indian Ocean | East Africa: gold, ivory, skins; S. India: cotton, pepper, pearls; China: silk, porcelain | S.W. Asia: horses, citrus, dates, books |
| Trans-Saharan | Gold, ivory, cloth, slaves (south → north) | Salt, horses, books, textiles (north → south) |

### Religions Spread

| Network | Religion Diffused |
|---------|------------------|
| Silk Roads | Buddhism (S. Asia → East/SE Asia); Neo-Confucianism (China → Korea, Japan, Vietnam); Islam (SW Asia → South Asia) |
| Indian Ocean | Buddhism (S. Asia → East/SE Asia); Neo-Confucianism (China → Korea/Japan/Vietnam); Islam (S. Asia → SE Asia); Christianity (Mediterranean Basin) |
| Trans-Saharan | Islam (SW Asia and North Africa → Sub-Saharan Africa) |

### Currencies and Commercial Practices

Each network developed unique financial instruments:

- **East and Central Asia**: silk as currency; paper money; flying cash; banks
- **Southeast Asia**: tin ingots as currency standard
- **West Africa**: cowrie shells; cotton cloth; gold; glass beads; salt
- **Over time**: all regions shifted toward **metal coin-based money economies**

China invented flying cash and the precursors to banking (Topic 2.1). The shift from commodity currencies (silk, cowries) to metal coins and then to paper money is a longterm change that the Silk Roads accelerated.

## Social Implications of the Networks

### Proto-Industrialization

Rising demand for luxury goods forced **production to scale up**:

- China entered a period of **proto-industrialization** to meet demand for iron, steel, and porcelain
- As maritime trade volume began to *exceed* overland trade volume, larger ships and better navigation became necessary
- New business practices emerged: **partnerships for sharing investment risk**

### Labor

The demand for labor rose with the demand for goods. Forms of labor in this period included:
- Free peasant farmers
- Artisans and craft workers in cottage industries
- **Debt bondage** — people forced to work to pay off debts
- **Enslaved people** — trade in slaves was common on both the Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan routes

Large infrastructure projects (irrigation canals, military defenses, great buildings) required organized mass labor — sometimes tens of thousands of workers. Kinship networks often coordinated these projects.

### Gender and Social Structures

The social structures of 1200–1450 were still defined by class or caste, and most societies were patriarchies. However, some exceptions existed:

- **Mongol women**: somewhat more freedom than women elsewhere — moved freely, refused foot-binding and the burka, could remarry and divorce, often served as advisors to the Great Khan
- **European women**: worked as farmers and artisans; had their own guilds
- **Southeast Asian women**: skilled in marketplace practices; controlled markets as representatives of powerful families

These exceptions are important for comparative AP questions about gender — they show that patriarchy was not uniform, and that contact through trade networks could affect gender norms.

## Environmental Processes

The interconnections of trade created both economic vitality and steep population decline via disease. The plague contributed to the decline of once-great cities like Constantinople. Soil erosion from deforestation and overgrazing forced populations to migrate. Political instability and increased agriculture strained environments.

At the same time, cultural diffusion accelerated the development of educational centers: Canton, Samarkand, Timbuktu, Cairo, and Venice all became intellectual hubs tied to trade wealth.

---

## The Big Synthesis

What does comparing all three networks reveal?

1. **Trade is transformative beyond economics**: Every network spread religions, languages, technologies, and diseases far beyond the goods they were designed to carry.

2. **Political stability is prerequisite**: No network flourished without protective states. The Pax Mongolica, the Mali Empire, and Malacca's navy all demonstrate that commerce requires governance.

3. **The world was more connected than we often assume**: A giraffe from East Africa living in a Chinese imperial zoo (brought by Zheng He). Cowrie shells from the Indian Ocean used as currency in Mali. Champa rice from Vietnam feeding Chinese cities. The connections were real, dense, and consequential.

4. **Environmental consequences were real and lasting**: Crop diffusion fed populations. Disease devastated them. Trade-driven environmental change is not a modern phenomenon.
