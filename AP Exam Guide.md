## The Six Historical Thinking Skills

Every exam question requires one or more of these skills:

1. **Identify and explain historical developments and processes** — describe characteristics and explain how/why something emerged using specific evidence.
2. **Analyze sourcing and situation of primary and secondary sources** — identify point of view, purpose, historical situation, and audience; assess significance and limitations.
3. **Analyze claims and evidence in primary and secondary sources** — identify what a source argues, the evidence it uses, and compare arguments across sources.
4. **Analyze the context of historical events, developments, or processes** — explain how a development fits within its broader historical context.
5. **Analyze patterns and connections using historical reasoning** — identify patterns across developments and explain how one development relates to another.
6. **Develop an argument** — make a defensible claim, support it with evidence, use historical reasoning, and corroborate/qualify/modify the argument.

---

## The Three Historical Reasoning Processes

| Process | What It Requires |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Comparison** | Describe, compare, contrast, and evaluate two or more historical events or developments — same or different eras, same or different locations. Evaluate from multiple perspectives. |
| **Causation** | Identify, analyze, and evaluate relationships between events as causes and effects. Distinguish primary from secondary causes. Be careful: correlation ≠ causation. |
| **Continuity and Change over Time (CCOT)** | Recognize, analyze, and evaluate the dynamics of history over periods of time. Identify important patterns. Thematic analysis is the key tool. |

## Section I-A: Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)

- 55 questions in 55 minutes — about 1 minute per question.
- Questions come in sets of 2–4, all tied to a shared stimulus (primary source, secondary source, map, image, chart).
- **No penalty for guessing** — always answer every question.
- Read the question *before* reading the stimulus passage or image.
- Eliminate answers with absolute language ("always," "never") — history rarely supports such certainty.
- If a question is hard, make your best guess and move on. Return if time allows.

> [!exam-tip]
> The MCQ tests your ability to apply historical reasoning skills to unfamiliar sources — not just to recall facts. Practice reading stimuli critically: What's the argument? What's the context? What's the author's purpose?

---

## Section I-B: Short-Answer Questions (SAQs)

- 4 questions presented; you answer 3 in 40 minutes.
- **No thesis required.** Each question has three parts (A, B, C).
- SAQ 1 (required): secondary source stimulus; topics from 1200–2001.
- SAQ 2 (required): primary source stimulus; topics from 1200–2001.
- SAQ 3 or 4 (choose one, no stimulus): SAQ 3 covers 1200–1750; SAQ 4 covers 1750–2001.

Write concisely and directly. Each part typically earns 1 point for a specific, historically accurate response with supporting evidence.

## Section II: Document-Based Question (DBQ)

**Structure:** 7 documents + 1 prompt. 60 minutes total (15-minute reading period + 45 minutes writing). Topics from 1450–2001.

**Scoring (7 points possible):**

| Category | Points | What's Required |
|----------|--------|----------------|
| **A. Thesis/Claim** | 0–1 | Historically defensible thesis that establishes a line of reasoning; must be at least one sentence in the intro or conclusion. Cannot simply restate the prompt. |
| **B. Contextualization** | 0–1 | Describe broader historical context *before, during, or after* the time frame. Must be more than a phrase — requires a developed explanation. |
| **C. Evidence from Documents** | 0–2 | 1 pt: accurately describe content of 3 documents. 2 pts: accurately describe 6 documents AND use them to support arguments. |
| **C. Evidence Beyond Documents** | 0–1 | At least one specific piece of outside evidence relevant to the argument (not used for contextualization). |
| **D. Sourcing (HAPP)** | 0–1 | For at least 3 documents, explain how/why point of view, purpose, historical situation, or audience is relevant to the argument. Must explain *significance*, not just identify. |
| **D. Complexity** | 0–1 | Demonstrate complex understanding: analyze multiple causes, similarities AND differences, continuity AND change, or corroborate/qualify/modify the argument. |

> [!exam-tip]
> Don't let the document order control your essay structure. Group documents by how they support your thesis. Aim to use all 7 — but using 6 well is enough for full evidence points.

> [!warning]
> The most common DBQ mistake: writing a descriptive list of documents instead of an analytical argument. The documents are evidence; your thesis is the argument. Integrate, don't narrate.

**DBQ Writing Tips:**
- Use the 15-minute reading period to annotate documents and sketch a thesis.
- Cite documents as (Doc. 1), (Doc. 2), etc. — readers know the content; don't quote at length.
- Address contradictory evidence — a strong thesis accounts for complexity.
- HAPP sourcing requires more than "the author is biased" — explain *why* the source's POV/purpose/situation matters to *your argument*.

## Section II: Long Essay Question (LEQ)

**Structure:** Choose 1 of 3 prompts in 40 minutes. All three options test the same reasoning process; time periods differ (1200–1750 / 1450–1900 / 1750–2001).

**Scoring (6 points possible):**

| Category | Points | What's Required |
|----------|--------|----------------|
| **A. Thesis/Claim** | 0–1 | Defensible thesis that establishes a line of reasoning; intro or conclusion; not a restatement. |
| **B. Contextualization** | 0–1 | Broader historical context beyond the prompt's time frame. More than a phrase. |
| **C. Evidence** | 0–2 | 1 pt: identify specific relevant historical examples. 2 pts: use specific examples to *support* the argument. |
| **D. Analysis and Reasoning** | 0–2 | 1 pt: use historical reasoning (causation, comparison, or CCOT) to frame arguments. 2 pts: demonstrate complex understanding — multiple causes, similarities/differences, continuity/change, or corroborate/qualify/modify. |

> [!exam-tip]
> Before writing, spend 3–5 minutes outlining. Identify: (1) the topic, (2) the targeted reasoning process, (3) your thesis, (4) 3–4 pieces of evidence and how each links to the thesis.

## Essay Writing Fundamentals

### The Five Steps

1. **Analyze the question** — Underline key verbs (evaluate, explain, compare). Identify the topic AND the reasoning process. An essay that ignores part of the prompt earns a lower score no matter how well-written.

2. **Organize the evidence** — Spend 5 minutes on a brief outline or graphic organizer before writing. Know your evidence before committing to a thesis.

3. **State your thesis** — A thesis takes a *position*. It must be evaluative, not descriptive. It must address all parts of the question. Readers are not looking for a "right answer" — they are looking for a defensible argument supported by evidence.

4. **Write supporting paragraphs** — Each paragraph develops one main point, stated clearly in a topic sentence. Every paragraph must link evidence back to the thesis. Avoid "laundry lists" of facts — analyze, don't narrate.

5. **Evaluate and conclude** — Restate the thesis in fresh language. Answer "So what?" — explain why the historical development matters in a broader context. Keep the conclusion short if you're running out of time.

### Key Verbs on the Exam

| Verb | What It Demands |
|------|----------------|
| **Compare** | Similarities and/or differences |
| **Describe** | Relevant characteristics of a topic |
| **Evaluate** | Judge significance, importance, or accuracy |
| **Explain** | How or why something occurred, using evidence and reasoning |
| **Identify** | Indicate information without elaboration |
| **Support an argument** | Provide examples and show how they support a claim |

### General Writing Rules

- Write in the **third person** — avoid "I" and "we."
- Write in the **past tense** (except when describing sources that currently exist: "the document implies").
- Use the **active voice**: "The Mongols conquered China" beats "China was conquered."
- **Be precise**: use specific names, dates, and places. Avoid vague pronouns ("they," "others") unless the referent is crystal clear.
- **Anticipate counterarguments**: the strongest essays confront contradictory evidence and explain why it doesn't undermine the thesis.
- **Don't editorialize**: avoid "good guys/bad guys" framing. Stay analytical.

## Quick-Reference: Earning Complexity

Complexity (the hardest point to earn on both DBQ and LEQ) requires demonstrating a nuanced understanding. Ways to earn it:

- Analyze **more than one cause** for a historical development
- Discuss both **similarities AND differences** in a comparison
- Address both **continuity AND change** in a CCOT argument
- **Corroborate** your argument with evidence from a different region, time period, or theme
- **Qualify or modify** your thesis by acknowledging limits or exceptions to your argument

> [!warning]
> Complexity is not earned by adding a "however" paragraph at the end. It must be integrated into the argument and connected to the thesis.
