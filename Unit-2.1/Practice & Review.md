# Practice & Review — Topic 2.1: The Silk Roads

---

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 82–83 (printed)*

**Questions 1–3** refer to the following passage:

> "The road you travel from Tana to Cathay [China] is perfectly safe, whether by day or by night, according to what the merchants say who have used it. . . . Cathay is a province which contained a multitude of cities and towns. Among others there is one in particular, that is to say the capital city, to which is great resort of merchants, and in which there is a vast amount of trade; and this city is called Cambalec. And the said city hath a circuit of one hundred miles, and is all full of people and houses and of dwellers in the said city."
>
> — Francesco Pegolotti, *The Merchant's Handbook*, c. 1471

---

**Question 1.** Which statement about China and trade does this passage best support?

(A) Trade between Europe and Cathay was greatly enhanced by the security established by the Mongol and Ming Empires.
(B) Merchants rarely traveled between Tana and Cathay because the road was very dangerous.
(C) Cambalec was an important trading hub, but it was not a large city.
(D) The author felt the risks of trade with China outweighed the rewards.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage explicitly states the road is "perfectly safe" (reflecting the Pax Mongolica) and describes Cambalec (Khanbaliq/Beijing) as a city of "vast amount of trade" with a circuit of "one hundred miles." This supports the idea of security-enhanced commerce. B contradicts the source directly. C contradicts the description of a huge city. D contradicts the enthusiastic, encouraging tone of the passage.

---

**Question 2.** Which development was the turning point that led to the expansion of trade between Asia and Europe?

(A) The formation of the Silk Roads by Marco Polo allowed goods from Asia to be imported into Europe for the first time.
(B) The conquests by Mongols, which led to improved trade routes and reduced trading costs.
(C) The invention of silk cloth during the early Mongol rule allowed the Mongols to trade for pottery, wool, and other European goods.
(D) The invention of paper money by Venetian bankers helped to facilitate trade between Europe and Asia.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Mongol conquests unified the Silk Roads corridor under a single authority, improved road infrastructure, suppressed banditry (the Pax Mongolica), and dramatically lowered the risks and costs of overland trade. A is wrong — Marco Polo did not create the Silk Roads, and trade predated his journey by over a millennium. C is wrong — silk was invented centuries before Mongol rule. D is wrong — paper money was a Chinese invention, not Venetian.

---

**Question 3.** Which objects or ideas was the author most likely writing about that represented continuity or change in Chinese trade?

(A) Silver, gems, and gold went from Europe to China; wool, horses, and the ideas of Islam went from China to Europe
(B) Silk, paper, and Christianity went from Europe to China; gunpowder, silver, and Buddhism went from China to Europe
(C) Silver, gems, and gold went from Europe to China; silk, paper, and gunpowder went from China to Europe
(D) Paper and Christianity went from Europe to China; silk, gunpowder, horses, and wool went from China to Europe

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Historically, Europe exported precious metals (silver, gold) to China in exchange for luxury goods, while China exported silk, paper, and technologies like gunpowder westward. A is wrong — Islam spread *into* China, not out from it, and wool was not a major Chinese export. B is wrong — Christianity did not flow from Europe to China in this period. D is wrong — paper and Christianity were not major European exports to China.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO p. 83 (printed)*

**Question 1.** Use the passage below to answer all parts.

> "Throughout the city there are fine roadways with open spaces well laid out: and round these are seen many great buildings and houses, each with its main doorway facing the square. Such are the caravanserais [inns]: and within are constructed separate apartments and shops with offices that are planned for various uses. Leaving these caravanserais you pass into the market streets where goods of all kinds are sold: such as silk stuffs and cotton cloths, crapes [crepes, a type of silk or wool fabric], taffetas [a fine silk fabric], raw silk and jewelry: for in these shops wares of every kind may be found. There is indeed an immense concourse of merchants and merchandise here . . . . Now the dress the women wear in the streets is that they go covered in a white sheet, and they wear over their faces a black mask of horse-hair, and thus they are concealed completely so that none may know them. Throughout Tabriz [a city in what is now northwestern Iran] many fine buildings may be seen, the Mosques more especially these being most beautifully adorned with tiles in blue and gold. . . ."
>
> — Ruy González de Clavijo, account of Silk Roads travels, 1402–1406

**(A)** Identify ONE aspect of social structure in Tabriz that demonstrated continuity with earlier periods in history.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity
> The passage describes women wearing veils and coverings in public — a practice rooted in Islamic social norms that had characterized Islamic societies in the region for centuries. This gendered social structure represented continuity with earlier Islamic civilizations in the Middle East and Central Asia.

**(B)** Explain Clavijo's attitude toward the society and culture at Tabriz.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation/Analysis
> Clavijo's account is largely positive and admiring. He praises the city's organized layout ("fine roadways with open spaces"), the beauty of the mosques ("adorned with tiles in blue and gold"), and the volume and variety of commerce ("immense concourse of merchants and merchandise"). While he notes the women's veiling as different from European customs, he describes it descriptively rather than condemningly. Overall, Clavijo presents Tabriz as a sophisticated, wealthy, and well-organized city — evidence of his respect for its commercial and architectural achievement.

**(C)** Explain how caravanserais affected interregional trade in luxury goods during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Caravanserais were a critical piece of infrastructure that enabled sustained long-distance trade across inhospitable terrain. By providing shelter, water, food, and trading opportunities at regular intervals (roughly every 100 miles — one day's camel travel), they reduced the physical hardships and risks of overland travel. This lowered the cost and danger of transporting luxury goods across Central Asia, making trade more reliable and profitable. The caravanserais also became commercial hubs in their own right, where merchants from different regions could exchange goods directly, further expanding the reach and volume of interregional trade.

**Question 2.** Answer all parts.

**(A)** Identify ONE example of cultures spreading between the East and West.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Islam spread westward along Silk Roads trade corridors into Central Asia, reaching cities like Kashgar and Samarkand, which became centers of Islamic scholarship despite their origins as primarily Buddhist or Zoroastrian communities.

**(B)** Explain ONE example of commercial practices that affected Silk Roads trade during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The bill of exchange, originally a Chinese financial instrument, spread westward along trade routes and was adopted by European banking houses in the 1300s. By allowing merchants to transfer large sums of money across distances without physically transporting heavy coins, the bill of exchange reduced the costs and risks of long-distance commerce, enabling merchants to conduct larger transactions and expanding the scale of Silk Roads trade.

**(C)** Explain ONE example of money economies that affected Silk Roads trade during the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> China's development of paper money (beginning c. 800 CE) and the flying cash credit system allowed merchants to deposit paper currency in one city and withdraw the equivalent elsewhere, eliminating the need to carry bulky copper coins across dangerous long-distance routes. This made it practical to conduct the high-volume trade that characterized the Pax Mongolica era, directly enabling the expansion of Silk Roads commerce.

## Historical Reasoning Practice

*From Think As a Historian, AMSCO p. 84*

**CONTEXTUALIZATION — Identify the bigger picture** by naming a cause for each Song Dynasty development below:

1. China developed new financial systems in response to increasing trade with Arab merchants from the Abbasid Empire.
   - **Context:** The Abbasid revival of the Silk Roads in the 8th–9th centuries created high-volume trade that overwhelmed copper-coin economies, forcing Chinese financial innovation.

2. Russia was able to exchange goods and services with cultures farther west.
   - **Context:** Mongol conquest unified Eurasian trade routes under the Pax Mongolica, connecting Russia's Golden Horde to the broader Silk Roads network.

3. The demand for exported silks and Chinese porcelain in Europe and India grew as the Indian Ocean trade grew.
   - **Context:** The Crusades awakened European desire for Asian luxury goods; simultaneously, Indian Ocean expansion linked South and Southeast Asia as new consumer markets.

4. Thriving cities developed in Asia.
   - **Context:** The stability and infrastructure of the Silk Roads (caravanserais, Mongol road improvements) drew merchants and populations to key waypoints, transforming oasis towns into major commercial cities.

5. Guns and gunpowder spread from China to all parts of Eurasia.
   - **Context:** The Silk Roads were a diffusion corridor for technology; the same routes that carried silk and spices also carried Chinese military innovations westward into the Islamic world and eventually Europe.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

*In one to three paragraphs, explain the causes and effects of the growth of networks of exchange after 1200.*

> [!tip] Model Response — Synthesis
> The growth of networks of exchange after 1200 was driven by three interconnected causes: political unification, technological improvement, and expanding demand. The most powerful cause was the rise of the Mongol Empire, which by conquering the Abbasid Caliphate in 1258 and eventually China, unified the Silk Roads corridor under a single authority. The resulting Pax Mongolica made overland travel safer than it had been in centuries, dramatically reducing the risk and cost of long-distance trade. Improvements in transportation — better camel saddles, the Chinese junk, the magnetic compass — and infrastructure — caravanserai networks, improved roads — further lowered the barriers to exchange. Finally, demand: the Crusades had introduced European elites to Eastern luxury goods, and their appetite for silk, spices, and porcelain drove a sustained expansion of production and export from China, Persia, and India.
>
> The effects of this expansion were equally significant. New trading cities like Kashgar and Samarkand flourished at the intersection of routes and rivers, becoming cosmopolitan centers of commerce and Islamic scholarship. Commercial innovations — paper money, flying cash, bills of exchange, and banking houses — spread westward from China, providing the financial infrastructure that would eventually seed European capitalism. Cultural diffusion accelerated: Islam spread into Central Asia; Chinese technologies including papermaking, gunpowder, and the compass reached the Islamic world and Europe. Most catastrophically, the same routes that carried silk carried plague — the Black Death spread from Central Asia westward along trade corridors beginning in 1347, killing an estimated one-third of Europe's population and demonstrating that connectivity had profound costs as well as benefits.
