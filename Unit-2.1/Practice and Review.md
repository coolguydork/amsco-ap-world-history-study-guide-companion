# Topic 2.1 — Practice and Review

## Multiple Choice Practice

**Question 1**

Which of the following best explains why trade on the Silk Roads increased significantly after 1200?

A) The Crusades permanently destroyed the Byzantine Empire, opening new trade routes  
B) The Mongol Empire unified the overland routes under one political authority, improving safety  
C) China's Song Dynasty collapsed, forcing merchants to find new trading partners  
D) European demand for luxury goods declined, allowing Asian merchants to lower prices

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Mongol Empire's unification of the Silk Roads — enforcing consistent laws, punishing bandits, and improving roads — is the central cause of the third golden age of Silk Roads trade. Choice A is incorrect because the Crusades *stimulated* European interest but didn't destroy Byzantine trade. C is wrong because the Song Dynasty was actually thriving. D is backward — European demand *increased*.

**Question 2**

The Hanseatic League of the 13th–17th centuries is best understood as an example of:

A) A religious alliance formed to protect Christian merchants from Muslim competition  
B) A political confederation of states seeking to conquer new trade territories  
C) A commercial alliance of cities that used collective power to protect and monopolize regional trade  
D) A banking system that facilitated the exchange of bills of exchange across Europe

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The Hanseatic League was a merchant guild confederation — cities working together to eliminate pirates and control access to North Sea and Baltic trade. It wasn't religious (A), wasn't military conquest (B), and wasn't a banking system (D), though it operated alongside banking innovations.

**Question 3**

"Flying cash" was significant in the history of trade because it:

A) Allowed merchants to avoid paying taxes to local governments  
B) Created a standardized currency accepted across the entire Silk Roads network  
C) Reduced the physical risks and logistical burden of carrying large quantities of coins over long distances  
D) Was adopted immediately by European merchants and replaced the barter system

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Flying cash solved a practical problem: copper coins were heavy and dangerous to transport. By allowing deposits and withdrawals at different locations, it enabled long-distance trade with much less risk. It was *not* a universal currency (A/B), and it did *not* spread immediately to Europe (D).

## Short Answer Question (SAQ) Practice

**SAQ Prompt:**

Use the following excerpt and your knowledge of world history to answer all parts.

> *"And don't forget that if you treat the custom-house officers with respect, and make them something of a present in goods or money, as well as their clerks and dragomen, they will behave with great civility, and always be ready to appraise your wares below their real value."*
> — Italian merchant Francesco Balducci Pegolotti, c. 1340

**Part A**: Briefly explain ONE piece of historical context that would help explain why an Italian merchant was writing a practical guide to traveling the Silk Roads in the 14th century.

**Part B**: Briefly explain ONE cause of the increased volume of trade on the Silk Roads in the period c. 1200–c. 1450.

**Part C**: Briefly explain ONE effect of the growth of exchange networks on either cities or commercial practices in the period c. 1200–c. 1450.

> [!tip] Model Responses
> **Part A**: The Pax Mongolica (c. 1250–c. 1350) created a period of relative stability across Eurasia after the Mongol conquests. Under the Mongols, the routes from China to Persia were protected by a single military force, making it practical — and profitable — for Italian merchants from cities like Venice and Genoa to travel or send agents as far as Central Asia and China. Pegolotti's guide reflects the real possibility of such travel.
>
> **Part B**: The Mongol Empire unified the overland Silk Roads under a single political authority, punishing bandits and improving roads. For the first time since the Roman and Han periods, merchants could travel the full length of the route with a reasonable expectation of safety, dramatically increasing trade volume.
>
> **Part C**: As trade increased, thriving oasis cities like Kashgar and Samarkand grew into major commercial and cultural centers — not just rest stops, but hubs of Islamic scholarship, artisan production, and cultural exchange. Alternatively: commercial innovations like flying cash and bills of exchange emerged to manage the increasing scale of trade, eventually inspiring the banking houses of 14th-century European cities.

## LEQ/DBQ Thinking: Causation Prompt

**Prompt:** Evaluate the most important cause of the expansion of Silk Roads trade between c. 1200 and c. 1450.

**Thesis frame**: The most important cause was [X] because [reason that addresses magnitude, scope, or mechanism better than alternatives].

**Strong thesis example**: "Although growing European demand for luxury goods and improvements in transportation technology both contributed to the expansion of Silk Roads trade between 1200 and 1450, the establishment of the Mongol Empire was the most important cause because it unified disparate stretches of the route under a single political authority, fundamentally transforming the *safety and scale* of overland commerce in ways that no market demand alone could achieve."

**Evidence to use**:
- Mongol punishing of bandits and road improvement
- The Pax Mongolica (c. 1250–c. 1350)
- Commercial innovations (flying cash, bills of exchange) as *responses* to increased trade volume
- Thriving trade cities (Kashgar, Samarkand) flourishing under Mongol protection

**Counterargument to address**: Technology (camel saddles, junks) was necessary but not sufficient — camels existed before 1200; it was political stability that unlocked their potential at scale.

## CCOT Prompt

**Prompt:** Describe ONE continuity and ONE change in Silk Roads trade between c. 200 BCE and c. 1450 CE.

**Continuity**: The *types* of goods traded (silk, spices, precious metals) and the geographic routes themselves remained largely constant across 1,500+ years of Silk Roads history.

**Change**: The *political organization* of the routes changed dramatically. Before the Mongols, different empires controlled different segments, creating gaps in security and commercial infrastructure. The Mongol Empire (13th–14th centuries) unified the routes under a single authority, producing the safest and highest-volume period the Silk Roads had ever experienced.

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning]
> **Misconception 1**: Merchants traveled the full length of the Silk Roads from China to Rome. In reality, goods passed through many hands — a product might be traded five or six times before reaching its final destination. Most merchants were regional specialists.

> [!warning]
> **Misconception 2**: The "Silk Roads" was a single road. It was a network of routes, including both overland and maritime extensions.

> [!warning]
> **Misconception 3**: Paper money was invented in medieval Europe. Chinese innovation in paper currency predates European banking by centuries. Flying cash appeared c. 800 CE; European banking houses appeared in the 1300s, modeled partly on Chinese concepts transmitted via Silk Roads contact.
