# Topic 2.1 — The Silk Roads

**Unit 2: Networks of Exchange, c. 1200–c. 1450**
**AP Themes:** Economic Systems (ECN) · Technology and Innovation (TEC) · Cultural Developments and Interactions (CDI)
**Learning Objective A:** Explain the causes and effects of the growth of networks of exchange after 1200.

---

## The Big Picture

The [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads\|Silk Roads]] were the most important overland trade network in Afro-Eurasia, connecting China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and eventually Europe. After a period of decline following the fall of the Han and Roman empires, they experienced a dramatic revival — first under the Abbasid Caliphate and Tang China in the 8th–9th centuries, then reaching their second golden age under the Pax Mongolica in the 13th–14th centuries. By 1200, the Silk Roads were not just a conduit for goods but a highway for ideas, religions, technologies, and — tragically — disease.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about **causes** of expanded exchange (new empires, technology, demand for luxury goods) and **effects** (trade cities, commercial innovations, cultural diffusion, plague). Be ready to use Silk Roads evidence in Causation and CCOT essays.

---

## Causes of the Growth of Exchange Networks after 1200

### 1. Rise of Powerful Empires

The single most important cause was the rise of the **[[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongol Empire]]**. After conquering the Abbasid Caliphate in 1258 and eventually absorbing China under the Yuan Dynasty, the Mongols unified vast stretches of the Silk Roads under a single authority for the first time. They improved roads, punished bandits, and respected merchants. This era of stability is known as the **[[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]]** — the "Mongol Peace" — and it made long-distance overland travel safer than it had been in centuries. (AMSCO p. 78)

Before the Mongols, Arab merchants of the **Abbasid Empire** had already revived both the overland Silk Roads and Indian Ocean sea routes during the 8th–9th centuries, creating the second golden age. Tang and Song China contributed critical exports — porcelain, silk, tea — and technologies including the compass, paper, and gunpowder. (AMSCO p. 77–78)

The **[[Concept Notes/Crusades|Crusades]]** also played a role by awakening European demand for Eastern luxury goods, driving lords and merchants to seek access to Asian markets. (AMSCO p. 80)

### 2. Improvements in Transportation Technology

- **Caravans** — traveling in groups increased safety and allowed heavier loads
- **Camel saddle design** — improved saddle technology greatly increased load capacity
- **Magnetic compass and rudder** — Chinese naval innovations (developed in the Han era) enabled more reliable sea travel along the South China Sea
- **Chinese junk** — up to 400 feet long, with multiple sails and a compartmentalized hull for added strength, far larger than contemporary European ships (AMSCO p. 78)

### 3. Growing Demand for Luxury Goods

Demand from Europe, Africa, and the Islamic world for Chinese silk, porcelain, and tea drove expanded production. Artisans across China, Persia, and India scaled up output for export markets. China also expanded its iron and steel production, a sign of proto-industrialization. Europe supplied gold and silver in exchange. (AMSCO p. 81)

## Effects of the Growth of Exchange Networks

### Trading Cities and Oases

Long overland routes through arid Central Asia required reliable stopping points. Two types emerged:

**Caravanserais** — inns spaced approximately 100 miles apart (roughly one day's camel travel), offering rest for merchants and animals, water, food, and trading opportunities. The word comes from Persian words for "caravan" and "palace." (AMSCO p. 79)

**Thriving trade cities** grew at river crossings and route junctions:
- **Kashgar** (western China) — at the junction of northern and southern Silk Roads branches, watered by the Kashgar River, known for textiles, rugs, leather goods, and Islamic scholarship. (AMSCO p. 78–79)
- **Samarkand** (present-day Uzbekistan) — in the Zeravshan River valley, a crossroads of [[Concept Notes/Buddhism\|Buddhism]], Christianity, Zoroastrianism, and Islam; famous for its artisans and Islamic learning. (AMSCO p. 79)

### Commercial Innovations

The explosion of trade volume required new financial instruments. China led the way:

| Innovation | Description | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **Flying cash** | Paper credit system: deposit money in one location, withdraw it elsewhere | China, c. 700 C.E. |
| **Paper money** | Currency in paper form, replacing heavy copper coins | China, c. 800 C.E. |
| **Bill of exchange** | Written promise of payment on a set date; precursor to the check | China, c. 200 B.C.E. |
| **Banking houses** | Institutions facilitating bills of exchange; spread to European cities in the 1300s | China, spreading west |
| **Caravanserai** | Inn/trading post along routes, also a commercial hub | Persia, c. 500 B.C.E. |
| **Hanseatic League** | Commercial alliance of northern German/Scandinavian cities controlling North Sea and Baltic trade | Germany, 1296 C.E. |

(AMSCO pp. 79–81)

### Cultural and Biological Diffusion

The same routes that carried silk and spices also carried religions, ideas, and disease. Islam spread into Central Asia along Silk Road corridors. Technologies like papermaking and gunpowder diffused westward from China. Most catastrophically, the **bubonic plague** ([[Concept Notes/Black Death\|Black Death]], beginning c. 1347) spread from Central Asia along Silk Roads trade routes into Europe, killing an estimated one-third of Europe's population. (AMSCO p. 75; see also [[Unit-2.6/Environmental Consequences Overview|Unit 2.6]])
