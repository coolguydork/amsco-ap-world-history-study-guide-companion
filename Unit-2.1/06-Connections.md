## Concepts This Topic Sets Up (For Later Units)

- **Unit 3 — Age of Exploration:** The decline of the overland Silk Road after Mongol collapse → European search for direct maritime routes to Asia → Portuguese exploration of African coast → Vasco da Gama reaches India (1498). The Silk Roads' disruption is a **cause** of European expansion.
- **Unit 3 — Gunpowder Empires:** Chinese gunpowder traveled the Silk Roads westward → adopted by Islamic states and eventually European armies → the Military Revolution and gunpowder empires of the 16th century
- **Unit 3 — Columbian Exchange:** The logic of biological exchange established by the Silk Roads (disease, crops, animals moving through trade networks) prefigures the Columbian Exchange — the Americas just weren't connected to the network yet
- **Unit 4 — Atlantic World:** Paper, printing, and compass (all Silk Road diffusions from China) enabled the European intellectual and navigation revolutions that made Atlantic exploration possible

## Comparative Civilizations / Contrast Points

- **Silk Roads vs. Indian Ocean trade ([[../Unit-2.3/00-Overview|Unit 2.3: Exchange in the Indian Ocean]]):** Overland routes required political stability (Pax Mongolica); maritime routes were driven by natural forces (monsoons) and were less dependent on any single empire — this made maritime trade more resilient after Mongol collapse
- **Silk Roads vs. Trans-Saharan routes ([[../Unit-2.4/00-Overview|Unit 2.4: Trans-Saharan Trade Routes]]):** Both were vectors for Islamic spread and luxury goods exchange, but Trans-Saharan routes moved gold and salt rather than silk and porcelain; West African states like Mali were the western anchors of a parallel network
- **Marco Polo vs. Ibn Battuta:** Both traveled in the 13th–14th centuries along Silk Road routes; Marco Polo's account emphasized Chinese wealth and fired European commercial ambition; Ibn Battuta's account emphasized the unity of the Islamic world and the reach of Muslim merchant communities — different cultural lenses on the same network
- **Pax Mongolica vs. Roman Pax Romana:** Both represent imperial peace enabling trade; both ended with political collapse that disrupted commercial networks — a recurring historical pattern of empire-trade interdependence
