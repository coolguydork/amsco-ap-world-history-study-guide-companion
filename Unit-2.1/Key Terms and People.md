# Topic 2.1 — Key Terms and People

## Technology: Transportation & Navigation

**Magnetic compass** — Chinese invention (Han Dynasty) that allowed sailors to determine direction at sea; spread via the Silk Roads to the Islamic world and then to Europe, revolutionizing navigation. See [[Unit-1.1/Song Dynasty and East Asia]].

**Rudder (stern rudder)** — A moveable steering device attached to the rear of a ship; Chinese innovation that gave ships better maneuverability and stability in rough seas.

**Chinese junk** — Large Chinese sailing vessel, up to 400 feet long with multiple sails and a hull divided into watertight compartments. Triple the size of typical European ships. Dominated South China Sea trade.

**Camel saddle** — Various designs for different purposes; the Somali load-bearing saddle (up to 600 lbs) was most important for trade, transforming camels into reliable long-distance cargo animals.

## Economics: Trade Innovations

**Money economy** — An economic system based on currency (coins, paper) rather than barter. China had long been a money economy; this is what made paper money and credit systems possible.

**Flying cash** — Chinese credit system allowing a merchant to deposit paper money at one location and withdraw it at another. The practical ancestor of modern banking. Eliminated the need to carry heavy coins across long distances.

**Paper money** — Currency in paper form; developed in China around c. 800 CE. Described by Marco Polo to skeptical Europeans in the 13th century.

**Banking houses** — Financial institutions emerging in European cities in the 1300s, modeled on Chinese flying-cash offices. A person could present a **bill of exchange** and receive payment.

**Bill of exchange** — A written legal instrument stating that the holder would be paid a fixed amount on a set date. Enabled long-distance credit and reduced the risks of carrying coins across Eurasia.

**Hanseatic League** — A commercial alliance formed in 1296 CE by cities in northern Germany and Scandinavia (including Lübeck, Hamburg, and Riga). It controlled trade in the North Sea and Baltic Sea, expelled pirates, and monopolized trade in timber, grain, leather, and salted fish. One of history's first common markets.

**Caravanserai** — Persian word meaning "caravan palace." Inns built along the Silk Roads roughly every 100 miles — the distance a camel caravan could travel before needing water. Provided rest for merchants and animals, and became informal cultural exchange points.

## Government: New Empires

**Mongol Empire** — The largest contiguous land empire in history, established by [[Genghis Khan]] in the early 13th century. Its unification of the Silk Roads under a single authority created the third golden age of overland trade. See [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview]].

## Culture: Trade Cities

**Kashgar** — Oasis city at the western edge of China, at the junction of northern and southern Silk Roads branches. Watered by the Kashgar River. Known for textiles, rugs, leather goods, and pottery. Originally Buddhist; became a center of Islamic scholarship. A model trade city. (AMSCO p. 78)

**Samarkand** — Ancient trade city in present-day Uzbekistan, in the Zeravshan River valley. A crossroads between China and the Mediterranean. Home to diverse religions (Christianity, Buddhism, Zoroastrianism, Islam). Center of Islamic learning and magnificent mosque architecture. (AMSCO p. 78)

> [!tip]
> Kashgar and Samarkand are recurring examples on the AP exam for **trade cities as centers of cultural exchange**. They show that trade didn't just move goods — it blended civilizations.

## The Big Comparison: Why Camels Over Horses?

| Feature | Camel | Horse |
|---------|-------|-------|
| Water needs | Can drink 50+ gallons at once, go days without more | Needs frequent water |
| Load capacity | Up to 600 lbs (with load saddle) | Less, and spooks easily |
| Desert performance | Native to Arabian Desert; thrives in heat | Cannot tolerate high heat |
| Control | Cannot use a bit; harder to steer | Controlled by bit |
| Battle use | Poor — can't be quickly controlled | Excellent |
| Trade use | **Ideal** for long desert hauls | Poor for desert trade |

> [!warning]
> A common misconception: camels replaced horses everywhere. In reality, horses remained dominant in Europe, warfare, and steppes. Camels **specifically** dominated desert trade routes.
