# People, States & Movements — Topic 2.1: The Silk Roads

---

## Key People

**Marco Polo (c. 1254–1324)**
Venetian merchant and traveler who journeyed to China under Kublai Khan approximately 1271–1295. His account, *The Travels of Marco Polo*, introduced Europeans to the wealth and sophistication of China and Central Asia. His journey was made possible by the Pax Mongolica. Marco Polo's descriptions of the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads\|Silk Roads]] routes, trading cities like Cambalec (Beijing/Khanbaliq), and the volume of Chinese commerce helped fuel European demand for Asian luxury goods. (AMSCO pp. 77–78)

**Ibn Battuta (1304–c. 1368)**
Moroccan Muslim scholar and traveler who began his journeys in 1325. He used Silk Road networks extensively — crossing North Africa, the Middle East, Central Asia, India, Southeast Asia, and China. His *Rihla* (travelogue) is one of the most important primary sources for understanding the reach and connectivity of 14th-century trade networks. His ability to travel so widely reflects the cosmopolitan, interconnected world the Silk Roads created. (AMSCO p. 75 timeline)

**Francesco Balducci Pegolotti (fl. early 14th c.)**
Italian merchant from Florence who wrote *The Merchant's Handbook* (c. 1471 in some editions). His account of the road from Tana (on the Black Sea) to Cathay (China) describes the route as "perfectly safe, whether by day or by night" — a direct testimony to the effectiveness of the Pax Mongolica in securing trade. Pegolotti's manual provided practical advice on customs officers, tariffs, and goods, showing the sophisticated commercial culture of the Silk Roads. (AMSCO p. 77)

> [!exam-tip]
> Pegolotti's account is a primary source that appears on the AP exam as stimulus material. When you see it, immediately think: **Pax Mongolica → safe trade routes → evidence of Mongol facilitation of commerce**.

**Genghis Khan (c. 1162–1227)**
Founder of the Mongol Empire. Unified nomadic tribes of Central Asia and launched conquests across Eurasia. While his campaigns were violently destructive, they created the unified political framework that enabled the Pax Mongolica under his successors. His grandson Kublai Khan completed the conquest of China, establishing the Yuan Dynasty. (AMSCO p. 78; see [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]])

**Zhang Qian (c. 2nd c. BCE)**
Han Dynasty Chinese envoy who opened the original Silk Roads by traveling westward to establish diplomatic and trade contacts. While not a Period 1 figure, he provides essential historical context — the routes that revived in 1200–1450 were first established in his era.

## Key States & Empires

**Mongol Empire / Yuan Dynasty**
The Mongol Empire at its height controlled territory from China to Eastern Europe. Its sub-regions included the Yuan Dynasty (China), the Ilkhanate (Persia), the Chagatai Khanate (Central Asia), and the Golden Horde (Russia/Eastern Europe). This unprecedented political unity across Eurasia created the infrastructure and security that made the Pax Mongolica possible. See [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]]. (AMSCO p. 78)

**Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258)**
The Islamic caliphate centered in Baghdad. Arab merchants of the Abbasid Empire revived both the overland Silk Roads and the Indian Ocean sea routes in the 8th–9th centuries, ushering in what AMSCO calls the "second golden age of the Silk Roads." The Abbasids were conquered by the Mongols in 1258, transferring control of the Central Asian trade corridors to Mongol authority. See [[Unit-1.2/Dar al-Islam Overview|Unit 1.2]]. (AMSCO pp. 77–78)

**Tang/Song/Yuan China**
China was the eastern anchor of the Silk Roads throughout this period. Tang China contributed the compass, paper, and gunpowder to the revived trade network. Song China exported silk, porcelain, and tea, and developed the financial innovations (flying cash, paper money, bills of exchange) that supported high-volume commerce. Under the Mongol Yuan Dynasty, China was directly integrated into the Silk Roads network at its height. See [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|Unit 1.1]]. (AMSCO pp. 77–78)

**Italian City-States (Venice, Genoa)**
The western terminus of the Silk Roads in Europe was dominated by Italian city-states, especially Venice and Genoa. Their merchants organized the trans-Mediterranean leg of the trade route, connecting European markets to goods arriving from Arab caravans. Travelers like Marco Polo came from this merchant class. The commercial techniques that developed in Italian banking houses (bills of exchange, credit instruments) drew directly on models diffused from China and the Islamic world.

**Hanseatic League**
A commercial confederation of northern German and Scandinavian cities (Lübeck, Hamburg, Riga, and others) that controlled North Sea and Baltic trade. While geographically distant from the Central Asian Silk Roads, the league represented the western extension of trade networks stimulated by demand for Asian goods. League ships eventually connected to Mediterranean Arab caravan trade. (AMSCO p. 80)

**Ilkhanate (Persia)**
One of the four khanates of the divided Mongol Empire, controlling Persia and parts of the Middle East. The Ilkhanate governed key sections of the Silk Roads corridor through Persia, connecting Central Asia to the Mediterranean and enabling cities like Tabriz to flourish as cosmopolitan trade hubs.

## Key Movement: The Crusades

**Crusades (1095–1291)**
A series of European military campaigns aimed at the Holy Land. For the Silk Roads, the Crusades matter not as a military story but as a **demand driver**: European lords and knights who traveled to the Middle East encountered Eastern luxury goods — spices, silks, ceramics — and brought back both the goods and the appetite for them. This helped stimulate European demand that drove Silk Roads commerce in the 12th–14th centuries. See [[Concept Notes/Crusades|Crusades]]. (AMSCO p. 80)
