# People, States & Movements

**Unit 8.6 — Newly Independent States** | AMSCO pp. 589–597

---

## Key Figures

**Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1876–1948)**
Founder and first Governor-General of Pakistan. Originally a member of the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]] and advocate of Hindu-Muslim unity, Jinnah shifted after World War I to lead the Muslim League and demand a separate Muslim state. His "Two-Nation Theory" — that Hindus and Muslims were distinct nations requiring separate states — drove the 1947 partition. He died just over a year after Pakistani independence. Comparison: contrast Jinnah's separatist nationalism with Gandhi's vision of a unified, plural India.

> [!tip]
> A key historiographical debate surrounds Jinnah: was he a principled Muslim nationalist, or an ambitious politician who used religion as leverage? Primary sources on the AP exam often require students to evaluate such competing interpretations using the TAH skill of using evidence to support, modify, or refute a claim.

**Indira Gandhi (1917–1984)**
India's first female Prime Minister (1966–1977, 1980–1984), daughter of Jawaharlal Nehru. Not related to [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]]. Initially underestimated as a figurehead for party elders, she proved politically formidable — nationalizing banks, overseeing India's first nuclear test (1974), and winning a decisive military victory over Pakistan in the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War with Soviet backing. She declared a State of Emergency (1975–1977), jailing political opponents and suspending elections. Returned to power in 1980 but was assassinated in 1984 by her Sikh bodyguards following her controversial order to storm the Golden Temple in Amritsar.

> [!exam-tip]
> Indira Gandhi illustrates the tension between democratic governance and authoritarian tendencies in newly independent states — a pattern the AP exam frequently tests. Her 1975 Emergency showed that even the "world's largest democracy" was vulnerable to authoritarian backsliding.

**Sirimavo Bandaranaike (1916–2000)**
First female Prime Minister in world history (Ceylon/Sri Lanka, 1960). Her husband, Prime Minister Solomon Bandaranaike, was assassinated in 1959; she ran for his seat and won. Served three non-consecutive terms (1960–65, 1970–77, 1994–2000). Pursued socialist economic policies, Sinhalese cultural nationalism (renaming Ceylon to Sri Lanka), and land reform. Her preferential treatment of the Sinhalese Buddhist majority over Tamil Hindus contributed to the ethnic conflict that erupted into civil war in 1983 and lasted until 2009.

**Benazir Bhutto (1953–2007)**
First female Prime Minister of Pakistan (1988–90, 1993–96) and the first female head of government of a Muslim-majority nation. Daughter of PM Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, executed by military dictator Zia ul-Haq in 1979. She faced relentless pressure from the Pakistani military establishment and Islamic conservatives throughout both terms, and corruption charges — many believed to be politically motivated — ended both tenures. Returned from self-imposed exile in 2007 to campaign for elections; assassinated by a suicide bomber in December 2007.

> [!tip]
> All three South Asian female leaders — Bandaranaike, Indira Gandhi, Bhutto — rose to power through family political dynasties (husband/father). This pattern of "dynastic feminism" recurs in postcolonial Asia (also: Corazon Aquino in the Philippines, Sheikh Hasina in Bangladesh). Useful for Comparison SAQs.

**Julius Nyerere (1922–1999)**
First President of Tanzania (1964–1985), widely known as *Mwalimu* ("Teacher"). Led Tanganyika to independence from Britain in 1961 and oversaw its unification with Zanzibar to form Tanzania in 1964. His *ujamaa* socialism — outlined in the 1967 Arusha Declaration — emphasized cooperative villages, state-owned industry, and self-reliance. Tanzania achieved high literacy rates and political stability, but forced collectivization hurt agricultural productivity. Nyerere voluntarily resigned in 1985 — a rare example of peaceful, democratic transfer of power in postcolonial Africa. He remained a moral voice in African politics until his death in 1999.

**Yasser Arafat (1929–2004)**
Chairman of the [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|]] PLO (1969–2004) and President of the Palestinian Authority (1996–2004). Dominated Palestinian nationalism for four decades. Recognized Israel and signed the Oslo Accords in 1993, earning the Nobel Peace Prize. Later negotiations (Camp David 2000, Taba 2001) failed to produce a final-status agreement, and he died in 2004 with Palestinian statehood unrealized. Critics accused him of corruption and failing to accept reasonable peace offers; supporters saw him as the irreplaceable symbol of Palestinian aspirations.

**Pol Pot (1925–1998)**
Leader of the Khmer Rouge and de facto ruler of Cambodia (1975–1979) under the title "Brother Number One." A French-educated intellectual who turned to radical Maoism, Pol Pot implemented "Year Zero" — a program to obliterate all traces of modernity, empty the cities into rural labor camps, and create an agrarian utopia. The result was one of the 20th century's worst genocides: roughly 1.5–2 million deaths in three years. Removed from power by Vietnam's 1979 invasion; died under house arrest in 1998 without facing formal trial.

## States and Movements

**State of Israel (founded 1948)**
Established by a declaration of independence on May 14, 1948, the day Britain's Palestinian mandate expired. Immediately recognized by the US and USSR. Won the ensuing Arab-Israeli War, but at the cost of roughly 700,000 Palestinian refugees. Fought four major wars in its first 25 years (1948, 1956, 1967, 1973). Its founding reflects the intersection of Jewish [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|nationalism]] (Zionism), the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]], and Cold War geopolitics. The unresolved Palestinian question remains the defining issue of Middle Eastern politics.

**Pakistan (founded 1947)**
Created from the Muslim-majority provinces of British India under Muhammad Ali Jinnah's leadership. From its founding, Pakistan faced existential crises: the 1947 partition violence, the 1948 Kashmir War, the 1971 civil war that produced Bangladesh, and recurring tension between civilian and military rule. Pakistan became a nuclear state in 1998 — heightening the stakes of its ongoing conflict with nuclear-armed India over Kashmir.

**Tanzania (founded 1964)**
Formed by the union of Tanganyika (independent 1961) and Zanzibar (independent 1963). Under Nyerere's leadership, Tanzania pursued an independent socialist path, notably declining World Bank structural adjustment programs. Despite economic difficulties, it became a model of political stability and peaceful power transitions in sub-Saharan Africa. Its [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]] was expressed through support for liberation movements in southern Africa.

**Zionist Movement**
Late 19th-century Jewish nationalist movement founded by Theodor Herzl after the Dreyfus Affair. Sought the creation of a Jewish state — primarily in Palestine — as the only permanent solution to European antisemitism. Gained momentum with the Balfour Declaration (1917) and explosive growth in Jewish immigration to Palestine after the Holocaust. Succeeded with Israel's founding in 1948 but created the Palestinian displacement crisis.

**Khmer Rouge (active 1968–1998)**
Cambodian communist movement inspired by Maoist ideas. Rose to power after the Vietnam War destabilized Cambodia. Its "Year Zero" program (1975–79) attempted to abolish money, private property, religion, and formal education — creating an agrarian society based on forced labor. The [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|genocide]] it perpetrated killed approximately one-quarter of Cambodia's population. Connect to [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] and compare with Stalinist collectivization [[Unit-7.4/Economy in the Interwar Period Overview|Economy in the Interwar Period Overview]] and Mao's Great Leap Forward [[Unit-8.4/Spread of Communism after 1900 Overview|Spread of Communism after 1900 Overview]].

**Non-Aligned Movement (context)**
Many newly independent states attempted to avoid choosing sides in the Cold War. Cambodia tried neutrality under Sihanouk; India under Nehru was a founding member of the Non-Aligned Movement (1961). Tanzania's Nyerere also sought to build an independent African socialist path free from both superpower blocs. See [[Unit-8.3/Effects of the Cold War Overview\|Topic 8.3]].
