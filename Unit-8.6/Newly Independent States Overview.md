# Newly Independent States

**Unit 8** — Cold War and Decolonization | **AP Period 4** | AMSCO pp. 589–597

**Essential Question:** What political, economic, and social challenges did newly independent nations face?

**AP Themes:** GOV, SIO, ECN | **AP Skills:** Causation, Comparison

---

## The Big Picture

Between 1945 and 2000, the number of independent states in the world more than doubled. But independence did not automatically bring stability. Former colonial powers drew borders that ignored ethnic, religious, and linguistic realities — and when they departed, those contradictions exploded. The core AP argument for this topic: **decolonization created new states but inherited old problems**, as colonial boundary-drawing, resource extraction, and political under-development left newly independent nations structurally vulnerable.

The topic spans four regional case studies — the Middle East (Israel/Palestine), Southeast Asia (Cambodia), South Asia (India/Pakistan), and East Africa (Tanzania) — each illustrating different dimensions of the post-independence challenge: territorial conflict, genocide, ethnic partition, and socialist development experiments.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the **consequences of decolonization**, not just its causes. Know the specific challenges each new state faced: border conflicts (India/Pakistan, Israel/Palestine), political instability (coups, authoritarianism), economic underdevelopment, and population displacement.

## The Middle East: Israel, Palestine, and Ongoing Conflict

The modern Israeli-Palestinian conflict has roots in the late 19th-century [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] movement of Zionism, which called for a Jewish homeland in Palestine. Following the Dreyfus Affair [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment Overview]], Theodor Herzl organized the First Zionist Congress (1897). Britain's 1917 Balfour Declaration endorsed a Jewish national home in Palestine while also (contradictorily) promising not to harm Arab rights — a double promise that created lasting conflict.

After the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] accelerated Jewish immigration and international sympathy, the UN partitioned Palestine in 1947. In 1948, Israel declared independence and immediately faced invasion by neighboring Arab states. Israel won, but roughly 700,000 Palestinians became refugees in what Palestinians call the *Nakba* (catastrophe). Three more wars followed:

- **1956 Suez Crisis** — Israel, France, and Britain invaded Egypt after Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal (see [[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview\|Topic 8.5]]); international pressure forced withdrawal.
- **1967 Six-Day War** — Israel defeated Egypt, Jordan, and Syria in six days, capturing the Sinai Peninsula, Gaza Strip, West Bank, Golan Heights, and East Jerusalem.
- **1973 Yom Kippur War** — Egypt and Syria launched a surprise attack; Israel repelled it with US aid; the war triggered the Arab oil embargo against the US.

The 1978 **Camp David Accords**, brokered by President Carter, produced the first Arab-Israeli peace treaty: Egypt recognized Israel and Israel returned the Sinai Peninsula. However, the PLO and most Arab states rejected the deal. The Palestinian split between the Fatah-controlled West Bank and Hamas-controlled Gaza in the 21st century deepened the conflict further.

> [!warning]
> The Camp David Accords were between **Israel and Egypt only** — not a comprehensive Middle East peace. The Palestinian issue remained unresolved. Anwar Sadat was assassinated in 1981 by opponents of the treaty.

## Cambodia: Independence, Cold War, and Genocide

Cambodia gained independence from France in 1953 and initially tried to maintain Cold War neutrality. But proximity to Vietnam drew it into the conflict. After the Vietnam War destabilized the region, the communist [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Khmer Rouge]] under Pol Pot seized power in 1975. Modeling themselves on Mao's Cultural Revolution, they targeted intellectuals, ethnic minorities, and urban populations — executing or starving roughly 1.5–2 million people (about one-quarter of Cambodia's population). Mass graves in the "killing fields" continue to be discovered.

Vietnam invaded in 1979, toppling Pol Pot and stabilizing the country. A UN-monitored peace agreement in 1991 eventually restored constitutional monarchy under Prince Norodom Sihanouk.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Khmer Rouge genocide is a strong **comparison** example: compare with the Holocaust [[Unit-7.8/Mass Atrocities Overview|Mass Atrocities Overview]], the Armenian Genocide [[Unit-7.2/Causes of World War I Overview|Causes of World War I Overview]], or the Rwandan Genocide. The AP regularly asks students to compare episodes of mass atrocity and explain state-sponsored violence.

## India and Pakistan: Partition and Its Consequences

In 1947, Britain partitioned colonial India into Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan. The partition was catastrophically violent: 10–20 million people fled across borders, and 500,000–1 million died in communal violence. India became the world's largest democracy; Pakistan alternated between elected leaders and military dictators. Both countries claimed the Muslim-majority Kashmir region, and multiple wars were fought over it (1947, 1965, 1971, 1999). Tensions intensified when both countries developed nuclear weapons.

> [!warning]
> Partition is a frequent AP comparison prompt. It parallels the UN partition of Palestine in 1947 — both involved a European power drawing lines that ignored demographic realities, creating refugee crises and lasting territorial conflicts.

## Women as Heads of State in Newly Independent Nations

One of the more surprising patterns in post-independence South Asia is the rise of female heads of state — often daughters or widows of male leaders, but genuine wielders of power in their own right:

- **Sirimavo Bandaranaike** (Sri Lanka/Ceylon, 1960) — world's first female prime minister
- **Indira Gandhi** (India, 1966–1977, 1980–1984) — oversaw India's nuclear program and [[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]; assassinated 1984
- **Benazir Bhutto** (Pakistan, 1988–1990, 1993–1996) — first female leader of a Muslim-majority nation; assassinated 2007

> [!tip]
> These women gained power in societies with deeply patriarchal traditions, often by leveraging family political dynasties. This is a useful **comparison** point: contrast with women's limited political roles in Western democracies at the same time.

## Tanzania: African Socialism and Development

Tanzania (formerly Tanganyika and Zanzibar) gained independence from Britain in 1961. Its first president, **Julius Nyerere**, pursued *ujamaa* ("familyhood") — an African socialist model of cooperative agriculture and collective development outlined in the 1967 Arusha Declaration. His literacy campaigns achieved impressive results, and he was widely respected for his anti-corruption principles and voluntary resignation in 1985. However, ujamaa's forced collectivization produced economic stagnation rather than development, illustrating the common pattern: **ambitious post-independence programs faced structural economic limits inherited from colonialism**.

## Migration: Colonial Ties Persist

After independence, migrants from newly independent countries often moved to former colonial metropoles — London, Paris, Lisbon — maintaining economic and cultural ties. South Asians migrated to Britain; North and West Africans to France; Filipinos to the United States. These migrations reflect the lasting economic inequality between former colonies and their colonizers, and they created multicultural immigrant communities in Western cities.

> [!exam-tip]
> "Metropole" is a key term. Post-independence migration is an ECN and SIO theme that connects to Unit 9 topics on globalization and cultural exchange.

## AP Exam Strategy

**High-frequency LEQ prompts:** Explain the causes or consequences of decolonization. Compare independence movements or post-independence challenges across regions. Evaluate continuity and change in the relationship between former colonies and colonial powers.

**Top causal chains for SAQ/LEQ:**
1. Colonial boundary-drawing → ethnic/religious conflict at independence (India/Pakistan; Israel/Palestine)
2. Cold War superpower rivalry → support for authoritarian regimes in newly independent states
3. Economic underdevelopment under colonialism → structural poverty after independence → political instability
4. Holocaust + Zionism → Israeli statehood → Arab-Israeli wars → refugee crisis → ongoing conflict
