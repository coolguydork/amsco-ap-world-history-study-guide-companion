# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 8.6 — Newly Independent States** | AMSCO pp. 589–597

---

**Middle East**
Region of Southwest Asia and North Africa that became a major Cold War flashpoint due to vast oil reserves and the Arab-Israeli conflict. Both superpowers competed for influence through arms sales and aid. Multiple Arab-Israeli wars (1948, 1956, 1967, 1973) defined the region's postwar history. For the AP exam, the Middle East is the primary case study for post-independence territorial conflict rooted in colonial-era boundary decisions.

**Six-Day War**
June 1967 war in which Israel rapidly defeated Egypt, Jordan, and Syria on three simultaneous fronts. Israel captured the Sinai Peninsula, Gaza Strip, West Bank, Golan Heights, and East Jerusalem — dramatically expanding Israeli territory and deepening the Palestinian refugee crisis. The occupied territories became a central obstacle to peace for decades. Connect: US support for Israel deepened Arab hostility toward the US throughout the Cold War.

**Yom Kippur War**
October 1973 surprise attack by Egypt and Syria against Israel on the Jewish holy day of Yom Kippur. Israel repelled the attack with US military aid while the Soviet Union aided Arab states, making this a Cold War proxy conflict (see [[Unit-8.3/Effects of the Cold War Overview\|Topic 8.3]]). The war triggered the Arab oil embargo against the US, causing an energy crisis in the West. It ultimately set the stage for the Camp David Accords.

**Camp David Accords**
1978 peace agreement brokered by US President Jimmy Carter between Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin. Egypt recognized Israel's right to exist and Israel returned the Sinai Peninsula — the first Arab-Israeli peace agreement. Sadat was assassinated in 1981 by opponents of the deal. The Accords did not resolve the Palestinian question, and most Arab states rejected the treaty.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Camp David Accords are frequently paired with primary source excerpts on the AP exam. Know: (1) who mediated (Carter), (2) who signed (Sadat and Begin), (3) what was exchanged (recognition for Sinai), and (4) what remained unresolved (Palestinian statehood, other Arab states).

**Palestinian Liberation Organization (PLO)**
Political and paramilitary organization founded in 1964 representing Palestinian Arabs seeking statehood. Led by Yasser Arafat for decades. Initially rejected by Israel as a terrorist organization; recognized in the 1993 Oslo Accords. The PLO represents Palestinian national self-determination and the demand for a Palestinian state in the West Bank and Gaza Strip. It split into rival factions, with Fatah dominating the PLO and the West Bank.

**Fatah**
The largest faction of the PLO, founded by Yasser Arafat; politically secular and nationalist rather than Islamist. Controls the Palestinian Authority in the West Bank. Signed the Oslo Accords with Israel in 1993, which recognized Israel and sought a two-state solution. Rival of Hamas; their split in 2007 divided Palestinian leadership between the West Bank (Fatah) and Gaza (Hamas).

**Hamas**
Palestinian Islamist organization founded in 1987 during the First Intifada (Palestinian uprising). Controls the Gaza Strip since 2007. Refuses to recognize Israel's right to exist. Its growth reflects the rise of Islamist alternatives to secular Palestinian nationalism after the Oslo peace process stalled. The Israeli government's tight border controls on Gaza created humanitarian and political crises.

> [!warning]
> On the AP exam, distinguish Fatah (secular, PLO, West Bank, accepts two-state solution) from Hamas (Islamist, Gaza, rejects Israel's existence). Both are Palestinian nationalist movements but with fundamentally different ideologies and strategies.

**Khmer Rouge**
Cambodian communist guerrilla movement that overthrew the government in 1975. Under Pol Pot, implemented an agrarian revolution targeting intellectuals, ethnic minorities, and city dwellers — killing an estimated 1.5–2 million people (about 25% of Cambodia's population) through execution, forced labor, and famine. Removed from power by Vietnamese invasion in 1979. Compare with [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] and the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] [[Unit-7.8/Mass Atrocities Overview|Mass Atrocities Overview]].

**Kashmir**
Muslim-majority region disputed between India and Pakistan since the 1947 partition. Its Hindu maharaja chose India, while its Muslim population leaned toward Pakistan — creating an irresolvable territorial claim. Multiple wars were fought over it (1947, 1965, 1971, 1999). Tensions intensified dramatically when both countries developed nuclear weapons. India controls ~45%, Pakistan ~35%, and China ~20% of the region.

**Sirimavo Bandaranaike**
Prime Minister of Sri Lanka (Ceylon) elected in 1960 — the world's first female elected head of government. Her husband, assassinated in office in 1959, had previously held the position. She pursued socialist economic policies and Sinhalese cultural nationalism. Served three terms (1960–65, 1970–77, 1994–2000). Her policies favoring the Sinhalese majority over Tamil minorities contributed to the ethnic civil war that erupted in 1983.

**Indira Gandhi**
Prime Minister of India (1966–1977, 1980–1984), daughter of India's first PM Jawaharlal Nehru (not related to [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]]). Oversaw India's Green Revolution (agricultural modernization), first nuclear test (1974), and military victory in the 1971 Indo-Pakistani War with Soviet support. Declared a State of Emergency (1975–1977) suspending civil liberties. Assassinated in 1984 by her own Sikh bodyguards after ordering a military assault on the Golden Temple in Amritsar.

**Benazir Bhutto**
Prime Minister of Pakistan (1988–1990, 1993–1996) — the first female head of government of a Muslim-majority nation. Daughter of Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, who was executed by the military in 1979. She faced persistent opposition from the Pakistani military and Islamic conservatives, and corruption charges cut short both terms. Returned from exile in 2007 and was assassinated during the election campaign.

**Julius Nyerere**
First President of Tanzania (1964–1985). Championed *ujamaa* (African socialism) — cooperative agriculture, collective farming, and state-led development outlined in the 1967 Arusha Declaration. Achieved some of Africa's highest literacy rates and maintained personal integrity (refusing personal enrichment). Resigned voluntarily in 1985 — a model of peaceful power transfer in post-colonial Africa. His forced villagization programs, however, produced economic stagnation.

> [!tip]
> Nyerere is an excellent **comparison** subject: contrast his African socialist model with Nkrumah's pan-Africanism [[Unit-8.5/Decolonization after 1900 Overview|Decolonization after 1900 Overview]], Mao's collectivization in China [[Unit-8.4/Spread of Communism after 1900 Overview|Spread of Communism after 1900 Overview]], or Soviet collectivization. All attempted to modernize agricultural societies through state control; all produced economic hardship alongside political goals.

**metropole**
The dominant urban or national center of a colonial empire — London, Paris, Lisbon, Amsterdam. Used in postcolonial studies to describe the imperial center to which resources and labor flowed from colonies. After independence, former colonial subjects migrated to metropoles for economic opportunity (South Asians to London, Algerians to Paris, Filipinos to the US), maintaining economic and cultural ties between former colonies and colonial powers.

**ujamaa**
Swahili for "familyhood"; Julius Nyerere's African socialist philosophy and policy for Tanzania. Emphasized cooperative agriculture, collective villages (*vijiji*), and communal self-reliance rather than dependence on foreign aid. Literacy campaigns, free education, and egalitarian land distribution were central. While ideologically appealing, forced villagization disrupted traditional agricultural patterns and contributed to economic stagnation.

**Balfour Declaration**
1917 British government statement endorsing "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine, while simultaneously promising not to harm "the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities." A foundational contradiction: Britain made incompatible promises to Zionists and Arab nationalists during World War I. After World War II, these contradictions exploded into the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

**Zionism**
Late 19th-century Jewish nationalist movement calling for the creation of a Jewish state, primarily in Palestine. Founded by Theodor Herzl after the Dreyfus Affair [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment Overview]] convinced him that Jewish assimilation in Europe had failed. Zionism is an example of [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] applied to a diaspora people. It succeeded with the establishment of Israel in 1948 but created the displacement of Arab Palestinians.
