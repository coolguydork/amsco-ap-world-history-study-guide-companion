# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Unit 8.6 — Newly Independent States** | AMSCO pp. 589–597

---

## AP Themes in This Topic

### GOV — Governance

The central governance challenge of Topic 8.6 is that independence did not automatically produce stable, democratic governments. Newly independent states inherited colonial administrative structures designed for extraction, not self-rule. The result across most of the developing world was political instability: military coups, authoritarian leaders, and the suppression of political opponents. India was the major exception — it maintained democratic institutions despite poverty and diversity — but even Indira Gandhi suspended democracy with the 1975 Emergency.

The Cold War reinforced authoritarianism. Both superpowers supported friendly dictators in newly independent states, prioritizing strategic alignment over democratic values. Pakistan's recurring military coups, Cambodia's vulnerability to Khmer Rouge takeover, and the Arab states' defeat in multiple wars against Israel all reflect governance failures with roots in the colonial past.

### SIO — Social Interactions and Organization

The most dramatic social consequence of decolonization was **population displacement**. India/Pakistan's partition produced 10–20 million refugees and 500,000–1 million dead in communal violence — one of the largest forced migrations in history. Palestinian displacement (700,000 refugees in 1948) created a refugee crisis that persists today. These displacements reflected colonial powers' failure to account for ethnic, religious, and linguistic realities when drawing borders.

A second social pattern is the emergence of **female political leaders** in postcolonial South Asia. Sirimavo Bandaranaike, Indira Gandhi, and Benazir Bhutto all rose to power in societies with deeply patriarchal traditions, typically through family dynasties. Their leadership challenges simple narratives about women's political exclusion in the postcolonial world.

Post-independence **migration to metropoles** also transformed social composition in former colonial powers: South Asian communities in British cities, North African communities in France, Filipino communities in the US. Economic and cultural ties between colonies and colonizers persisted long after formal independence.

### ECN — Economics and Technology

Newly independent states faced structural economic underdevelopment inherited from colonialism. Colonial economies had been organized to extract raw materials and provide markets for manufactured goods — not to build industrial capacity or train local professionals. Post-independence leaders responded with various development strategies:

- **Import-substitution industrialization** (India, Tanzania, Egypt) — state-led industrialization behind protective tariffs
- **Ujamaa/African socialism** (Tanzania) — collectivized agriculture and cooperative village economies
- **Arab oil power** (Middle East) — nationalization of oil resources as an economic strategy (also Topic 8.5)

Most of these strategies achieved mixed results: literacy campaigns and basic infrastructure improved, but economic growth remained sluggish, and foreign debt mounted. The 1973 Arab oil embargo showed newly independent states could sometimes use resources as geopolitical leverage.

## Historical Reasoning: Causation

**Essential Question:** What caused the political, economic, and social challenges that newly independent states faced?

The core causal argument:

1. **Colonial boundary-drawing** created states with incompatible ethnic, religious, and linguistic populations (India/Pakistan, Israel/Palestine, much of sub-Saharan Africa). When colonial powers withdrew, these contradictions produced violence.

2. **Underdeveloped political institutions** — colonial governments had not trained local populations for self-governance. Newly independent states inherited administrations built for control, not participation.

3. **Cold War intervention** — both superpowers competed for influence, supporting authoritarian leaders who served strategic interests and destabilizing governments that seemed to lean toward the opposing bloc.

4. **Economic dependency** — colonial extraction created structural poverty that made economic development extremely difficult after independence.

> [!exam-tip]
> For LEQ, the most defensible thesis argues that **the challenges of newly independent states were primarily structural** (inherited from colonialism) rather than failures of individual leaders or cultures. This argument can sustain a sophisticated argument by acknowledging how Cold War intervention compounded colonial legacies.

## Historical Reasoning: Comparison

This topic is rich for comparison across regions and with earlier topics:

**Partition as a pattern:**
- India/Pakistan (1947) → colonial power draws line; religious violence; mass displacement; lasting territorial conflict (Kashmir)
- Israel/Palestine (1947–48) → UN partition; Arab rejection; war; refugee crisis; lasting conflict
- Both cases show: partition rarely resolves ethnic/religious conflict; it often intensifies it

**Female heads of state — dynastic pattern:**
- Sirimavo Bandaranaike (Ceylon), Indira Gandhi (India), Benazir Bhutto (Pakistan), Corazon Aquino (Philippines), Sheikh Hasina (Bangladesh)
- Pattern: women succeed fathers or husbands; traditional patriarchal norms simultaneously constrain and elevate them

**[[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] comparison:**
- Khmer Rouge (Cambodia, 1975–79) vs. [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] (Germany, 1941–45) vs. Armenian Genocide (1915) vs. Rwandan Genocide (1994)
- Compare: ideology driving violence; methods; international response (or non-response); perpetrators' use of state power

**African socialist models:**
- Tanzania's ujamaa vs. Nkrumah's Ghana vs. Nyerere's "Third Way" — all sought independence from both capitalist West and Soviet-style communism; all faced economic difficulties

## Think as a Historian: Using Evidence to Support, Modify, or Refute a Claim

AMSCO's TAH section for Topic 8.6 focuses on the **partition of India** and asks students to evaluate three primary sources — Gandhi's argument for unity, Jinnah's acceptance of partition, and a historian's analysis of Jinnah's motivations.

The key skill: **the same piece of evidence can be used to support, modify, or refute different claims**. This flexibility is fundamental to historical argumentation.

**Practice example:**

*Claim A:* The partition of India was necessary to protect Muslim interests and prevent discrimination in a Hindu-majority state.

*Claim B:* The partition of India was a tragic failure of leadership that needlessly divided a culture with centuries of shared history.

Both claims can use the same primary sources — Gandhi's quote, Jinnah's address — to make opposite arguments. The historian's job is not to find "the right answer" but to evaluate the **relative strength** of competing claims given the available evidence.

> [!tip]
> For the AP DBQ and LEQ, sophisticated argument (Point 4 of the rubric) requires acknowledging a counterargument or complication. The partition debate is ideal practice: acknowledge Jinnah's reasonable concerns about Muslim minority rights while also noting that partition produced catastrophic violence — then argue which factor better explains the long-term outcome.

## Historical Perspectives

**Nationalist perspective:** Independence was a triumph over colonial oppression, regardless of the difficulties that followed. The challenges of newly independent states were imposed by colonialism, not inherent to their peoples or cultures.

**Modernization theory (1950s–60s Western view):** Newly independent states were at an earlier "stage" of development and would eventually catch up to Western institutions and prosperity if they adopted market economies and democratic governance.

**Dependency theory / Postcolonial critique:** The problems of newly independent states were structural, embedded in their continuing economic relationship with former colonial powers. Modernization theory was itself a form of neo-colonialism that served Western interests.

**Cold War realist perspective:** Both superpowers viewed newly independent states instrumentally — as potential allies in global competition — rather than as sovereign nations deserving self-determination. US and Soviet interventions frequently undermined genuine independence.
