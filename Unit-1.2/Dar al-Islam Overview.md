# Topic 1.2 — Developments in Dar al-Islam

> [!summary] Essential Question
> In the period from c. 1200 to c. 1450, **how did Islamic states arise**, and how did **major religious systems shape society**?

## Context: The Islamic World as Connector

After the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632 CE, Islam spread rapidly outward from Arabia — through military conquest, merchant activity, and missionary work. By 1200, the Islamic world stretched from Spain to Southeast Asia. The **Abbasid Caliphate**, based in Baghdad, had been the center of this civilization. But by the 1100s and 1200s, the Abbasid Empire was under severe pressure from multiple directions.

The central story of Topic 1.2 is: **the decline of the Abbasid Caliphate** and **the fragmentation of the Islamic world into multiple new states** — all while Islamic intellectual and cultural achievements continued to flourish.

> [!exam-tip]
> "Dar al-Islam" literally means "House of Islam" — the totality of lands under Muslim political or cultural influence. The key insight: even as political unity fragmented, **cultural and intellectual unity** (shared language, law, trade networks) persisted across Dar al-Islam.

---

## The Decline of the Abbasid Caliphate

The Abbasid Empire faced four major challengers in the 11th–13th centuries:

### 1. The Egyptian Mamluks
Arabs frequently purchased enslaved soldiers — often ethnic Turks from Central Asia — called **[[Concept Notes/Mamluks|Mamluks]]**. In Egypt, Mamluks seized control of the government, establishing the **Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517)**. They prospered by facilitating cotton and sugar trade between the Islamic world and Europe. When Portuguese and other European sea routes bypassed their territory, Mamluk power declined.

> [!tip]
> The Mamluks later stopped the Mongol advance at the Battle of Ain Jalut (1260) — the first major Mongol defeat. This is often tested as a turning point.

### 2. The Seljuk Turks
The **Seljuk Turks** — Central Asian Muslims — began conquering parts of the Middle East in the 11th century, eventually extending power almost to Western China. The Seljuk leader took the title **sultan**, reducing the Abbasid caliph to a *religious* rather than political authority. The Seljuks also limited Christian pilgrim access to Jerusalem, sparking the [[Concept Notes/Crusades|Crusades]].

### 3. The Crusaders
European Christians organized military campaigns (the **Crusades**, 1095–1200s) to retake Jerusalem and the Holy Land. These were partly a response to Seljuk restrictions on Christian pilgrims. The First Crusade captured Jerusalem in 1099; Muslim forces under **Saladin** retook it in 1187. The Crusades promoted cultural exchange between Europe and the Islamic world.

### 4. The Mongols
The **[[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]]** were the most devastating force. They conquered and destroyed the remaining Abbasid Empire in **1258**, burning Baghdad and ending the caliphate. They then pushed westward but were stopped in Egypt by the Mamluks.

### Economic Decline of Baghdad
Even before the Mongol conquest, trade patterns had shifted northward, away from Baghdad. As the city lost its place at the center of trade, it lost tax revenue and population. Canals fell into disrepair, agriculture contracted, and infrastructure crumbled. This **economic competition** weakened the Abbasid state from within — the Mongol attack finished it off.

## The Islamic World After the Abbasids: Fragmentation and Continuity

After the Abbasid collapse, the Islamic world splintered politically. New states were led primarily by **Turkic peoples** from Central Asia, not Arabs. Key examples:

- **Mamluk Sultanate** — Egypt and North Africa (Turkic origin)
- **Seljuk Turks** — Middle East
- **Delhi Sultanate** — South Asia (see [[Unit-1.3/South and Southeast Asia Overview|South and Southeast Asia]])
- *Later* (16th century): **Ottoman Empire** (Turkey), **Safavid Empire** (Persia), **Mughal Empire** (India) — see [[Unit-3.1/Gunpowder Empires Overview|Land-Based Empires]]

Despite political fragmentation, Islamic civilization maintained **cultural continuity** through:

- **Shariah** — a shared legal system creating similar laws across regions
- **Arabic** — common language of scholarship and religion
- **Great universities** — in Baghdad (Iraq), Córdoba (Spain), Cairo (Egypt), and Bukhara (Central Asia)
- **Merchant networks** — connecting Islamic lands via the Indian Ocean and Silk Roads

> [!tip]
> This fragmentation-with-continuity pattern is a major theme. Think of it like this: the political map of Islam changed dramatically, but its cultural map remained coherent. A scholar from Morocco (Ibn Battuta) could travel to Delhi and Mali and find Islamic law, Arabic literacy, and familiar institutions everywhere.

## The Islamic Golden Age: Intellectual Achievements

### Cultural Continuities: Preserving and Transmitting Knowledge

Islamic scholars embodied Muhammad's advice: *"Go in quest of knowledge even unto China."* They:

- **Translated Greek classics** into Arabic, preserving Aristotle and other thinkers
- **Transferred Indian mathematics** (including algebra, geometry, and what the West calls "Arabic numerals" — actually Indian in origin) to Europeans
- **Taught Europeans to make paper** (a technology adopted from China)

The **[[Concept Notes/House of Wisdom|House of Wisdom]]** in Baghdad was the greatest center of learning in the world — a library, translation center, and research institution.

### Cultural Innovations: Original Achievements

**Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274)** — One of the most celebrated Islamic scholars. Contributed to astronomy, law, logic, ethics, mathematics, philosophy, and medicine. Built the world's most advanced observatory. Laid the groundwork for **trigonometry** as an independent field.

**Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)** — Pioneer of **historiography** (the study of historical methods) and **sociology**. Known for his cyclical theory of civilization.

**ʿĀʾisha al-Bāʿūniyya (1460–1507)** — The most prolific female Muslim writer before the 20th century. A Sufi poet whose works describe the journey toward mystical illumination.

Medical advances, hospital care, and licensing of doctors and pharmacists (through examinations) improved in cities like Cairo.

## Sufism: The Mystical Heart of Islam

**[[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]]** was a mystical movement within Islam that emphasized **inner reflection** and direct experience of the divine, rather than intellectual study of the Quran. Sufis believed in truths that could not be understood through learning alone.

Sufism likely originated as a reaction to the perceived luxury of early Umayyad rulers. Key points:

- Sufis were **important missionaries** who spread Islam across Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia
- They **adapted to local cultures**, sometimes weaving local religious elements into Islamic practice — making conversion feel less like abandoning one's roots
- Sufi missionaries were especially effective in [[Unit-1.3/South and Southeast Asia Overview|Southeast Asia]] and [[Unit-1.5/Africa Overview|West Africa]]

Compare: The **[[Unit-1.3/Bhakti Movement|Bhakti Movement]]** in Hinduism had similar characteristics — mystical, emotionally driven, accessible to lower-caste people and women.

## Social Structures in Dar al-Islam

### Commerce and Merchants
Unlike in China or Europe, Islamic society **elevated the status of merchants**. Muhammad himself had been a merchant, as had his first wife. Merchants who maintained fair dealings and gave to charity were esteemed. Some were sent out as missionaries. This made commerce morally compatible with religious devotion — a significant difference from Chinese Confucian culture, where merchants ranked last.

### Slavery
Islam permitted slavery but with important restrictions: Muslims could not enslave other Muslims; Jews, Christians, and Zoroastrians were also exempt. Slavery was common (slaves imported from Africa, Kievan Rus, and Central Asia), but **hereditary slavery had not fully developed**. Many slaves converted to Islam and were freed.

Enslaved women in Islamic households sometimes had more practical freedoms than legal wives — they could go to markets, perform music, and accumulate money toward buying their freedom.

### The Status of Women
Muhammad raised the status of women compared to pre-Islamic Arabian norms:
- Insisted **dowries** be paid to the bride, not her father
- Forbade **female infanticide**
- His first wife Khadijah was educated, owned a business, and chose to propose marriage to him

Under Islamic law, women could **inherit property**, **retain it after marriage**, **remarry if widowed**, and in some cases **initiate divorce**. Their court testimony was protected but worth half that of a man.

> [!warning]
> A common misconception: "Islamic women had no rights." In comparative context, Islamic women often had *more* legal rights than Christian or Jewish women of the same era. However, urbanization in Islamic areas brought *new* restrictions — veiling and harem practices grew as cities expanded. Context matters.

### Islamic Rule in Spain: Al-Andalus

After conquering North Africa, Muslim forces invaded Spain in **711** and designated **Córdoba** as their capital. They were stopped at the **Battle of Tours (732)** — the limit of Islamic expansion into Western Europe.

Under the Umayyads, Córdoba became the most sophisticated city in Western Europe:
- **Largest library in the world**
- A climate of **toleration** — Muslims, Christians, and Jews coexisted and exchanged ideas
- **Al-Andalus** (the Islamic name for Spain) became a center of learning

Key intellectual transfers from al-Andalus to Europe:
- **Ibn Rushd (Averroes)** wrote influential commentaries on Aristotle that influenced Jewish philosopher **Maimonides**, who in turn influenced Christian philosopher **Thomas Aquinas**
- Paper-making, Indian mathematics, and medical knowledge moved from the Islamic world to Christian Europe through Spain

> [!exam-tip]
> Al-Andalus is one of the most important **cross-cultural exchange** examples on the AP exam. The chain goes: India → Islamic scholars → al-Andalus → Christian Europe → Renaissance and Scientific Revolution. Be able to trace this chain.
