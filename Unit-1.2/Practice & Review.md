## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari, History of the Prophets and Kings, "Peace Terms with Jerusalem, 636 C.E.," 10th century*

> "In the name of God the Merciful and the Compassionate: This is the safe-conduct accorded by the servant of God Umar, the Commander of the Faithful, to the people of Jerusalem. He accords them safe-conduct for their persons, their property, their churches, their crosses, their sound and their sick, and the rest of their worship. . . . No constraint shall be exercised against them in religion nor shall any harm be done to any among them. . . .
>
> The people of Jerusalem must pay the jizya [required tax on non-Muslims] in the same way as the people of other cities. They must expel the Romans [Byzantine soldiers and officials] and the brigands from the city. . . .
>
> Those of the people of Jerusalem who wish to remove their persons and effects and depart with the Romans [Byzantines] and abandon their churches and their crosses shall have safe-conduct for their persons, their churches, and their crosses, until they reach safety."

---

**Question 1.** Which trait of Islamic rule described in this passage was also evident in Islamic rule in al-Andalus?

(A) The persecution of other religions by Islamic leaders
(B) The toleration of other religions by Muslims in conquered territory
(C) The conflict between Romans and Byzantines over territory
(D) The desire to improve relations with the Romans in Jerusalem

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The passage shows Umar guaranteeing the safety of Jerusalem's Christians, their property, churches, and right to worship — the definition of religious toleration. This same policy of coexistence was practiced in al-Andalus, where Muslims, Jews, and Christians all lived together and even influenced one another intellectually. (A) is the opposite of what the passage describes. (C) and (D) misread the passage: Romans/Byzantines are mentioned only as people leaving the city, not as parties in conflict with each other.

---

**Question 2.** What viewpoint led Umar to this trait of Islamic rule?

(A) He did not actively seek converts to his faith.
(B) He believed Christians and Jews were only a small threat to his faith.
(C) He was more concerned with political than religious issues.
(D) He considered Christians and Jews to be "People of the Book."

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The concept of **dhimmi** — non-Muslims (especially "People of the Book," meaning Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians) who share monotheism and scriptural traditions — is the theological basis for Islamic toleration of these groups. Because Umar viewed Jerusalem's Christians as fellow monotheists with a shared prophetic tradition, he protected them. (A) is incorrect — Islam actively sought converts; (B) is not supported by the text; (C) contradicts Umar's explicit religious framing ("In the name of God the Merciful and Compassionate").

---

**Question 3.** Which development resulted from a change in Umar's policy toward Jerusalem by the Seljuks?

(A) The beginning of the Crusades by European Christians
(B) A shift in trade patterns
(C) An increase in the power of the Byzantine Empire
(D) A decrease in taxes paid by Jerusalem residents

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> When the Seljuk Turks replaced the tolerant Abbasid policy and restricted Christian pilgrimages to Jerusalem, European Christians organized the Crusades to reclaim access. This is one of the most direct cause-and-effect chains in Topic 1.2. (B) is not directly caused by Jerusalem policy; (C) is incorrect — Byzantine power was declining; (D) is the opposite — the Seljuks were not known for tax relief.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Religious Syncretism and Sufi Practice

*Use the passage below (a Muslim describing Christians in the 12th century, from Francesco Gabrieli, Arab Historians of the Crusades, 1969):*

> "I paid a visit to the tomb of John the son of Zechariah . . . After saying my prayers, I came out into the square . . . I found a half-closed gate, opened it and entered a church. Inside were about ten old men, their bare heads as white as combed cotton. They were facing east, and wore on their chests [wooden sticks] ending in crossbars turned up like the rear of a saddle. . . . The sight of their piety touched my heart, but at the same time it displeased and saddened me, for I had never seen such zeal and devotion among the Muslims.
>
> For some time I brooded on this experience, until one day . . . we dismounted and went into a long building . . . I saw about a hundred prayer mats, and on each a sufi, his face expressing peaceful serenity, and his body humble devotion. This was a reassuring sight . . . Before this I had never seen sufis in their monastery, and was ignorant of the way they lived."

**(A) Identify ONE way that religious syncretism appeared in Dar al-Islam in the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> One way that religious syncretism appeared in Dar al-Islam was through the practices of Sufi missionaries, who adapted Islamic worship to local traditions when spreading the faith into new regions. Sufis often incorporated local spiritual practices, music, and customs into Islamic expression, blending them with Islamic devotion rather than demanding rigid conformity to Arab norms. This produced hybrid forms of Islam that preserved local cultural elements while embracing the core Islamic faith.

**(B) Explain ONE way in which the behavior of the Sufis demonstrates a difference within the practice of Islam in the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> The Sufis described in the passage demonstrate a difference within Islam by emphasizing contemplative, physical devotion — sitting on prayer mats in "peaceful serenity" with "humble devotion" — rather than the intellectual study of the Quran favored by orthodox Muslims. While mainstream Muslims prioritized rational understanding of Islamic texts (a tradition exemplified by scholars like Ibn Rushd and the ulama), Sufis believed that direct, mystical experience of God could only be achieved through introspection and self-discipline — not through book learning. This difference in emphasis created a distinct movement within Islam that sometimes made orthodox scholars uncomfortable.

**(C) Explain ONE outcome of Christians and Muslims living in close proximity in the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> One outcome of Christians and Muslims living in close proximity was the transfer of knowledge that transformed European intellectual life. In al-Andalus, where Muslim, Jewish, and Christian scholars worked together, the philosopher Ibn Rushd (Averroes) produced commentaries on Aristotle that were later adopted by the Jewish thinker Maimonides and then by the Christian philosopher Thomas Aquinas. This chain of intellectual borrowing — which would not have been possible without cross-faith proximity — helped lay the foundations for the European Renaissance and Scientific Revolution.

### SAQ 2 — Women's Status and Umayyad Spain

**(A) Identify ONE way the status of women under Islam differed from the status of women in China during the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> One way the status of Islamic women differed from Chinese women was in their legal right to own and inherit property. Under Islamic law (Sharia), women could inherit property and retain ownership of it after marriage, as well as receive cash settlements upon divorce. By contrast, Chinese women under Neo-Confucian norms had extremely limited property rights and were increasingly controlled by male family members — a reality reinforced by the growing practice of foot binding, which physically constrained women's mobility and independence.

**(B) Explain ONE way in which the Umayyad rulers in Córdoba were successful during the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> One way the Umayyad rulers in Córdoba were successful was by creating a climate of religious toleration that attracted scholars and merchants from across the Mediterranean world. By allowing Muslims, Christians, and Jews to coexist and collaborate, the Umayyads made Córdoba into one of the world's greatest centers of learning, with the largest library in the world. This intellectual environment produced scholars like Ibn Rushd (Averroes) and attracted trade from China and Southeast Asia into the rest of Europe, making al-Andalus both a cultural and commercial powerhouse.

**(C) Explain ONE reason why Umayyad leaders were able to keep power in Spain during the period 1200–1450.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> One reason the Umayyads were able to maintain power in Spain was their use of the dhimmi system, which gave non-Muslim subjects — Christians and Jews — defined legal protections and the right to practice their religions in exchange for paying the jizya tax. This practical arrangement reduced resistance from the large non-Muslim population of Spain and made Islamic rule acceptable to communities that might otherwise have organized rebellion. By governing through accommodation rather than forced conversion, the Umayyads maintained political stability across a religiously diverse population for centuries.

## Historical Reasoning Practice

### Think As a Historian: Cause and Effect

**1. The Decline of Baghdad**

Read the "Economic Competition" section (AMSCO p. 16). The decline of Baghdad follows a classic cause-and-effect chain:

> **Cause**: Trade routes shifted northward, away from Baghdad's traditional position at the hub of Afro-Eurasian trade.
>
> **Effect 1**: Baghdad lost wealth and population as merchants and revenue left the city.
>
> **Effect 2**: The city could no longer maintain its canal infrastructure.
>
> **Effect 3**: Agricultural decline followed — farmers could no longer produce enough food.
>
> **Effect 4**: Urban infrastructure decayed, weakening the city before the Mongol invasion of 1258.

*Key insight*: The Mongols destroyed what was already a weakened city. Economic decline preceded and enabled military collapse.

### Think As a Historian: Continuity

**2. Knowledge Transmission as Historical Continuity**

Muhammad's advice — "Go in quest of knowledge even unto China" — established a **cultural norm** that persisted across centuries and political changes. This single principle produced remarkable historical continuity:

- 8th century Abbasid scholars translated Greek texts in Baghdad.
- 12th century scholars in al-Andalus built on that foundation with Averroes.
- 13th century scholars like Thomas Aquinas received those insights.
- 15th–16th century European Renaissance scholars drew on centuries of accumulated Islamic scholarship.

Even as caliphates rose and fell, even as the Mongols destroyed Baghdad, the commitment to preserving and building on knowledge continued.

### Think As a Historian: Historical Process — Knowledge Transfer

**3. The al-Andalus → European Enlightenment Chain**

The historical process begins in al-Andalus and ends in the European Renaissance:

1. Jews, Muslims, and Christians living together in al-Andalus shared ideas and texts.
2. Islamic scholars translated and preserved Aristotle; Ibn Rushd wrote accessible commentaries.
3. Jewish scholars like Maimonides absorbed this and applied it to Jewish theology.
4. Christian scholars like Aquinas absorbed it from Maimonides and applied it to Christianity.
5. Making paper — a technology that came from China through Islamic civilization — enabled the printing press and the mass spread of these ideas.
6. The Renaissance and Scientific Revolution built on this accumulated foundation.

> [!exam-tip]
> This process — Islamic scholarship → European Renaissance — is one of the best examples of **unintended consequences** in Period 1. Islamic scholars preserving Greek texts did not intend to spark an intellectual revolution in a distant continent centuries later. Unintended consequences is a historical concept the AP exam rewards in FRQs.

## Essential Question Response

*In one to three paragraphs, explain how Islamic states arose and how major religious systems shaped society in the periods between c. 1200 and c. 1450.*

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> Islamic states arose through a combination of Turkic military expansion, religious legitimacy, and the collapse of the Abbasid Caliphate. When Central Asian Turkic peoples — including the Seljuk Turks, the Mamluks, and the founders of the Delhi Sultanate — converted to Islam and conquered new territories, they established states governed by Islamic law (Sharia) and legitimated by the ulama. The Mongols' destruction of Baghdad in 1258 ended the Abbasid Caliphate, but new Islamic states quickly filled the power vacuum because the cultural institutions of Dar al-Islam — Sharia, madrasas, and merchant networks — survived the political collapse.
>
> Islam shaped society in Dar al-Islam through both its legal framework and its missionary movements. Sharia created comparable legal systems across politically diverse Islamic states, governing everything from commerce to family life. The Five Pillars tied Muslims across the world into shared obligations. Sufism — emphasizing personal mystical experience over intellectual study — spread Islam far beyond the Arab heartland through missionaries who blended local traditions with Islamic practice, producing religious syncretism across Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. The dhimmi system shaped the lives of non-Muslims: tolerated but taxed, protected but legally subordinate.
>
> Perhaps most significantly for world history, Dar al-Islam's commitment to scholarship — expressed through the House of Wisdom, the madrasas, and the principle of "Go in quest of knowledge even unto China" — made the Islamic world the global center of intellectual life in Period 1. The knowledge transfers that flowed from al-Andalus laid the groundwork for the European Renaissance. In this way, Islamic civilization shaped not only the societies within Dar al-Islam but the trajectory of European history as well.
