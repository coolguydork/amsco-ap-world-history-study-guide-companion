# Timeline & Chronology — Topic 1.2: Dar al-Islam

Chronological anchor points for the Islamic world, c. 600–c. 1450. Dates before 1200 provide essential context for the AP period; dates within the period (c. 1200–c. 1450) are primary exam material.

---

## Pre-Period Context (600–1099)

| Date | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **632** | Death of the Prophet Muhammad | Triggers rapid Islamic expansion outward from Arabia |
| **661–750** | Umayyad Caliphate | Islam spreads across North Africa, Persia, and into Spain; Umayyad harshness toward non-Arabs sows resistance |
| **711** | Muslim forces invade Spain | Córdoba becomes capital of al-Andalus; seven centuries of Islamic rule in Spain begin |
| **732** | Battle of Tours (Battle of Poitiers) | Frankish forces stop Islamic advance into Western Europe; marks the limit of rapid westward expansion |
| **750** | Abbasid Caliphate begins | Replaces Umayyads; centered in Baghdad; launches the Islamic Golden Age |
| **c. 800s** | Height of the House of Wisdom | Baghdad is the world's greatest center of learning; scholars from across Afro-Eurasia gather |
| **1095–1099** | First Crusade | European Christians respond to Seljuk restrictions on pilgrims; capture Jerusalem in 1099 |

> [!tip]
> You don't need to memorize every pre-1200 date, but you need to *contextualize*. The Abbasid Golden Age was already centuries old when the AP period begins. What Topic 1.2 covers is the *decline and fragmentation* of what had been a mature, sophisticated civilization.

---

## AP Period (c. 1200–c. 1450): Core Dates

| Date | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **1099–1200s** | Crusades (multiple) | Series of European military campaigns; promoted cultural exchange between Europe and Dar al-Islam; ultimately failed to hold the Holy Land |
| **11th–12th c.** | Seljuk Turks dominate Middle East | Reduce Abbasid caliph to religious figurehead; take the title *sultan*; restrict Christian pilgrims → trigger Crusades |
| **1187** | Saladin retakes Jerusalem | Muslim recapture of the Holy City; symbol of Islamic resistance to Crusader incursion |
| **1201** | Birth of Nasir al-Din al-Tusi | Peak Islamic scholar: astronomy, trigonometry, mathematics; built world's most advanced observatory |
| **1250** | Mamluk Sultanate established in Egypt | Formerly enslaved Turkish soldiers seize power; prosper via cotton/sugar trade with Europe |
| **1258** | Mongols destroy Baghdad | Hulegu sacks Baghdad, kills the caliph, burns the House of Wisdom; end of the Abbasid Caliphate |
| **1260** | Battle of Ain Jalut | Mamluks defeat the Mongols in Egypt — the first major Mongol defeat; stops Mongol advance into North Africa |
| **c. 12th c.** | Ibn Rushd (Averroes) active in al-Andalus | Commentaries on Aristotle transmitted Greek classical knowledge to Jewish and Christian Europe |
| **c. 1135–1204** | Maimonides (al-Andalus) | Jewish philosopher; synthesized Aristotelian reason with biblical interpretation; influenced by Ibn Rushd |
| **1225–1274** | Thomas Aquinas (Europe) | Christian philosopher; influenced by Maimonides and Ibn Rushd; completes the Greek → Islamic → Jewish → Christian knowledge chain |
| **1332** | Birth of Ibn Khaldun | Founder of historiography and sociology; cyclical theory of civilizations; symbol of Islamic intellectual achievement after the Abbasid collapse |
| **c. 1400s** | Sufism spreads Islam to new frontiers | Sufi missionaries active in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia; adaptive, syncretic approach |
| **1460** | Birth of ʿĀʾisha al-Bāʿūniyya | Most prolific female Muslim writer before the 20th century; Sufi poet; evidence of women's intellectual life in Dar al-Islam |

## Causation Chain: Abbasid Decline

```
Trade routes shift northward (8th–11th c.)
        ↓
Baghdad loses trade revenue → infrastructure decays
        ↓
Seljuk Turks seize political power (11th c.) → caliph reduced to figurehead
        ↓
Crusaders destabilize region (1095–1200s)
        ↓
Mongols destroy Baghdad (1258) → Abbasid Caliphate ended
        ↓
Islamic world fragments → Mamluk, Delhi, later Ottoman/Safavid/Mughal states
```

> [!exam-tip]
> The 1258 destruction of Baghdad is the single most important date in Topic 1.2. It marks the end of one era and the beginning of another — but also demonstrates continuity, since Islamic civilization survived and new states arose. This is a perfect CCOT anchor.

---

## Causation Chain: Knowledge Transfer to Europe

```
Ancient Greek texts (Aristotle, etc.)
        ↓
Preserved in Byzantine Empire; translated into Arabic by Abbasid scholars
        ↓
House of Wisdom scholars add original work (al-Tusi, Ibn Khaldun, etc.)
        ↓
Knowledge flows to al-Andalus (Islamic Spain)
        ↓
Ibn Rushd (Averroes) writes commentaries on Aristotle (12th c.)
        ↓
Maimonides (Jewish, al-Andalus) synthesizes Aristotle with Jewish thought
        ↓
Thomas Aquinas (Christian, Europe) builds on Maimonides and Ibn Rushd
        ↓
European Renaissance and Scientific Revolution (15th–17th c.)
```

## Geographical Spread of Islam: Key Dates

| Region | Approximate Period | Key Agent |
|---|---|---|
| Arabia | 620s–632 | Muhammad's unification of Arabia |
| North Africa, Persia, Spain | 632–711 | Umayyad military campaigns |
| Central Asia, South Asia | 8th–11th c. | Military conquest, then Sufi missionaries |
| Sub-Saharan Africa (West Africa) | 8th–14th c. | Trans-Saharan merchants; later Sufis |
| Southeast Asia | 12th–15th c. | Indian Ocean merchants; Sufi missionaries |

> [!tip]
> Notice that **military conquest** spread Islam early, but **merchants and Sufi missionaries** spread it to frontiers like Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia. This distinction matters for AP essays about the methods of religious diffusion.
