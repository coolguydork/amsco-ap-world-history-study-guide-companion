# Topic 1.2 — Key Terms, Concepts & People

## Core Vocabulary

**Dar al-Islam**
"House of Islam" — the collective term for all lands under Muslim political or cultural influence. Even when politically fragmented, Dar al-Islam was united by shariah, Arabic, trade networks, and shared intellectual traditions.

**Abbasid Caliphate**
The major Islamic caliphate centered in Baghdad (750–1258). At its height, it was a center of world learning (House of Wisdom). Weakened by the Seljuk Turks, Crusaders, and ultimately destroyed by the Mongols in 1258.

**Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517)**
A state in Egypt founded by formerly enslaved soldiers (often Turkic) who seized power. Stopped the Mongol advance and facilitated trade between the Islamic world and Europe. Declined as European sea routes bypassed their territory.

**Seljuk Turks**
Central Asian Muslim group that conquered much of the Middle East in the 11th century. Reduced the Abbasid caliph to a religious figurehead, taking the political title of *sultan*. Their restriction of Christian pilgrims triggered the Crusades.

**Sultan**
Political ruler in the Islamic world, as distinct from the caliph (religious authority). The Seljuks separated political and religious leadership by taking the title of sultan.

**Crusades (1095–1200s)**
European Christian military campaigns to retake the Holy Land from Muslim control. Partly triggered by Seljuk restrictions on pilgrims. The First Crusade captured Jerusalem (1099); Saladin retook it (1187). The Fourth Crusade never reached the Holy Land — it sacked Constantinople instead. Promoted cultural exchange between Europe and the Middle East.

**House of Wisdom**
The great center of learning in Baghdad — a library, translation bureau, and research institution. Symbol of the Islamic Golden Age. Scholars here translated Greek texts, studied Indian mathematics, and made original contributions to science, medicine, and philosophy.

**Shariah**
Islamic law derived from the Quran and the Hadith (sayings of Muhammad). Created a shared legal framework across Dar al-Islam, contributing to cultural continuity even amid political fragmentation.

**Sufism**
A mystical movement within Islam emphasizing direct inner experience of God over intellectual study. Sufis were key missionaries who spread Islam by adapting to local cultures. Compare to the Bhakti Movement in Hinduism (Topic 1.3).

**Hijab**
The practice of modest dress (or a specific head covering) observed by Muslim women. Existed in Central Asian and Byzantine culture before Muhammad and solidified under Islam.

**Al-Andalus**
The Islamic state in Spain (711–1492), with Córdoba as its capital. A center of toleration and learning — Muslims, Christians, and Jews coexisted. Home to some of the world's most important intellectual transfers to Europe.

**Battle of Tours (732)**
Frankish forces stopped the Islamic military advance into Western Europe. Marked the limit of rapid Islamic expansion west of Spain. Most of Europe remained Christian.

**Dhows**
Ships used in Indian Ocean trade, with long, thin hulls ideal for cargo. First developed in India or China; became the workhorse of Islamic maritime commerce.

**Jizya**
A tax imposed on non-Muslim subjects in Islamic-ruled territories. Under the Delhi Sultanate in India, this applied to Hindus and was a source of resentment. (Discussed more in Topic 1.3.)

## Key People

**Muhammad (d. 632 CE)**
Prophet of Islam. A former merchant — this shaped Islam's positive view of commerce. His policies elevated the status of women relative to pre-Islamic Arabian norms: he required dowries go to the bride, forbade female infanticide, and respected his wife Khadijah's business acumen.

**Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274)**
The most celebrated Islamic scholar of his era. Contributed to astronomy (built the world's most advanced observatory), trigonometry (made it a separate subject), law, ethics, and medicine. A prime example of Islamic Golden Age innovation.

**Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)**
Islamic historian and sociologist. Considered the founder of historiography (the study of how history is written) and sociology. Famous for his cyclical theory of civilizational rise and decline.

**ʿĀʾisha al-Bāʿūniyya (1460–1507)**
The most prolific female Muslim writer before the 20th century. A Sufi poet whose work reflects both deep learning and mystical spiritual experience. Her existence challenges assumptions about the invisibility of women in Islamic intellectual history.

**Ibn Rushd / Averroes (12th century)**
Philosopher from al-Andalus (Spain). Wrote influential commentaries on Aristotle that were studied by Jewish and Christian scholars and helped transmit classical Greek knowledge to medieval Europe.

**Maimonides (c. 1135–c. 1204)**
Jewish philosopher from al-Andalus, influenced by Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle. Synthesized Aristotelian reasoning with biblical interpretation. Influenced Thomas Aquinas — a perfect example of cross-cultural intellectual transfer.

**Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)**
Christian philosopher who was influenced by both Ibn Rushd and Maimonides. His work shows the chain: Indian knowledge → Islamic scholars → al-Andalus → Christian Europe.

**Saladin**
Muslim military leader who retook Jerusalem from Crusaders in 1187. Symbol of Islamic resistance to European Crusader incursion.
