# People, States & Movements — Topic 1.2: Developments in Dar al-Islam
*AMSCO pp. 15–22*

---

## Key People

### Ibn Battuta (1304–1368/69)
The greatest Muslim traveler of the medieval world. Over nearly three decades (1325–1354), Ibn Battuta journeyed approximately 75,000 miles across Dar al-Islam — from Morocco to Mali, the Middle East, Central Asia, South and Southeast Asia, and China. His accounts (*Rihla*) are a primary source for understanding the breadth and cultural unity of the Islamic world in Period 1. His travels demonstrate how a shared religion, language, and merchant culture allowed a Muslim to cross vast distances and find familiar institutions everywhere.

> [!exam-tip]
> Ibn Battuta is a high-frequency AP source. If you see a passage describing the cultural unity of Dar al-Islam from a traveler's perspective — or a source describing different Islamic societies — it is almost certainly Ibn Battuta.

### Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274)
One of the most celebrated Islamic scholars. Contributed to astronomy, law, logic, ethics, mathematics, philosophy, and medicine. Built the most advanced observatory in the world, which produced the most accurate astronomical charts of the era. His work laid the groundwork for trigonometry as a separate mathematical subject. Represents the creative output of the Islamic Golden Age beyond mere preservation of earlier knowledge (AMSCO p. 17).

### Ibn Rushd / Averroes (12th century)
Islamic philosopher and jurist from al-Andalus (Spain). Wrote influential commentaries on Aristotle that were the most important bridge between Greek philosophy and medieval Europe. His works shaped the thinking of the Jewish philosopher Maimonides and, through him, the Christian philosopher Thomas Aquinas. **Key causal role**: his commentaries made Aristotle's logic accessible to European scholars and contributed to the intellectual foundations of the Renaissance (AMSCO p. 19).

### Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406)
Islamic scholar from North Africa. Widely considered the founder of **historiography** (the study of historical methods) and **sociology** (the study of society and social institutions). His *Muqaddimah* introduced concepts of cyclical history and the sociological forces driving the rise and fall of civilizations (AMSCO p. 17).

### Al-Ghazali (1058–1111)
Islamic theologian and philosopher who reconciled [[Sufism]] with orthodox Sunni thought, making Sufism acceptable to mainstream Islamic society and enabling its rapid spread. His synthesis is one of the reasons Sufism became a mainstream force rather than a fringe movement.

### 'A'ishah al-Ba'uniyyah (1460–1507)
Sufi poet and mystic, likely the most prolific female Muslim writer before the 20th century. Her best-known work — a long poem honoring Muhammad — demonstrates her mastery of Islamic literary tradition and her engagement with the Sufi path toward mystical experience. Her life and work highlight that educated women could achieve intellectual renown within Islamic society, while also illustrating the gendered restrictions of the period (AMSCO p. 17).

### Saladin (Salah ad-Din, 1137–1193)
Kurdish Muslim sultan who unified Egypt and Syria and famously recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187 — a moment of enormous symbolic importance in the Islamic world. Saladin's victory reversed what the Crusaders had achieved and is celebrated as evidence of Islamic political and military resilience in the face of European pressure.

### Maimonides (c. 1135–c. 1204)
Jewish philosopher who lived in al-Andalus and later Egypt. Influenced by [[Ibn Rushd (Averroes)|Ibn Rushd's]] commentaries on Aristotle, he developed a synthesis of Aristotelian reasoning and biblical (Torah) interpretation. His work then influenced the Christian philosopher Thomas Aquinas — completing the Muslim → Jewish → Christian intellectual chain that originated in al-Andalus (AMSCO p. 19).

### Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)
Christian philosopher and theologian. Though not Islamic, he represents the end point of the knowledge-transfer chain that began in Dar al-Islam. His use of Aristotelian logic (learned through Averroes and Maimonides) to systematize Christian theology is one of the most direct effects of Islamic intellectual innovation on European civilization.

### Mahmud of Ghazni (971–1030)
Afghan sultan who conducted raids into northern India in the early 11th century, spreading Islam into the subcontinent. Predecessor to the [[Delhi Sultanate]] — a key mechanism for the Islamization of South Asia.

## Key States & Empires

### Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258)
The dominant Islamic empire from 750 to 1258, centered in Baghdad. Built by Arab and Persian leadership, it oversaw the Islamic Golden Age and the [[House of Wisdom]]. Served as the hub of Afro-Eurasian trade. Gradually weakened by Seljuk Turks (political), Crusaders (western pressure), shifting trade routes (economic), and finally destroyed by the Mongols in 1258. Its fall is one of the pivotal events of Period 1.

> [!warning]
> The Abbasid Caliphate didn't collapse all at once. It was progressively hollowed out — Seljuks took real power (11th century), the Crusades added pressure (11th–12th centuries), Baghdad's trade position eroded (12th century) — before the Mongols delivered the final blow in 1258.

### Mamluk Sultanate (1250–1517)
Established by enslaved Turkic soldiers who seized control of Egypt. Prospered through cotton and sugar trade between the Islamic world and Europe. Critically important for two reasons: (1) they stopped the Mongol advance at the Battle of Ain Jalut (1260), preventing total Islamic collapse; (2) they declined when Portuguese sea routes bypassed their overland trade networks in the late 1400s. Connects forward to Unit 2 (trade networks) and Topic 4.2 (European exploration).

### Seljuk Turks (1037–1194)
Central Asian Turkic people who conquered much of the Middle East, reduced the Abbasid caliph to a figurehead, and triggered the Crusades by restricting Christian access to Jerusalem. Represent the first major wave of Turkic power in the Islamic world — precursor to the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires of Period 2.

### Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526)
Islamic state in northern India established after Mahmud of Ghazni's raids. Governed a Hindu-majority population under an Islamic ruling class. The first major Islamic state in South Asia and a predecessor to the Mughal Empire. The Delhi Sultanate illustrates the Turkic pattern: its ruling elite were Turkic Central Asians, not Arabs. Links to [[Topic 1.3]] (South and Southeast Asia) and forward to Topic 3.1 (Mughal Empire).

### Fatimid Caliphate (909–1171)
A Shia Islamic dynasty that ruled Egypt and North Africa. A rival to the Sunni Abbasids and an important regional power before being displaced by Saladin's Ayyubid dynasty. Its presence illustrates the Sunni-Shia split within Islam.

### al-Andalus (711–1492)
The Islamic state in Spain, centered on Córdoba. Lasted nearly eight centuries, making it one of the longest-lived Islamic political entities. Distinguished by its climate of cultural coexistence (Muslims, Jews, Christians) and intellectual productivity — Córdoba's library was the largest in the world. The eventual Christian reconquest (**Reconquista**) of al-Andalus, completing in 1492, connects forward to Topic 4.2 (Spanish exploration) and Topic 4.4 (Spanish Empire).

## Key Movements

### Sufism
A mystical movement within Islam emphasizing personal experience of God through introspection, prayer, poetry, and music. Made orthodox by [[Al-Ghazali]] in the 11th century. Spread Islam far beyond the Arab heartland through the work of **Sufi missionaries** who adapted to local cultures. Represents the most important mechanism of Islamic expansion in Period 1.

### The Crusades (1095–1291)
European Christian military expeditions to recapture the Holy Land from Muslim control, triggered by Seljuk restrictions on pilgrimages to Jerusalem. From the Islamic perspective, the Crusades were a significant but ultimately unsuccessful external challenge. The Crusades also generated intellectual exchange: European contact with Islamic learning in the Holy Land and Spain contributed to the later Renaissance. Connects to [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|Topic 1.1]] indirectly (both regions benefited from Silk Roads disrupted by conflict) and directly to Topic 1.6 (European perspective).

### Islamic Missionary Activity
Beyond Sufi missionaries, Islamic merchants regularly served as missionaries. Trade and religion reinforced each other: a Muslim merchant who conducted fair dealings and gave to charity embodied the Five Pillars and demonstrated Islam's virtues to non-Muslim trading partners. This pattern meant Islam spread wherever Islamic merchants traded — the Indian Ocean, the Silk Roads, and sub-Saharan Africa.
