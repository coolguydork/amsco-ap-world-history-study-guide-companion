# Unit 1 Overview — The Global Tapestry
*c. 1200–c. 1450 | AP Period 1*

> *"The world is divided into men who have wit and no religion and men who have religion and no wit."*
> — Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna), 980–1037

---

## What Is Unit 1 About?

Unit 1 is a snapshot of the world **before the great connections**. Before the Mongol trade routes fully opened, before European explorers crossed oceans, before the Black Death reshuffled civilizations — this is what each region looked like on its own terms, c. 1200–1450.

The AP exam calls this unit "The Global Tapestry" because it asks you to see each civilization as one thread: distinct in texture and color, but part of a larger pattern. The central question of the unit is one of **comparison**: How did different societies build states, organize social hierarchies, and use religion and trade — and what did they share despite having little contact with each other?

Unit 2 will then ask: what happened when those threads began to interweave?

## AP Themes in Unit 1

Unit 1 focuses on three major AP themes. Every topic threads through at least two of them.

**Governance (GOV)** is the dominant theme. The central question is: how do states build and legitimize power? Answers varied — Confucian bureaucracy in China, Islamic law and the caliphate in Dar al-Islam, Hindu-Buddhist divine kingship in South/Southeast Asia, tribute systems in the Americas, feudalism in Europe. But the underlying pattern is the same: all states needed a legitimizing ideology, a mechanism for extracting resources, and a way to manage diverse populations.

**Cultural Developments and Interactions (CDI)** explains how religion, knowledge, and artistic traditions spread across regions. Islam spread via merchants and Sufi missionaries. Buddhism spread via monks and the Silk Roads. Neo-Confucianism spread from China to Korea and Japan. Christianity spread via the Catholic Church and the Crusades. In all cases, cultural diffusion followed trade routes and military contacts — a preview of the Unit 2 story.

**Social Interactions and Organization (SIO)** anchors the unit's comparison of social hierarchies. The AP exam frequently asks about the status of merchants (high in Dar al-Islam, low in Confucian China) and the status of women (increasingly restricted in Song China via foot binding; more legally protected in Dar al-Islam than in Europe; opportunities for educated women in Buddhist and Christian religious communities).

## The Four Types of State-Building

AMSCO's Topic 1.7 (pp. 65–68) offers the most useful framework for any LEQ on Unit 1 state-building. Every major state in Period 1 fits one of four patterns:

| Pattern | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergence of New States** | Arise in land once controlled by another empire | Mamluk Sultanate (former Abbasid territory); Seljuk Turks (former Abbasid territory); Delhi Sultanate (former Gupta territory) |
| **Revival of Former Empires** | New leadership continues or rebuilds a previous empire with innovations | Song Dynasty (Han Dynasty legacy); Mali Empire (Ghana Kingdom legacy); Holy Roman Empire (Roman Empire legacy) |
| **Synthesis of Different Traditions** | A state adapts foreign ideas to local conditions | Japan (Chinese + indigenous); Delhi Sultanate (Islamic + Hindu); Neo-Confucianism (Confucian + Buddhist) |
| **Expansion in Scope** | An existing state expands through conquest, trade, or tribute | Aztec Empire (Mesoamerica); Inca Empire (South America); East African city-states |

> [!exam-tip]
> This four-category framework is excellent LEQ evidence. If an AP prompt asks you to "evaluate the extent to which state-building processes in the period c. 1200–1450 were similar," you can argue that nearly all states fit one of these four patterns — which is a strong similarity argument — while also noting that the mechanisms (religion, trade, conquest, bureaucracy) varied significantly by region.

## Religion as a State-Building Tool

One of the most consistent patterns across Unit 1 is that **rulers used religion to legitimize authority and unify diverse populations**. This applies everywhere, though the specific religion and mechanism differed:

In **Dar al-Islam**, Sharia law and the shared language of Arabic held a politically fragmented world together. Islamic rulers from West Africa to Southeast Asia drew legitimacy from Islam regardless of their ethnicity (Arab, Turkic, Berber). The [[Concept Notes/Sufism\|Sufis]] carried Islam further than any army could, adapting to local cultures and creating a global religious community.

In **East Asia**, Confucianism — especially [[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism\|Neo-Confucianism]] — gave the Song Dynasty's bureaucracy its intellectual foundation and moral authority. It spread to Korea and Japan, where rulers used it to justify hierarchy and consolidate political control.

In **South and Southeast Asia**, Hindu-Buddhist divine kingship gave rulers sacred authority. The Khmer at Angkor Wat and the Vijayanagar Empire both built their legitimacy on Hindu cosmology. Where Islam entered South Asia via the Delhi Sultanate, it had to negotiate with this deep Hindu cultural foundation — producing the Synthesis pattern above.

In **the Americas**, religion was inseparable from the state. Aztec rulers derived authority from their role as intermediaries with the gods, including the practice of human sacrifice to sustain the sun. Inca rulers were considered divine sons of the sun god Inti.

In **Europe**, the Catholic Church was simultaneously a partner and a rival to secular state-building. It provided the only continent-wide administrative structure during the weakest centuries of European governance, but as stronger kingdoms emerged after 1200, tensions between popes and monarchs increased.

> [!warning]
> Don't flatten all religious state-building into one model. The AP exam distinguishes between how different religions legitimized power. Islam used law (Sharia) as a unifying mechanism; Confucianism used meritocratic bureaucracy; Hindu-Buddhist states used cosmological symbolism; the Catholic Church used sacramental authority and canon law.

## The Role of Nomadic Peoples

[[Concept Notes/Mongols\|Mongol]] expansion is the most dramatic development in Unit 1 — though it connects primarily to Unit 2 [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Mongol Empire Overview]]. The key Unit 1 point is that the **Mongols accelerated the end of the Abbasid Caliphate** (Baghdad fell 1258) and temporarily reorganized political power across Central Asia, Persia, and China. The [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica\|Pax Mongolica]] that followed created the trade conditions Unit 2 describes.

The **Turkic peoples** played an equally large long-term role. Unlike the Mongols, who built one coordinated empire, different Turkic groups built separate empires: the Seljuks controlled the Middle East and reduced the Abbasid caliph to a figurehead; the Ottomans rose in the eastern Mediterranean; the Delhi Sultanate's rulers were Turkic Central Asians. This Turkic pattern — converting to Islam and then conquering Islamic territory — is one of the key mechanisms of Islamic political change in Period 1.

By c. 1450, the era of nomadic dominance was ending. Organized merchant networks and gunpowder armies (pioneered by China, spreading westward) made nomadic cavalry less decisive.

## Social Hierarchies: A Cross-Regional Comparison

| Feature | East Asia | Dar al-Islam | South Asia | Americas | Africa | Europe |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Merchant status** | Low (bottom of Confucian hierarchy) | High (Prophet Muhammad was a merchant) | Vaisya caste — middle rank | Variable | Variable | Low (church opposed lending at interest) |
| **Women's status** | Declining (foot binding in Song China) | More legal rights than European women; veil/harem restrictions | Varied by caste and region | Variable; some matrilineal societies | Variable; matrilineal in some West African societies | Very limited; convent life offered alternatives |
| **Slavery** | Limited | Mamluks — enslaved soldiers could rise to power | Limited | Aztec sacrifice and captives | Trans-Saharan slave trade | Serfdom (not chattel slavery) |
| **Basis of hierarchy** | Confucian scholar-gentry at top | Religious scholars (Ulama) influential | Caste system | Military/religious elite | Lineage and trade wealth | Feudal lords and clergy |

> [!exam-tip]
> The merchant status comparison (Dar al-Islam high vs. Confucian China low) is one of the most tested comparisons on the AP exam. Know it cold, and know **why**: Islamic religious sanction of commerce vs. Confucian suspicion of profit-seeking.

## Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

### What Changed, c. 1200–c. 1450?

The biggest structural change of the period is the **decline of the Abbasid Caliphate** and the rise of multiple successor states — Mamluks, Seljuks, Delhi Sultanate, Ottoman Turks. Political fragmentation replaced the relative unity of the early Abbasid period.

In **East Asia**, the Mongol conquest ended the Song Dynasty (1279), replacing it with the Yuan Dynasty — a foreign Mongol regime. The Ming restoration (1368) represented continuity of Chinese culture after a century of Mongol rule.

In **Europe**, the gradual decline of feudalism and the emergence of stronger centralized kingdoms in France, England, and Spain represents the key change — setting up the state-building that enables European exploration in Unit 4.

### What Stayed the Same?

Across all regions, several continuities hold throughout the period: patriarchal social organization was universal; religion continued to legitimate political authority everywhere; trade networks (Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan) continued to function despite political disruptions; and the basic agricultural foundations of all these civilizations persisted.

## How Unit 1 Connects to Unit 2

Unit 1 describes regions **in relative isolation**. Unit 2 describes what happens when they become **connected**. The mechanisms of connection are:

- The [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads\|Silk Roads]] [[Unit-2.1/Silk Roads Overview|Silk Roads Overview]] — overland Eurasian trade, expanded under the Pax Mongolica
- The [[Concept Notes/Mongols\|Mongol Empire]] [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Mongol Empire Overview]] — the political framework that made cross-Eurasian movement possible
- The [[Concept Notes/Indian Ocean Trade Network\|Indian Ocean Trade Network]] [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Overview|Indian Ocean Overview]] — maritime connections between East Africa, Arabia, South Asia, and China
- The [[Concept Notes/Trans-Saharan Trade\|Trans-Saharan Trade]] [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview|Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview]] — connections between North and sub-Saharan Africa

Every civilization you studied in Unit 1 will appear again in Unit 2, transformed by contact with the others.

## Exam Strategy for Unit 1

**LEQ prompts commonly test:**

Comparison LEQs asking you to compare state-building, social hierarchies, or the role of religion across two or more Unit 1 regions. The four state-building types (above) and the comparison tables in this note are your primary frameworks.

CCOT LEQs asking you to trace change and continuity in governance or cultural practices across the period c. 1200–c. 1450.

**Best evidence pairings for essays:**

To argue that **religion was the primary state-building tool**: Song Dynasty (Confucianism + civil service), Mali Empire (Islam + Mansa Musa's hajj), Delhi Sultanate (Islam governing a Hindu majority), Aztec Empire (religion + sacrifice).

To argue that **trade drove state power**: Mali Empire (gold-salt trade), Song China (manufacturing and commerce), Mamluk Sultanate (cotton-sugar trade with Europe), Indian Ocean city-states.

To argue that **nomadic peoples disrupted and transformed state structures**: Mongols destroy Baghdad (1258), Seljuk Turks hollow out the Abbasid Caliphate, Turkic sultans replace Arab caliphs throughout Dar al-Islam.

> [!exam-tip]
> Unit 1 is the most comparison-heavy unit on the AP exam. Every region you study is potential evidence for an LEQ in **any** unit — the AP exam will ask you to reach back to Period 1 as a point of comparison even on prompts focused on Period 2 or 3. Learn these civilizations, not just as a list, but as a set of **contrasting models**.
