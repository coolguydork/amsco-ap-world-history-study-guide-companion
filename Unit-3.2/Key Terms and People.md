# Topic 3.2 — Key Terms and People

---

## Governance Terms by Region

### Europe

**divine right of kings** — The belief that a monarch's authority came directly from God, placing the ruler above earthly law. Claimed by James I of England; fully realized by Louis XIV of France. Challenged by Parliament in England (English Bill of Rights, 1689).

**justices of the peace** — English officials selected from the landed gentry to maintain peace in the counties and carry out the monarch's laws. Established under the Tudors (1485–1603). Became central to Tudor governance; held many House of Commons seats.

**English Bill of Rights (1689)** — Signed by William and Mary; guaranteed civil liberties, required Parliament's consent for taxation and army, and placed legal limits on the crown. England's key step toward constitutional monarchy.

**absolute monarchy** — Government in which one ruler holds all authority — legislative, judicial, executive. Exemplified by Louis XIV: "L'état, c'est moi" (I am the state).

**Cardinal Richelieu** — Minister to Louis XIII (1610–1643); architect of French absolutism; created the intendant system.

**intendants** — Royal officials in France sent to the provinces to carry out central government orders and oversee tax collection. Sometimes called "tax farmers." A tool of absolutism under Louis XIV.

**Louis XIV** — "The Sun King" (ruled 1643–1715); epitome of French absolutism; built the Palace of Versailles; expelled Huguenots in 1685; expanded France's borders.

**Versailles** — Palace of Louis XIV; more than 700 rooms, 60 staircases, 30,000+ acres of gardens; political tool to keep nobles close and dependent on the king.

**tax farmers** — Private officials or local citizens who collected taxes on behalf of the state, keeping a portion as their fee. Used in France (intendants), the Ottoman Empire, and Ming China.

### Russia

**boyars** — The Russian noble landowning class; at the top of Moscow's social hierarchy; frequent rivals of the tsars.

**serfdom** — The system in which peasants were bound to land owned by nobles, with little personal freedom; transferred with land when ownership changed. Expanded under Peter the Great's economic pressures.

**oprichnina** — Ivan IV's black-clad paramilitary force, loyal to the tsar; drawn from lower bureaucrats and merchants to avoid boyar loyalty; prefigured later Russian secret police.

**Romanov Dynasty** — Russian dynasty that took power in 1613 after turmoil following Ivan IV's death; ruled until 1917; produced Peter the Great.

**Peter I / Peter the Great** (ruled 1682–1725) — Modernized Russia along Western European lines; created provinces with salaried officials; founded St. Petersburg; abolished the Orthodox patriarch; introduced the head tax; built state industries.

**Holy Synod** — Replaced the Orthodox patriarch under Peter the Great; composed of clergymen overseen by a secular official answering to the tsar. Incorporated the Church into the state apparatus.

**St. Petersburg** — New Russian capital founded by Peter the Great on the Baltic Sea; designed in European architectural style; politically strategic location to oversee boyars required to serve the state.

---

### Ottoman Empire

**[[Concept Notes/Devshirme\|devshirme]]** — System by which Christian boys ages 8–20 from conquered Balkan territories were forcibly recruited into the Ottoman military and civil service; converted to Islam; trained to be loyal to the sultan. The basis of Ottoman bureaucracy.

**[[Concept Notes/Janissaries\|Janissaries]]** — Elite Ottoman military forces created through the devshirme system; technically "slaves of the state" but offered significant upward mobility; fiercely loyal to the sultan.

**tax farming (Ottoman)** — Ottoman tax collection method: private individuals paid a fixed sum to the government for the right to collect taxes in an area, keeping the excess. Led to corruption.

---

### China and Japan

**civil service exam (Ming/Qing)** — Examination system used to select government officials based on knowledge of Confucian classics; restored by the Ming after Mongol rule; key mechanism of bureaucratic governance.

**shogun** — Military ruler of Japan, governing in the emperor's name from the 12th–15th centuries; power fragmented among rival daimyo until Tokugawa unification.

**daimyo** — Japanese regional landholding lords, each with his own samurai army; source of Japan's fragmentation before Tokugawa unification.

**samurai** — Japanese warriors; paid in rice, later gold; loyal to their daimyo; central to Japan's feudal system.

**han** — One of 250 Japanese territories, each controlled by a daimyo under the Tokugawa shogunate.

**Tokugawa Ieyasu** (ruled 1600–1616) — The daimyo who unified Japan; declared shogun in 1603; moved capital to Edo (Tokyo); established the Tokugawa shogunate.

**Tokugawa shogunate** — The military government of Japan (1603–1868); required daimyo to maintain residences in Edo and leave families as hostages; created the "Period of Great Peace."

**Period of Great Peace** — Tokugawa era of stability in Japan; achieved through careful control of daimyo mobility and the hostage system.

---

### Mughal India

**Akbar** (ruled 1556–1605) — Greatest Mughal ruler; efficient government; religious tolerance; all subjects could appeal to him for final judgment; encouraged learning, art, architecture.

**zamindars** — Paid Mughal government officials (later given land grants) responsible for specific duties — taxation, construction, water supply. Over time, became corrupt by keeping excess taxes.

**Shah Jahan** (ruled 1628–1658) — Built the Taj Mahal; beautified Delhi; weakened treasury; succeeded by Aurangzeb.

**Taj Mahal** — Mausoleum built by Shah Jahan for his wife; the defining symbol of Mughal architectural achievement; combines Islamic calligraphy/geometry with local Indian artistic traditions.

---

### Songhai

**Askia the Great** (came to power 1493) — Ruler of Songhai; promoted Islam; made elaborate pilgrimage to Mecca; made Islam the official state religion; ran an efficient bureaucracy; largest kingdom in West Africa of its day.

## Key Comparison: Administrative Tools

| Method | Empire(s) | How It Worked |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| **Civil service exam** | Ming/Qing China | Merit-based official selection from scholar-gentry |
| **Devshirme** | Ottoman | Forced Christian boys converted to Islam; trained as loyal bureaucrats/soldiers |
| **Intendants** | France | Crown-appointed provincial officials overseeing tax collection |
| **Justices of the peace** | England | Gentry-selected local officials; legitimized crown through Parliament |
| **Zamindars** | Mughal India | Tax officials initially salaried, later given land grants |
| **Hostage system** | Japan (Tokugawa) | Daimyo families kept in capital; reduced independent power |
| **Oprichnina** | Russia (Ivan IV) | Personal paramilitary force loyal to the tsar above all |
