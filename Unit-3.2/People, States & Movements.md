*AMSCO pp. 155–166 | AP Themes: GOV · SIO · ECN*

## Key Rulers

**Louis XIV** *(r. 1643–1715, France)*
The "Sun King" — the AP exam's defining example of absolute monarchy. Louis XIV declared "L'état, c'est moi" ("I am the state"), combining lawmaking, justice, and executive power in himself. His key administrative tool was the system of **intendants** inherited from Cardinal Richelieu, which brought provincial France under direct royal control. His palace at **Versailles** served as both propaganda and a political trap for the nobility. His refusal to share power eventually weakened France, and his revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685) drove skilled Huguenots out of the country. *AMSCO p. 156*

**Akbar the Great** *(r. 1556–1605, Mughal Empire)*
The greatest administrator among the Mughal emperors. Akbar expanded the empire southward and westward while simultaneously building one of the most efficiently governed states of the era. He abolished the **jizya** (tax on non-Muslims), employed Hindus in high government positions, instituted the **mansabdar system** of ranked officials, and allowed his subjects the right of final appeal to him personally. Akbar's religious tolerance — unusual for any ruler of the era — was a conscious strategy to govern a predominantly Hindu population while maintaining Mughal (Muslim) authority. *AMSCO pp. 159–160*

**Shah Jahan** *(r. 1628–1658, Mughal Empire)*
Akbar's grandson, remembered primarily for monumental architecture: the **Taj Mahal** (tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal) and the **Red Fort** in Delhi. The Mughal Empire was prosperous under Shah Jahan, but his expensive building projects strained imperial finances. His reign illustrates how architectural legitimation could be both politically effective and economically costly. *AMSCO p. 161*

**Emperor Kangxi** *(r. 1661–1722, Qing Dynasty, China)*
Kangxi ruled for 61 years — one of the longest reigns in Chinese history — and presided over a Qing golden age. A Manchu by ethnicity, Kangxi mastered Confucian philosophy and governed through the traditional Chinese bureaucracy and civil service examination, giving him legitimacy among the Han majority. He opened ports to foreign trade, extended Qing control over Tibet, and supported both Western science and Chinese arts. His approach — Manchu ruler, Chinese governance methods — was the template for Qing success. *AMSCO pp. 145, 156*

**Peter the Great (Peter I)** *(r. 1682–1725, Russia)*
The most transformative ruler in Russian history. Peter forcibly modernized Russia along Western European lines: he built the navy, reorganized the government into salaried provincial administration, subordinated the Orthodox Church to the tsar's authority, required the boyar nobility to shave their beards and adopt Western dress, and built **St. Petersburg** as a new, European-style capital. Peter's modernization deepened serfdom (his head tax and forced labor in state industries fell hardest on peasants) while strengthening the state. *AMSCO pp. 158, 160*

**Ivan IV ("Ivan the Terrible")** *(r. 1547–1584, Russia)*
The first ruler to formally take the title of "tsar." Ivan expanded Russia east by conquering Tatar khanates (Topic 3.1), but his administration is defined by his brutal suppression of the **boyars**. He confiscated their lands, forced rivals to relocate to Moscow, and created the **oprichnina** — a black-clad paramilitary that terrorized his enemies. His reign combined impressive expansion with savage internal repression. *AMSCO p. 157*

**Suleiman the Magnificent** *(r. 1520–1566, Ottoman Empire)*
Called "the Lawgiver" by his own people (and "the Magnificent" by Europeans), Suleiman codified Ottoman law into a unified legal system, bringing order to the empire's diverse territories. He also commissioned major architectural works, including the **Suleymaniye Mosque** in Istanbul. His reign represents the Ottoman Empire at its administrative and military peak. *AMSCO p. 162*

**Shah Abbas I** *(r. 1588–1629, Safavid Persia)*
The greatest Safavid ruler, who modernized the Safavid military using European mercenaries and weapons, moved the capital to **Isfahan**, and made it one of the most beautiful cities in the world — a direct use of monumental architecture for political legitimacy. The Mosque of Isfahan remains his most visible legacy.

**Askia the Great (Askia Mohammad I)** *(r. 1493–1528, Songhai Empire)*
Came to power by overthrowing Sunni Ali, whom he accused of being insufficiently Islamic. Made an elaborate pilgrimage to Mecca (like Mansa Musa of Mali before him), promoted Islam throughout Songhai, and made it the empire's official religion. Simultaneously built an efficient administrative bureaucracy. His reign shows how religious legitimacy and bureaucratic governance could reinforce each other. *AMSCO p. 160*

**Cardinal Richelieu** *(1585–1642, France)*
Chief minister to Louis XIII (r. 1610–1643). Richelieu developed the **intendant system** and pushed French governance toward absolute monarchy, setting the stage for Louis XIV. His ideas — that the state, not the church or nobility, should be the ultimate power — were foundational to French absolutism. *AMSCO p. 156*

## Japanese Unifiers

Three daimyo completed Japan's unification over roughly 30 years:

**Oda Nobunaga** *(1534–1582)*
Armed with muskets purchased from Portuguese traders, Nobunaga captured Kyoto in 1568 and forced surrounding daimyo to submit. He had unified about one-third of Japan when he was assassinated in 1582. His genius was recognizing that gunpowder weapons could defeat the traditional samurai cavalry that had defined Japanese warfare.

**Toyotomi Hideyoshi** *(1537–1598)*
Nobunaga's successor, who completed Japan's territorial consolidation. Hideyoshi conducted nationwide **sword hunts** (disarming the peasantry) and a census to fix social hierarchies — preventing peasants from becoming samurai and samurai from becoming farmers. He died before completing his ambitions to conquer Korea and China.

**Tokugawa Ieyasu** *(r. 1600–1616)*
Defeated rival daimyo at the **Battle of Sekigahara (1600)** and was appointed shogun in 1603, establishing the Tokugawa shogunate. The Edo period (1603–1868) that followed is sometimes called the "Period of Great Peace" — over 250 years of domestic stability achieved through the sankin-kōtai hostage system and strict social control. *AMSCO p. 159*

> [!exam-tip]
> The three Japanese unifiers follow a famous proverb: "Nobunaga pounded the rice, Hideyoshi baked the cake, and Tokugawa ate it." Use this to remember the sequence and roles: Nobunaga began the military unification, Hideyoshi completed and consolidated it socially, and Tokugawa institutionalized it politically.

## Key States

**Ottoman Empire** — The largest and most durable of the Islamic [[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|gunpowder empires]]. Governed through the devshirme system, the millet system for religious minorities, and tax farming. Istanbul (formerly Constantinople) remained one of the world's great cities — a center of trade, arts, and Islamic learning. *AMSCO pp. 158–159, 161–162*

**Mughal Empire** — Ruled Muslim rulers over a Hindu-majority population in South Asia. Administrative success depended on the mansabdar/zamindar bureaucracy and, under Akbar, on religious tolerance. Mughal India was one of the wealthiest states in the world in 1700, producing roughly 25% of global GDP. *AMSCO pp. 159–161*

**Qing Dynasty** — Manchu rulers of China who, paradoxically, maintained Han Chinese administrative traditions (the civil service exam) while preserving Manchu ethnic identity. The Qing faced the challenge every conquest dynasty faces: how to govern a population that vastly outnumbers the rulers. *AMSCO pp. 156, 159*

**Tokugawa Japan** — A centralized feudal state in which the shogun nominally ruled in the emperor's name. The daimyo retained local power but were neutralized by the sankin-kōtai system. Japan's relative isolation during this period — limiting foreign trade to Chinese and Dutch merchants at Nagasaki — preserved internal stability but limited technological adoption. *AMSCO p. 159*

**Russian Tsardom** — Under the Romanov dynasty, Russia was a vast, autocratic state in which the tsar held supreme power over church, nobility, and peasants. Peter the Great's reforms began Russia's long modernization process, but the social cost fell on the peasant serfs who bore the burden of taxation and forced labor. *AMSCO pp. 157–158, 160*

**Songhai Empire** — The largest empire in West African history at its peak. Askia the Great's bureaucratic administration and promotion of Islam made Songhai a regional power and a center of Islamic scholarship (Timbuktu). *AMSCO p. 160*

## Comparative Summary

| Ruler | Empire | Key Method | Key Example |
|-------|--------|------------|-------------|
| Louis XIV | France | Divine right + bureaucracy | Intendants; Versailles |
| Akbar | Mughal India | Religious tolerance + merit bureaucracy | Abolished jizya; zamindars |
| Kangxi | Qing China | Confucian legitimacy + civil service | Exam system; Han officials |
| Peter the Great | Russia | Westernization + salaried administration | Provincial governors; St. Petersburg |
| Ivan IV | Russia | Military force + terror | Oprichnina; boyar suppression |
| Suleiman | Ottoman | Law + devshirme + architecture | Janissaries; Suleymaniye Mosque |
| Askia the Great | Songhai | Islamic legitimacy + bureaucracy | Hajj; official religion of state |
| Tokugawa Ieyasu | Japan | Hostage system + social hierarchy | Sankin-kōtai; sword hunts |
