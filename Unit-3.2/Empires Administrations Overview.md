# Topic 3.2 — Empires: Administrations

> [!summary] Essential Question
> How did rulers in land-based empires **legitimize and consolidate power** from 1450 to 1750?

## The Core Challenge: Governing Diversity

Every empire in this period faced the same problem: **how do you govern a vast, multiethnic, multi-religious territory?** The answer varied by region and culture, but the tools were similar — bureaucratic elites, professional militaries, religious legitimacy, and monumental architecture. This topic examines those tools empire by empire.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam tests Topic 3.2 heavily because it connects to **historical reasoning skills**: comparison (how did different empires consolidate power?) and continuity and change (what methods persisted from earlier periods?). Be ready to compare two or more empires' administrative structures.

## Europe: Toward Centralized Monarchy

### England — Constitutional Monarchy in the Making

England's **Tudors** (ruled 1485–1603) built royal authority through **justices of the peace** — officials drawn from the landed gentry, sworn to maintain peace in the counties and carry out the monarch's laws. Over time, justices became among the most powerful groups in England. Many held seats in the House of Commons.

**Parliament**, established in 1265, both legitimized the monarch *and* limited royal power:
- The **English Bill of Rights (1689)** — signed by William and Mary — guaranteed civil liberties, required legal process before arrest, and mandated Parliament's consent for taxation and raising an army.

This made England a **constitutional monarchy**: royal authority was real but bounded. King James I believed in the *divine right of kings* — but Parliament increasingly disagreed.

> [!tip]
> England's constitutional development is unusual compared to France and the Islamic empires. While France and the Gunpowder Empires moved toward **more** centralized authority, England moved toward **shared** authority between crown and Parliament. This distinction matters for later revolutions (Topic 5.1).

### France — Absolutism

France moved in the opposite direction from England. Under Louis XIII (1610–1643) and his minister **Cardinal Richelieu**, power centralized in the crown through a system of **intendants** — royal officials sent to the provinces to execute central government orders and oversee tax collection. These intendants were sometimes called **tax farmers** because they managed revenue.

**Louis XIV** ("the Sun King," ruled 1643–1715) took absolutism to its extreme:
- Declared "L'état, c'est moi" ("I am the state")
- Combined legislative and judicial authority in himself
- Kept nobles close at the **Palace of Versailles** — 700 rooms, 60 staircases, 30,000+ acres of gardens — so they couldn't scheme independently
- Espoused divine right: God gave him absolute authority

The palace itself was a political instrument: by requiring nobles to live at Versailles and compete for courtly favors, Louis kept potential rivals dependent on him.

| England | France |
|---------|--------|
| Constitutional monarchy with Parliament | Absolute monarchy — Louis is the law |
| Justices of peace from landed gentry | Intendants appointed by the crown |
| English Bill of Rights (1689) limits crown | No comparable legal limit on Louis |
| Divine right debated and contested | Divine right unquestioned under Louis XIV |

## Russia: Autocracy and Reform

### Ivan IV and the Boyars

Social hierarchy in Moscow was nearly static. The **boyars** (noble landowning class) stood at the top; below them were merchants; at the bottom were **serfs** — peasants bound to land, with little personal freedom, transferable with the land when ownership changed.

Ivan IV fought the boyars constantly. He confiscated the lands of those in Novgorod who opposed him and forced them to Moscow (to keep watch on them — mirroring Louis XIV's Versailles strategy). He established the **oprichnina**, a black-clad paramilitary force loyal personally to Ivan, drawn from lower bureaucrats and merchants (not nobles) to ensure their loyalty.

### Peter the Great (ruled 1682–1725)

Peter I reorganized Russian government systematically:
- Created **provinces** (8, later 50 administrative divisions)
- Provincial officials received **salaries** rather than living off bribes and fees from locals
- Established a **senate** — advisory council to govern when Peter was away
- Founded **St. Petersburg** on the Baltic — a new capital designed in European style to demonstrate Peter's admiration of Western Europe
- **Abolished the Orthodox patriarch** and incorporated the Church into the state through the **Holy Synod** (see [[Unit-3.3/Belief Systems Overview]])

Peter modernized Russia's army on the Western European model: English, French, and Dutch structures. When taxes proved insufficient, he compelled workers into state shipyards — urban serfdom. In 1718, he replaced the land tax with a **head tax** (per person), increasing the burden on peasants.

> [!exam-tip]
> Peter the Great is a classic AP **comparison target**: compare Peter with Kangxi of China (both ruled at the same time, both centralized power, but with very different methods and outcomes). AMSCO includes a comparison chart — use it.

## The Ottoman Empire: Devshirme

The Ottoman sultans solved the loyalty problem with the **[[Concept Notes/Devshirme\|devshirme system]]** — one of the most distinctive administrative innovations of the period.

Starting in the late 14th century, Christian boys ages 8–20 were **forcibly recruited** from conquered Christian lands in Europe (especially the Balkans). Since Islamic law prohibited enslaving Muslims and Jews ("people of the book"), Christian boys were taken as tribute — technically one-fifth of conquered territories' wealth.

These boys were:
- Educated at a high level in politics, arts, and military skills
- **Converted to Islam**
- Trained to be **fiercely loyal to the sultan** — not to their families, tribes, or regions

The most famous group: the **[[Concept Notes/Janissaries\|Janissaries]]** — elite military forces in the Ottoman army. Other boys became administrators, scribes, tax collectors, even diplomats. Some parents *wanted* their sons selected, because devshirme offered a path to upward mobility otherwise unavailable in the empire.

> [!warning]
> The devshirme system is sometimes called "slavery," and the Janissaries were technically called "slaves of the state." But in practice, successful Janissaries could rise to positions of significant power. The system was coercive but also provided social mobility — a nuance the AP exam may probe.

## East Asia: Civil Service and Feudal Control

### China: The Civil Service Exam

After the Mongol Yuan Dynasty collapsed, the **Ming Dynasty** (1368–1644) worked to erase Mongol influence:
- Restored the traditional **civil service exam** system
- Improved education through a national school system
- Reestablished the bureaucracy that had fallen into disuse under the Mongols

Under the **Qing Dynasty**, Qianlong's reign saw the efficient bureaucracy turn **corrupt** — officials levied high taxes, leading to the White Lotus Rebellion (1796–1804). The Qing suppressed it violently.

### Japan: Tokugawa Shogunate

Before 1600, Japan was governed by **shoguns** (military leaders ruling in the emperor's name), but **daimyo** (regional landholding lords) held real power, each with his own **samurai** army. Japan was fragmented.

Three powerful daimyo unified Japan through gunpowder:
1. **Oda Nobunaga** — used muskets from Portuguese traders to take Kyoto (1568); unified ~one-third of Japan before assassination (1582)
2. **Toyotomi Hideyoshi** — extended control to most of Japan
3. **Tokugawa Ieyasu** — declared shogun (1603); moved capital to Edo (Tokyo)

The **Tokugawa shogunate** then reorganized Japan:
- Japan divided into 250 **hans** (territories), each controlled by a daimyo
- Daimyo required to maintain residences both in their home territory **and** in the capital (Edo/Tokyo)
- If a daimyo visited home, his **family stayed in Tokyo as hostages**
- This reduced daimyo to landlord-managers, not independent rulers

**Samurai** were salaried — paid first in rice, then gold — giving them economic independence. The Tokugawa period became known as the **"Period of Great Peace"** — stability achieved through hostage-keeping and demilitarization of regional lords.

> [!tip]
> The Tokugawa hostage system is a brilliant example of **creative statecraft** — solving the problem of feudal independence without destroying the feudal structure. Compare to how Louis XIV kept French nobles at Versailles.

## Mughal India: Zamindars

Under **Akbar** (ruled 1556–1605):
- Defeated Hindu armies; extended empire southward and westward
- Established efficient government from Delhi
- All subjects could appeal to him for final judgment in any lawsuit
- Paid government officials called **zamindars** handled specific duties: taxation, construction, water supply
- Capable men from Central Asia served Akbar's civil service

Later rulers gave zamindars **land grants** instead of salaries. Zamindars kept a portion of peasant taxes — initially workable, but over time they began keeping *more* than their share, building personal armies and becoming semi-independent.

The zamindar system shows how **tax collection mechanisms can undermine central authority** if not carefully monitored.

## Legitimizing Power: Religion and Art

Every empire in this period used religion and art to legitimate their authority. Key examples:

| Empire | Religious Legitimacy | Artistic/Architectural Legitimacy |
|--------|---------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **France** | Divine right of kings | Palace of Versailles |
| **Ottoman** | Caliph as successor to the Prophet | Topkapi Palace; Suleymaniye Mosque; miniature paintings |
| **Mughal** | Akbar's syncretic approach; later rulers' Islamic piety | Taj Mahal; Delhi forts; calligraphy |
| **Safavid** | Shi'a Islam as state religion | Mosque of Isfahan |
| **Russia** | Orthodoxy; tsar as "Defender of Orthodoxy" | St. Petersburg (Western style); Winter Palace |
| **Songhai** | Askia the Great promotes Islam; pilgrimage to Mecca | Timbuktu as center of Islamic learning |

### Ottoman Art and Architecture
Istanbul became a center of arts and learning. Poets and scholars met in coffeehouses and gardens. The Grand Bazaar continued operating. Ottoman miniature paintings and illuminated manuscripts became famous from the time of Mehmed II. **Suleiman I** ordered the construction of the **Suleymaniye Mosque** — one of the finest examples of Islamic architecture.

### Mughal Art and Architecture
Mughal craftspeople combined Islamic arts (calligraphy, illuminated manuscripts, ceramics) with local Indian traditions to create "airy structures with decorative geometric designs" (AMSCO p. 162). The **Taj Mahal**, built by Shah Jahan as a tomb for his wife, remains the defining symbol of Mughal cultural achievement.

## Financing Empires

All empires needed revenue for armies and administration. Their methods — and their failures — follow a similar pattern:

**Russia (Peter the Great)**: Established state-owned industries (shipyards, iron mines) and encouraged private metallurgy, woodwork, and gunpowder production. When industrialization fell short, Peter raised taxes and compelled labor in shipyards. Replaced land tax with a **head tax** in 1718.

**Ottoman Empire**: Levied taxes on peasants; used **tax farming** (local officials paid a fixed sum and kept the difference). Tax farmers grew wealthy and corrupt — skimming from collections. Agricultural villages bore the burden of supporting troops.

**Mughal Empire**: Zamindars collected taxes from peasants based on land and production. Over time, zamindars kept increasing shares and built personal armies.

**Ming China**: Wealthy families collected land taxes privately; rates were low. Taxes paid in grain (stored locally or sent by Grand Canal to military), then silver. After 1580, wars and extravagant spending pushed the dynasty toward bankruptcy.

**Tribute Systems**: China collected tributes from Korea (tributary state) and others as recognition of power. The Aztec empire had elaborate tribute lists. Songhai's Askia the Great assigned governors to tributary states in the Niger Valley.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Tax farming and tribute** are classic AP short-answer topics. Be able to explain how they worked, why they were used, and why they created long-term problems (corruption, weakened central authority, peasant resentment leading to rebellion).

## Continuity and Change

**Continuity**: The use of religious legitimacy to justify royal authority was not new — it stretched back to ancient Mesopotamia. Divine right of kings, the caliph's claim to prophetic succession, and the emperor's Mandate of Heaven all repeated the same underlying logic.

**Change**: The *mechanisms* of administration became more sophisticated — professional bureaucracies, salary-based officials (replacing fee-based corruption), standing armies, civil service exams. The period saw the **professionalization of government**.
