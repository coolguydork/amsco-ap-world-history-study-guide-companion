# Unit 3.2: Key People and Groups

#apwh #unit-3 #key-people

---

## Architects and Artists

### Sinan (Mimar Sinan) — c. 1489–1588 | Ottoman Empire
- Chief Ottoman imperial architect under sultans Suleiman I and Selim II; designed over 300 structures including mosques, bridges, schools, and caravanserais
- His masterpiece, the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, achieved a dome surpassing the Hagia Sophia in height — a deliberate architectural competition with Christianity's greatest church
- Sinan's career illustrates how the devshirme created talent pipelines: he was likely of Armenian or Greek Christian origin, recruited through the devshirme system

### Shah Jahan — r. 1628–1658 | Mughal Empire
- Mughal emperor best known for the Taj Mahal, built as a mausoleum for his wife Mumtaz Mahal (who died in childbirth in 1631)
- Also built the Red Fort in Delhi and the Peacock Throne — among the most lavish expressions of imperial wealth in world history
- His reign represents peak Mughal cultural achievement but also the beginning of financial strain: massive building programs depleted the treasury, contributing to later instability

## Administrators and Bureaucrats

### Kösem Sultan — c. 1589–1651 | Ottoman Empire
- Mother and regent for two sultans (Murad IV and Ibrahim I); one of the most powerful figures in the "Sultanate of Women" period
- Managed the empire during periods of weak sultans, negotiating with Janissary commanders and grand viziers
- Her eventual assassination by a rival faction illustrates the violent competition for power within the harem and the court

### Todar Mal — d. 1589 | Mughal Empire
- Revenue minister under Akbar; designed the zamindar land survey and revenue assessment system that became the financial backbone of the Mughal Empire
- His land survey (bandobast) measured agricultural productivity across the empire and set tax rates accordingly — one of the most sophisticated revenue systems in the premodern world
- His Hindu background and rise to the highest administrative positions reflects Akbar's inclusionary policies

### Nur Jahan — 1577–1645 | Mughal Empire
- Wife of Emperor Jahangir (Akbar's son); effectively co-ruled the Mughal Empire, issuing her own coins and sealing imperial farmans (decrees)
- Managed military campaigns, court appointments, and diplomatic correspondence; her name appeared alongside Jahangir's on official documents
- Represents the political agency available to elite women in the Mughal court system — a counterpoint to narratives of universal female subordination

> [!exam-tip]
> Nur Jahan and Kösem Sultan are both examples the AP exam uses to test students' understanding of **women's political agency within patriarchal systems** — not as rule-breakers, but as operators within existing power structures.

## Military Forces and Groups

### Janissaries (Ottoman)
- Elite infantry force created through the devshirme; armed with muskets and trained in formal military discipline
- At their peak (15th–16th centuries), the most professional standing army in Europe or the Middle East
- By the 17th–18th centuries, they had evolved from a military asset into a political liability: they blocked reforms, supported weak sultans, and eventually had to be massacred by Sultan Mahmud II in 1826 ("The Auspicious Incident")

### Sipahis (Ottoman Cavalry)
- Ottoman cavalry soldiers who received land grants (timars) in exchange for military service
- Represented the older, pre-gunpowder military tradition; gradually displaced in importance by the Janissary infantry
- Their decline illustrates the broader story of Unit 3: gunpowder technology shifted military power from cavalry to infantry

### Rajput Nobility (Mughal)
- Hindu warrior-aristocrats from Rajasthan who became incorporated into the Mughal imperial structure under Akbar through marriages (Akbar married a Rajput princess) and mansabdar appointments
- Provided crucial military manpower and administrative expertise in northern India
- Akbar's alliance with the Rajputs is a classic example of co-opting an existing elite to stabilize imperial rule; the relationship frayed under Aurangzeb

### Manchu Banner Leaders (Qing)
- The Eight Banners were led by princes of the Aisin Gioro clan (the Qing imperial family) and allied Manchu nobles
- Their privileged position — exempted from taxation, paid stipends, theoretically the empire's core military — became a long-term financial burden as the banner population grew while their military effectiveness declined
- By the 18th–19th centuries, the banners were increasingly ceremonial, forcing the Qing to rely on Han Chinese regional armies — a sign of institutional decay

## Religious Leaders

### Sheikh al-Islam (Ottoman)
- The highest religious official in the Ottoman Empire; the chief interpreter of Islamic law (sharia) with the authority to issue fatwas (legal rulings)
- Could theoretically depose a sultan who violated Islamic law — a check on absolute power
- The position illustrates the intimate relationship between religious authority and political power in Islamic empires

### Shia Ulama (Safavid/Iran)
- Safavid shahs imported Shia scholars from Arab lands (Lebanon, Bahrain, Iraq) to build a Shia clerical establishment in Persia, since Persia had been majority Sunni before 1501
- These scholars became increasingly powerful, forming the basis for the later Shia clerical establishment that still shapes Iranian politics today
- The institutionalization of Shia clerical authority under the Safavids is one of the most consequential long-term effects of this period
