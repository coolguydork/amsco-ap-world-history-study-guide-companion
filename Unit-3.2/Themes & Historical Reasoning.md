# Themes & Historical Reasoning — Empires: Administration

*AMSCO pp. 155–166 | AP Themes: GOV · SIO · ECN*

---

## AP Theme Analysis

### GOV — Governance

Topic 3.2 is the most governance-heavy topic in the entire AP World History curriculum. The central question — how did rulers legitimize and consolidate power? — is a direct test of the GOV theme.

**Legitimation** (making people *accept* authority) operated through:
- **Religious claims** — divine right in Europe; Islamic piety in Songhai and Mughal India; Confucian tradition in Qing China
- **Legal authority** — Suleiman's codification of Ottoman law; the English Parliament and Bill of Rights; Akbar's system of universal appeal
- **Artistic and architectural patronage** — Versailles, Taj Mahal, Suleymaniye Mosque, St. Petersburg

**Consolidation** (actually *holding and exercising* control) operated through:
- **Bureaucratic elites** — intendants (France), devshirme/Janissaries (Ottoman), zamindars (Mughal), civil service exams (Qing)
- **Control of rivals** — oprichnina/boyar suppression (Russia), sankin-kōtai hostage system (Japan), keeping nobles at Versailles (France)
- **Professional armies** — Janissaries (Ottoman), samurai salary system (Japan), Peter's modernized military (Russia)
- **Taxation** — tax farming (Ottoman/Mughal/France), head tax (Russia), tributes (China, Aztecs, Songhai)

**Key GOV pattern:** Every successful empire in this period combined *both* legitimation and consolidation. Legitimacy without force was fragile; force without legitimacy generated constant rebellion. The most stable rulers — Kangxi, Akbar, Louis XIV — mastered both.

### SIO — Social Interactions and Organization

**Social hierarchies and state power:** In Russia, the boyar-merchant-serf hierarchy was deliberately manipulated by both Ivan IV and Peter the Great to strengthen autocratic power at the expense of the noble class. Peter's head tax (1718) shifted the fiscal burden directly onto individual peasants, deepening serfdom.

**Devshirme and social mobility:** The Ottoman devshirme system created a paradox — boys taken by force from Christian families could rise to the highest levels of the empire's military and administration. The system both reinforced social control (only the sultan's personal recruits could hold these positions) and created unusual channels of upward mobility across religious and ethnic lines.

**Japan's fixed social hierarchy:** The Tokugawa shogunate institutionalized the rigid social order: samurai → farmers → artisans → merchants (shi-nō-kō-shō). The sword hunts (Hideyoshi) and census (Toyotomi) were specifically designed to prevent social mobility — keeping peasants as peasants and warriors as warriors. This rigidity later made Japan resistant to change when Western contact intensified in the 19th century.

**Religious minority management:** The Ottoman millet system and Akbar's policy of religious tolerance represent two successful strategies for governing multi-religious empires. Both avoided the costly, destabilizing cycle of forced conversion and rebellion. In contrast, European absolute monarchs like Louis XIV who pursued religious uniformity (revoking the Edict of Nantes, 1685) triggered emigration and economic loss.

### ECN — Economic Systems

**Tax farming:** Used in the Ottoman Empire, Mughal India, and France, tax farming was efficient in the short term but structurally corrupting. Local tax farmers enriched themselves at the government's expense, and the burden fell on agricultural villages — contributing to economic decline in both the Ottoman and Mughal empires over time.

**Tribute systems:** China, the Aztec Empire, and Songhai all used tribute as both economic extraction and political symbolism. Korea's tributary relationship with China required regular gifts of goods and acknowledgment of Chinese suzerainty — combining trade with political submission. See [[Concept Notes/Tribute System|Tribute System]].

**Architecture as economic statement:** The Taj Mahal, Versailles, and St. Petersburg were all enormously expensive. Shah Jahan's building projects strained Mughal finances; Peter the Great's St. Petersburg was built with peasant and prisoner-of-war labor. Monumental construction demonstrated wealth but also consumed it — a tension between legitimation and economic sustainability.

## Think As a Historian: Contextualization Across Cultures

*AMSCO p. 166 — TAH section*

The AMSCO text uses the **devshirme system** as a contextualization exercise: how was devshirme like or unlike slavery elsewhere in the 1450–1750 period?

**Approach:** Contextualization across cultures asks three questions:
1. In what other cultures were there similar developments?
2. In what ways were those developments *similar* to the one under study?
3. In what ways were they *different*?

**Devshirme in cross-cultural context:**

| Region | System | Origins | Purposes | Outcomes |
|--------|--------|---------|----------|---------|
| Ottomans | Devshirme | Tribute from conquered Christians | Administrative and military service | Janissaries; upward mobility possible |
| Americas (Aztec) | Tribute system | Conquered peoples | Economic extraction; political submission | Funded Aztec state; periodic rebellion |
| China | Civil service exam | Meritocratic tradition | Bureaucratic staffing | Stability; Han loyalty to Qing |
| Africa (Songhai) | Administrative officials | Askia's bureaucracy | Governing Niger Valley tributaries | Effective as long as center held |

**Conclusion:** Devshirme was unusual because it combined elements of slavery (forced removal, conversion, lifetime service) with genuine meritocracy (education, promotion, high status). Unlike chattel slavery in the Atlantic world (which had no path to freedom or high status), devshirme recruits could — and often did — rise to positions of great power. This made it a distinct form of coerced labor that served administrative rather than purely economic purposes.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Contextualization on the AP exam** means connecting your topic to a broader historical development, process, or event that is relevant but *not the immediate focus* of your essay. For Topic 3.2, strong contextualization might describe the broader pattern of empire-building after the Mongol collapse [[Unit-2.2/Mongol Empire Overview|Mongol Empire Overview]] or the role of gunpowder technology in making conquest possible [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview]] — before explaining how those empires then needed to solve the administration problem.

## Historical Reasoning Skills

### Causation

**Cause:** The need to administer vast, multi-ethnic empires over enormous distances.
**Effect:** Development of bureaucratic systems (intendants, devshirme, zamindars), military loyalty mechanisms (Janissaries, oprichnina, sankin-kōtai), and religious/artistic legitimation.

**Second-order cause:** Why did these administrative methods eventually weaken these empires?
- Tax farmers became corrupt and autonomous, reducing revenue to the center.
- Janissaries eventually became a political force that *blocked* reform rather than serving the sultan.
- Zamindars built private armies and undermined Mughal authority.
- The high cost of monumental construction (Versailles, Taj Mahal) strained imperial finances.

### Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

**Continuity:** The basic challenge of governing diverse, far-flung territories was the same in 1450 as it had been for the Roman Empire, the Abbasid Caliphate, and the Mongol khanates. Solutions — bureaucracy, military loyalty, religious legitimacy — were also not new.

**Change:** What was *new* in 1450–1750 was the *scale* of centralization. The devshirme system, the intendant system, and the sankin-kōtai hostage system all represent more systematic, institutionalized approaches to administration than earlier empires had achieved. Gunpowder weapons [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview]] also gave rulers new tools for suppressing internal as well as external enemies.

### Comparison

The AP exam frequently asks students to compare administrative methods across empires. Three strong comparison pairs:

**1. France vs. Ottoman Empire** — Both used intermediate officials (intendants vs. devshirme administrators) to bypass local power and execute central government orders. Difference: French intendants were free subjects; devshirme officials were coerced converts without family ties in the ruling class.

**2. England vs. France** — Both European monarchies used divine right as legitimation. Difference: England developed *limited* monarchy (Parliament, Bill of Rights, justices of the peace drawn from local gentry); France developed *absolute* monarchy (intendants replacing local power, Louis XIV as unchecked dictator).

**3. Mughal India vs. Ottoman Empire** — Both governed multi-religious populations. Difference: Akbar pursued active tolerance (abolishing jizya, employing Hindus); the Ottomans governed through separation (millet system), allowing minorities autonomy but keeping them subordinate.

## Key Historiographical Perspectives

**On devshirme:** Some historians emphasize its coercive nature (forced removal of children); others emphasize the social mobility it offered in an otherwise rigid hierarchy. Both are valid — the system was simultaneously exploitative and an unusual path to power for non-elites.

**On absolute monarchy:** Later historians (especially Enlightenment thinkers) criticized divine right as irrational and tyrannical. But in its context, divine right was an effective ideology for breaking the power of feudal lords and centralizing administration — it served a functional political purpose before it became a justification for arbitrary abuse.

**On Akbar's tolerance:** Some historians see Akbar as a genuinely enlightened ruler; others argue his tolerance was purely strategic — a rational policy for governing a Hindu majority that could not be forcibly converted without constant rebellion. The evidence supports both readings.
