## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 are based on the following passage (AMSCO p. 164):*

> "Demonized as an enemy of the faith by the Muslim narrative sources, yet lionized as a warrior hero in the oral tradition, Sunni Ali, who reigned from 1464 to 1492, is one of the most controversial figures of the African Middle Ages. … Relying on a swift and mobile cavalry force as well as on naval control of the Niger River, Sunni Ali had conquered the agriculturally rich central Niger … by the 1470s. … He was well aware that a vast empire could not be held together by military conquests alone, but needed an effective and efficient administrative structure as well. Indeed, the organization of Songhai government which was developed to a great degree under Sunni Ali differed substantially from previous Sudanic patterns of empire. These had been based more on alliances and relationships with tributary states than on the high degree of centralization characteristic of Songhai."
>
> — J. Rotondo-McCord, "Kingdoms of the Medieval Sudan," Xavier University

**1.** The Songhai Empire was like Japan under the Tokugawa shogunate because the Songhai

A. created a strong central government
B. expanded its territory by creating distant colonies
C. was ruled by a Muslim
D. was the first empire in its region to trade gold extensively

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage emphasizes Songhai's "high degree of centralization" — a strong central government. Tokugawa Japan similarly built a centralized state through the sankin-kōtai system and shogunal authority over the daimyo. Choice B is wrong because neither Songhai nor Tokugawa Japan created overseas colonies. Choice C fails because Tokugawa Japan was not Muslim. Choice D has no parallel to Japan.

---

**2.** The Songhai Empire under Sunni Ali was different from the empire under Askia the Great because

A. Sunni Ali made a pilgrimage to Mecca
B. Askia used religion to legitimize his control
C. Sunni Ali made Songhai the largest kingdom in West Africa
D. Askia used zamindars to collect taxes

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The passage describes Sunni Ali as "demonized as an enemy of the faith" — he did not use Islamic piety as legitimation. Askia the Great, by contrast, explicitly used Islam: he accused Sunni Ali of being a bad Muslim, made a famous hajj to Mecca, and declared Islam Songhai's official religion. Choice A is wrong — it was Askia who made the pilgrimage, not Sunni Ali. Choice C is wrong — it was under Askia that Songhai became the largest West African kingdom. Choice D is wrong — zamindars were a Mughal institution, not Songhai's.

---

**3.** How did Askia the Great challenge Sunni Ali's legitimacy?

A. He claimed Sunni Ali was a weak military commander.
B. He questioned Sunni Ali's faithfulness to the principles of Islam.
C. He established strong tributary ties that had more allegiances to him than to Sunni Ali.
D. He reformed taxation policies throughout the kingdom.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The passage explicitly states that Sunni Ali was "demonized as an enemy of the faith by Muslim narrative sources." Askia built his claim to power on the argument that Sunni Ali was insufficiently Islamic — a religious legitimacy challenge. This mirrors how other rulers in this period used religion to either gain or undermine political authority. Choice A is incorrect — the passage praises Sunni Ali's military achievements. Choices C and D describe things Askia may have done as ruler but are not how he *challenged Sunni Ali's legitimacy*.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Hideyoshi's Sword Hunt Edict

*Use the following passage (AMSCO p. 165):*

> "Farmers of all provinces are strictly forbidden to have in their possession any swords, short swords, bows, spears, firearms, or other types of weapons. If unnecessary implements of war are kept, the collection of annual rent (nengu) may become more difficult, and without provocation uprisings can [occur]. … The heads of the provinces, samurai who receive a grant of land, and deputies must collect all the weapons described above and submit them to Hideyoshi's government. … If farmers possess only agricultural implements and devote themselves exclusively to cultivating the fields, they and their descendants will prosper."
>
> — Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Imperial Regent of Japan, edicts issued in 1588

**Part A.** Identify ONE technological advance that allowed Hideyoshi and other shoguns to enforce such edicts over farmers.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> The development and spread of **firearms** (specifically muskets) allowed Hideyoshi and his samurai forces to enforce the sword hunt. Hideyoshi's predecessor Oda Nobunaga had used Portuguese-supplied muskets to defeat traditional samurai cavalry. By holding a monopoly on firearms while disarming the peasantry, the shogunate ensured that farmers lacked the means to resist — the very weapons the edict prohibits were the weapons Hideyoshi's forces possessed exclusively.

**Part B.** Explain ONE way in which Hideyoshi's goals were similar to those of Louis XIV.

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Both Hideyoshi and Louis XIV sought to **concentrate power in the central government by neutralizing potential sources of armed resistance**. Hideyoshi disarmed the peasantry through sword hunts, ensuring they could not threaten shogunal authority. Louis XIV required the French nobility to reside at Versailles, keeping them under royal observation and preventing them from building independent military power in their home provinces. In both cases, the ruler recognized that rivals with independent military capacity posed an existential threat, and used institutional mechanisms to eliminate that threat.

**Part C.** Explain ONE interpretation historians could develop of Hideyoshi's ideas about the Japanese economy from this piece of evidence.

> [!tip] Model Response — Historical Interpretation
> A historian could interpret this edict as evidence that Hideyoshi believed **social stability and agricultural productivity required fixed social hierarchies**. The edict explicitly links disarmament to economic prosperity: "if farmers possess only agricultural implements and devote themselves exclusively to cultivating the fields, they and their descendants will prosper." This reveals a Confucian-influenced view that each social class should perform its designated role — farmers farm, samurai fight — and that crossing these boundaries (farmers owning weapons) disrupts the natural economic order. Hideyoshi's sword hunts and concurrent census (fixing people as farmers or warriors by birth) reflect a conscious economic theory, not merely a security measure.

### SAQ 2 — Comparative Political Structures (1450–1750)

**Part A.** Identify ONE way in which the political structures of Europeans differed from those of the Ottomans in the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Difference
> European monarchies (especially England) developed **representative institutions** that checked royal power, while the Ottoman sultan exercised unconstrained autocratic authority. In England, Parliament and the English Bill of Rights (1689) required royal consent from legislative bodies and guaranteed civil liberties; no Ottoman equivalent existed. Even in France — the most absolutist European state — the theoretical model of divine right drew from Christian theology and Roman law traditions, while Ottoman authority derived from the sultan's role as Caliph and from Islamic law. The devshirme system, through which the Ottoman state recruited non-hereditary officials, also had no European parallel — European monarchies relied on hereditary noble officials.

**Part B.** Explain ONE way in which the political structures of the Aztecs were similar to those of China in the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Both the Aztec Empire and Ming/Qing China used **tribute systems** to govern subordinate states and extract resources. The Aztecs stationed officials in each provincial capital to collect tribute payments from local leaders; the tribute demonstrated political submission and funded the Aztec state's military and religious expenditures. China similarly collected tribute from tributary states such as Korea, requiring regular payments and acknowledgment of Chinese suzerainty. In both cases, tribute combined economic extraction with political symbolism — forcing subordinate rulers to publicly recognize the dominant empire's authority.

**Part C.** Explain ONE reason that the Mughal leaders constructed impressive buildings for worship and as memorials during the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Mughal leaders built monumental architecture to **legitimize their rule** by demonstrating their wealth, piety, and access to extraordinary resources. The Taj Mahal, built by Shah Jahan as a tomb for his wife, required the mobilization of thousands of skilled craftspeople, enormous quantities of white marble, and decades of labor — publicly demonstrating that the Mughal emperor commanded the wealth and organizational capacity of a great Islamic empire. The structure also blended Islamic calligraphy and geometric design with local Indian artistic traditions, visually representing Mughal cultural synthesis. For a Muslim ruling a predominantly Hindu population, such architectural piety reinforced the emperor's Islamic legitimacy while displaying imperial power to all subjects.

## Historical Reasoning Practice: Contextualizing Across Cultures

*AMSCO p. 166 — Think As a Historian*

**Task:** Within the context of global slavery between 1450 and 1750, how was the devshirme system like or unlike slavery elsewhere?

**Cross-cultural contextualization chart:**

| Region/Culture | Origins | Purposes | Outcomes |
|----------------|---------|----------|---------|
| Ottomans | Tribute from conquered Christian peoples | Educated and trained for administrative and military service; paid a salary | Became influential members of society; formed the first standing army (Janissaries) |
| Americas (Atlantic) | Capture in war; purchase from African traders; hereditary | Agricultural labor (sugar, tobacco, cotton); domestic service | Chattel slavery with no path to freedom; generational poverty; foundation of Atlantic economy |
| China | Examination system (meritocratic, not coerced) | Bureaucratic staffing; administrative continuity | Han officials maintained legitimacy for the Qing; stable governance; social mobility through scholarship |
| Africa (Songhai) | Askia's administrative appointments | Governing Niger Valley tributary states; tax collection | Effective central administration as long as the empire held; collapsed with Moroccan invasion (1591) |

**Conclusion:** The devshirme system was unusual because it combined coercion (forced removal, conversion, no family ties to ruling class) with genuine meritocracy and high social status — features absent from Atlantic chattel slavery. Unlike Chinese civil service examination (voluntary, open to any scholar), devshirme was not a choice. Unlike Atlantic slavery (hereditary, no path to freedom), devshirme recruits could rise to the empire's highest offices. It is best understood as a distinctive form of *coerced administrative service* rather than as either chattel slavery or free bureaucratic employment.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Contextualization on the AP exam** = connecting your specific topic to a *broader* historical context. For 3.2, strong contextualization might: (1) place administrative centralization in the context of post-Mongol empire-building, (2) connect devshirme to broader patterns of coerced labor in 1450–1750, or (3) frame the divergence of English limited monarchy vs. European absolutism as context for the later Atlantic Revolutions ([[Unit-5.2/Nationalism & Revolutions Overview|Topic 5.2]]).

## Essential Question Response

**Prompt:** In one to three paragraphs, explain how rulers in land-based empires legitimized and consolidated their power from 1450 to 1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> Rulers in land-based empires between 1450 and 1750 developed two overlapping strategies: *legitimation* (persuading subjects that their authority was rightful) and *consolidation* (creating institutional mechanisms that made their power real). Legitimation most commonly relied on religious claims — European monarchs invoked divine right, Askia the Great of Songhai promoted Islam and made the hajj, Akbar of the Mughal Empire pursued religious tolerance to win loyalty from his Hindu subjects, and Kangxi of Qing China governed through Confucian tradition. Rulers also commissioned monumental architecture — Versailles, the Taj Mahal, the Suleymaniye Mosque, St. Petersburg — as tangible demonstrations of their wealth, piety, and organizational power.
>
> Consolidation required loyal institutions to extend authority across vast territories. France developed a system of **intendants** — royal officials sent to bypass feudal lords and execute the king's orders directly. The Ottoman Empire used the **devshirme** system to recruit a non-hereditary administrative and military elite with no loyalty except to the sultan. Mughal rulers created **zamindars** to manage taxation and administration. Japan's Tokugawa shogunate neutralized the powerful daimyo through the **sankin-kōtai** hostage system. Russia under Peter the Great replaced corrupt local officials with salaried provincial governors and subordinated the Church and boyar nobility to the tsar.
>
> Despite these successes, consolidation had structural limits. Tax farmers enriched themselves at the state's expense; zamindars built private armies; Janissaries eventually resisted reform. The very mechanisms that built these empires contained the seeds of their later decline — a pattern that connects Topic 3.2 directly to the challenges examined in [[Unit-4.6/Internal & External Challenges to State Power Overview|Topic 4.6]].
