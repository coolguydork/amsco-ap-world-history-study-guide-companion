# Key Terms & Concepts — Empires: Administration

*AMSCO pp. 155–166 | AP Themes: GOV · SIO · ECN*

---

## Governance — Concepts

**Divine right of kings**
The belief, common in the Middle Ages and early modern Europe, that a monarch's authority was granted directly by God — placing the king above earthly law and any human challenge. King James I of England articulated this most forcefully. It was the primary ideological justification for absolute monarchy in Europe. *p. 155*

**Absolute monarchy**
A system of government in which the monarch holds complete, unchecked authority over the state — combining lawmaking, judicial, and executive power in one person. France under Louis XIV is the AP exam's defining example: "L'état, c'est moi." Contrast with England, where Parliament and the English Bill of Rights limited royal power. *p. 156*

**Millet system** *(Ottoman Empire)*
The Ottoman system of organizing non-Muslim subjects (Christians, Jews) into self-governing religious communities called millets. Each millet handled its own religious law, education, and internal affairs while paying taxes to the Ottoman state. This allowed the empire to govern diverse populations without forced conversion. *(mentioned in broader 3.2 context; central to Topic 3.3)*

**Devshirme** *(Ottoman Empire)*
Literally "collecting" in Ottoman Turkish. The system by which the Ottoman government recruited Christian boys aged 8–20 from conquered Balkan territories by force, converted them to Islam, and trained them for administrative and military service. The devshirme was a form of tribute from conquered Christian peoples. Because these recruits had no family connections in the Muslim ruling class, they were considered absolutely loyal to the sultan. *p. 158*

**Janissaries** *(Ottoman Empire)*
Elite Ottoman infantry soldiers recruited through the devshirme system. Rigorously trained, well-paid, and personally loyal to the sultan, the Janissaries were the most disciplined standing army of their era — a key source of Ottoman military power from the 14th through early 19th centuries. *p. 158*

**Sankin-kōtai** *(Tokugawa Japan)*
The "alternate attendance" system of the Tokugawa shogunate requiring daimyo (regional lords) to maintain residences both in their home domains and in the capital, Edo. When a daimyo returned home, his family remained in Edo — effectively as hostages. The constant travel also drained daimyo finances, preventing them from funding rebellions. *p. 159*

**Oprichnina** *(Russia)*
Ivan IV's personal paramilitary force, established in the 1560s. Dressed in black and fiercely loyal to Ivan, its members were drawn from lower-level bureaucrats and merchants — not from the boyar nobility Ivan distrusted. The oprichnina terrorized Ivan's enemies and is considered a forerunner of the later Russian secret police. *p. 157*

**Intendants** *(France)*
Royal officials dispatched to the provinces under Louis XIII and Louis XIV to execute the orders of the central French government. Sometimes called "tax farmers," intendants replaced local power-holders (feudal lords, regional courts) as the effective agents of royal authority. They were the mechanism by which absolute monarchy reached beyond Paris. *p. 156*

**Justices of the peace** *(England)*
Local officials selected from the landed gentry under Tudor rule to maintain order in English counties and carry out the monarch's laws. Unlike the French intendants, justices of the peace were not royal appointees sent from the center — they were local elites incorporated into the royal system. Their growing power in Parliament bridged the gap between crown and county. *p. 155*

## Governance — Institutions and Dynasties

**Tudor dynasty** *(England, 1485–1603)*
The English royal house that centralized power after the Wars of the Roses. The Tudors ruled through Parliament and the justices of the peace rather than against them, creating a distinctive English model of limited but effective monarchy. Henry VIII's break with Rome in 1534 made the Tudor monarch head of both state and church. *p. 155*

**Romanov Dynasty** *(Russia, 1613–1917)*
The Russian imperial dynasty that came to power in 1613 after Ivan IV's death and a period of instability. Under the Romanovs, three groups competed for power: the Orthodox Church, the boyar nobility, and the royal family. Peter the Great (Romanov) modernized Russia by subordinating all three to the tsar's autocratic will. *p. 158*

**Tokugawa shogunate** *(Japan, 1603–1868)*
Military government established by Tokugawa Ieyasu after his victory at the Battle of Sekigahara (1600) and his appointment as shogun (1603). The shogunate centralized control over Japan's 250 daimyo domains through the sankin-kōtai system, sword hunts, and strict social hierarchy. The resulting Edo period is also called the "Period of Great Peace." *p. 159*

**English Bill of Rights** *(1689)*
Document signed by William and Mary that guaranteed individual civil liberties and limited royal power — requiring parliamentary consent for taxation and raising armies. The Bill of Rights is the AP exam's key evidence that England developed *limited* rather than absolute monarchy, in contrast to France, Russia, and the Ottoman Empire. *p. 156*

## Economy — Tax Systems

**Tax farming**
A system in which the government grants private individuals (tax farmers) the right to collect taxes in a given area, keeping a portion of what they collect as compensation. Used in both the Ottoman Empire and Mughal India, tax farming was efficient in theory but prone to corruption as tax farmers enriched themselves at both the government's and peasants' expense. *pp. 156, 162*

**Zamindars** *(Mughal India)*
Originally paid government officials under Akbar responsible for specific administrative duties (taxation, construction, water supply). Later they received land grants and were permitted to keep a portion of collected taxes. Over time, zamindars accumulated wealth, built private armies, and became semi-autonomous — a key factor in Mughal administrative decline. *p. 160*

**Tributes**
Payments of wealth demanded by a dominant empire from subordinate states as recognition of power and political submission. China collected tribute from Korea; the Aztecs collected from conquered peoples; Songhai collected from states in the Niger Valley. Tribute combined economic extraction with political symbolism — forcing rivals to publicly acknowledge their subordinate status. See [[Concept Notes/Tribute System|Tribute System]]. *p. 163*

**Boyars** *(Russia)*
The hereditary noble landowning class in medieval and early modern Russia. Boyars stood at the top of Russian social hierarchy, above merchants and far above the peasant serfs bound to their land. Both Ivan IV and Peter the Great worked to break boyar power — by force (Ivan's oprichnina) and by requiring state service (Peter's administrative reforms). *p. 157*

**Serfdom** *(Russia)*
The system by which Russian peasants were legally bound to the land of a noble lord. Serfs received a plot of land and protection in exchange for labor and a portion of their produce; they could not leave without the lord's permission, and transfers of land transferred the serfs as well. Peter the Great's head tax and forced labor in state industries deepened serfdom rather than relieving it. *p. 157*

## Culture and Legitimacy

**Taj Mahal** *(Mughal India)*
A white marble mausoleum built by Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658) in Agra as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal. Combining Islamic calligraphy, geometric design, and local Indian artistic traditions, the Taj Mahal is the supreme example of Mughal architectural legitimation — demonstrating the empire's wealth, artistic sophistication, and Islamic piety. *p. 161*

**Palace of Versailles** *(France)*
Louis XIV's massive royal palace outside Paris, completed in the 1680s. With over 700 rooms, 60 staircases, and 30,000-acre gardens, Versailles was both a symbol of absolute royal power and a political instrument — requiring the nobility to reside there, away from their home provinces and dependent on the king's favor. *p. 162*

**Topkapi Palace** *(Ottoman Empire)*
The royal residence of the Ottoman sultans in Istanbul, begun by Mehmed II (r. 1451–1481). It housed the imperial harem, treasury, administrative offices, and the Janissary barracks — the physical center of Ottoman power and governance. *p. 162*

> [!exam-tip]
> When writing about legitimation in an AP essay, always connect the specific monument to the specific political need it served. Versailles → controlling the nobility. Taj Mahal → displaying Mughal wealth and Islamic piety. Suleymaniye Mosque → Suleiman's authority as both emperor and defender of Islam.
