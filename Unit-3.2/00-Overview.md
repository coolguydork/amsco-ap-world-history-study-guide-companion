# Unit 3.2: Empires: Administration

#apwh #unit-3 #period-2 #administration

## Topic Summary

Having established *how* land-based empires came to power (Unit 3.1), Unit 3.2 asks *how* they stayed in power. The answer involves three overlapping strategies: **bureaucratic administration**, **military organization**, and **cultural legitimation**. Empires that lasted — the Ottomans for six centuries, the Qing for nearly three — were not just military powerhouses. They built effective systems for collecting taxes, deploying armies, adjudicating disputes, and persuading their subjects that their rule was divinely sanctioned and culturally magnificent. This chapter is fundamentally about the architecture of power — both literal architecture (mosques, palaces, forts) and the invisible architecture of institutions and ideology.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain the methods by which rulers consolidated and maintained power
- Analyze how art, architecture, and religion served as tools of political legitimation
- Compare the administrative systems used by different land-based empires

## AP Key Concepts

- **KC-4.1.II** — Rulers employed a mix of taxation, military, and legal mechanisms to consolidate power
- **KC-4.3.I** — Empires used existing social structures, cultural frameworks, and belief systems to maintain power
- **KC-4.3.II** — Rulers adapted cultural practices to new audiences and contexts to build legitimacy

## Core Administrative Systems to Compare

| Empire | Tax System | Military System | Administrative Personnel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ottoman | Provincial tax farming; jizya | Janissaries (devshirme); timars (land grants to cavalry) | Devshirme-trained bureaucrats; ulama (religious scholars) |
| Mughal | Mansabdar (rank-based military/tax) | Mansabdar cavalry; war elephants; artillery | Mansabdars; Persian-speaking elite; Hindu Rajput nobles |
| Qing | Land tax; tribute from border peoples | Eight Banners (Manchu); Green Standard Army (Han) | Dyarchy of Manchu + Han officials |
| Safavid | Land tax; silk trade revenue | Ghulam (slave soldiers, modeled on devshirme) | Persian-educated administrators; Shia clergy (ulama) |
| Russian | Poll tax on peasants (serfs) | Streltsy; Cossack frontier forces | Boyars (noble class); later Westernized bureaucracy |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves comparison questions about administrative systems. Be ready to explain: What problem was each system solving? (Answer: How to govern a large empire without losing control to powerful nobles or military commanders.)

## Big Picture

Unit 3.2 is where "state-building" becomes concrete. The great question of Unit 3 is not just "who won?" but "how did they rule?" The empires that developed sophisticated administrative solutions — the Ottomans' devshirme, the Mughals' mansabdar, the Qing's dyarchy — proved far more durable than those relying on sheer military force alone. Art and architecture were not just decoration; they were political statements. The Taj Mahal, the Süleymaniye Mosque, the Isfahan Bazaar — all communicated divine mandate, imperial wealth, and cultural greatness to subjects and rivals alike.
