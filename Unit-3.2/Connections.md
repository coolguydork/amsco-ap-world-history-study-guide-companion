## Builds On (Prerequisites)

**[[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|Topic 3.1 — Empires Expand]]**
The empires whose *administrative* methods are examined in 3.2 were *established* in 3.1. You can't administer an empire you haven't yet conquered. The gunpowder technology that enabled conquest (3.1) also gave rulers new tools for suppressing internal rivals (3.2 — oprichnina, sword hunts, professional armies). The same rulers appear in both topics, but the lens shifts from expansion to governance.

**[[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|Topic 1.1 — Developments in East Asia]]**
The Chinese civil service examination system, first developed under the Tang and perfected under the Song, is the direct ancestor of the Qing bureaucratic model in 3.2. Kangxi's ability to rule through Han Chinese officials using Confucian-style administration depended on this centuries-old tradition. See [[Concept Notes/Civil Service Examination|Civil Service Examination]].

**Topic 2.2 — The Mongol Empire**
The collapse of the Mongol khanates (post-1350s) created power vacuums that the Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, and Russian empires filled. The Mongols also modeled large-scale multi-ethnic administration — both what to imitate (efficient courier systems, religious tolerance under Mongke Khan) and what to avoid (over-extension, succession crises). See [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]].

## Leads To (What Follows)

**Topic 3.3 — Empires: Belief Systems**
3.3 zooms in on one dimension of 3.2 — the use of religion to legitimize and administer empires. Topic 3.2 introduces the pattern; 3.3 examines it in depth, especially the interactions between rulers and religious institutions (the Ottoman devshirme and millet system, Mughal tolerance and Akbar's Din-i-Ilahi, the Catholic Church in Spanish America).

**Topic 4.5 — Maritime Empires Develop**
When European powers built their overseas empires, they faced the same administrative challenge as the land-based empires of 3.2: how do you govern distant, diverse territories with limited communications? European colonial administration (viceroys, audiencias, encomienda) is the maritime analog to the intendants, devshirme, and zamindars of 3.2. The comparison reveals both the universality of the administrative challenge and the differences in how maritime vs. land-based empires solved it.

**Topic 4.6 — Internal and External Challenges to State Power**
4.6 examines what happened when the administrative methods of 3.2 *failed* — when Janissaries became a political veto on reform, when zamindars built private armies, when nobles resisted absolutism, when peasants rebelled against taxation. Understanding 3.2's methods is prerequisite to understanding 4.6's breakdowns.

## Compare With

**Topic 4.5 — European colonial vs. land-based imperial administration**
Both types of empire faced the core challenge of governing diverse, distant territories. Land empires used geographic proximity and overland communication; maritime empires used sea routes and settler colonialism. Both developed intermediate officials (intendants / viceroys) and both faced the problem of officials becoming corrupt or autonomous over time.

**[[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|Topic 1.1]] — Civil service exam vs. devshirme**
The Chinese model recruited officials through *meritocratic examination* open to anyone who could pass; the Ottoman devshirme recruited through *forced conscription* of Christian boys. Both aimed to create loyal, competent administrators who owed their position to the emperor/sultan rather than to hereditary privilege — but through radically different mechanisms. The exam system rewarded Confucian learning; devshirme rewarded military and administrative training.

**Limited vs. absolute monarchy (England vs. France)**
England and France were neighbors with similar religious legitimation (Christian divine right) but divergent political outcomes. By 1689, England had a constitutional monarchy with parliamentary consent required for taxation and armies; France under Louis XIV was the archetype of absolute monarchy. This divergence matters enormously for Topics 5 and 6 — England's limited monarchy helped produce the conditions for the Industrial Revolution and democratic reform; France's absolutism produced the conditions for the French Revolution.

## AP Threads Running Through 3.2

**Bureaucracy and governance** — The fundamental AP World theme: rulers create institutions to extend their authority. Every administrative system in 3.2 (devshirme, intendants, zamindars, civil service exams, sankin-kōtai) is an answer to the question: how do you make your authority real across a vast territory?

**Slavery in administration** — The devshirme system complicates simple narratives about slavery. Devshirme recruits were simultaneously enslaved (forced, converted, separated from families) and privileged (educated, paid, eligible for the highest offices). This is distinct from both Atlantic chattel slavery and free bureaucratic service. The AP exam uses this to test students' ability to contextualize across cultures and avoid anachronistic judgments.

**Religious minority management** — Rulers in multi-religious empires had to decide: force conversion (risks rebellion, loses productive subjects), tolerate (risks religious legitimacy with your own community), or institutionalize separation (Ottoman millet system). Each choice had administrative and economic consequences.

**Tax systems and imperial sustainability** — All empires in this era depended on agricultural taxation as their primary revenue source. The corruption of tax-collection systems (tax farming, zamindars) was a structural weakness that contributed to long-term decline — connecting 3.2 to the later Topics 4.6 and 5.
