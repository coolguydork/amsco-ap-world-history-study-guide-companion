# Empires: Administration

**Period:** c. 1450–c. 1750 | **AP Themes:** GOV · SIO · ECN
**Essential Question:** How did rulers in land-based empires legitimize and consolidate their power from 1450 to 1750?

## The Central Problem of Empire

Conquering territory is one thing; holding it is another. By 1450, the great land-based empires studied in [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|Topic 3.1]] had established their borders. Now they faced the harder task: administering vast, multi-ethnic realms across enormous distances. Every ruler answered the same question differently — but all needed three things to survive: **money** (taxation), **loyalty** (bureaucracy or military), and **legitimacy** (religion, law, or spectacle). *AMSCO pp. 155–166*

> [!tip]
> The AP exam loves to ask you to **compare** administrative methods across empires. Know at least two specific examples for each method: bureaucratic elites, military loyalty, religious justification, and monumental architecture.

---

## Method 1 — Divine Right and Religious Legitimacy

The most universal method of consolidating power was claiming that the ruler's authority came from God or Heaven.

**England** — King James I (r. 1603–1625) articulated the **divine right of kings** most bluntly: "kings are not only God's lieutenants upon earth … but even by God himself they are called gods." The Tudor dynasty (1485–1603) strengthened this by breaking from Rome — when **Henry VIII** established the Church of England in 1534, the monarch became head of both church and state, fusing religious and political authority. Critically, the Tudors also worked *through* Parliament and relied on **justices of the peace** (officials from the landed gentry) to administer the counties. Parliament itself provided legitimacy and, after the **English Bill of Rights (1689)**, also checked royal power — a key contrast with absolute monarchy elsewhere.

**France** — Louis XIV (r. 1643–1715) took divine right to its logical extreme. "L'état, c'est moi" ("I am the state") — he combined lawmaking and justice in his person, kept the nobility weak by requiring their attendance at his palace at **Versailles**, and used his chief minister **Cardinal Richelieu's** system of **intendants** (royal officials sent to the provinces) to bypass local power. France under Louis XIV is the AP exam's archetype of **absolute monarchy**.

**Songhai (West Africa)** — **Askia the Great** (Askia Mohammad I, r. 1493–1528) used Islam as a legitimating force. He claimed his predecessor Sunni Ali was not a faithful Muslim, made an elaborate hajj to Mecca, and made Islam Songhai's official religion — uniting the empire under shared faith while building an efficient bureaucracy to govern it.

**Mughal India** — **Akbar the Great** (r. 1556–1605) took a different path: rather than enforcing Islam on his Hindu-majority population, he pursued **religious tolerance** — abolishing the jizya (tax on non-Muslims), employing Hindu officials in government, and creating a syncretic court culture. This was itself a form of legitimation: demonstrating that the Mughal ruler was just and fair to all subjects.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Comparison question favorite:** Akbar's religious tolerance vs. Louis XIV's religious uniformity (he revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, expelling French Protestants). Same era, opposite approaches — both aimed at consolidating power through religion.

## Method 2 — Bureaucratic Administration

Rulers who could not personally supervise every province delegated to loyal officials — but the challenge was keeping those officials loyal.

**France — Intendants:** Richelieu's system sent **intendants** directly to the provinces to execute the crown's orders. These officials oversaw tax collection and could not build independent power bases because they served at the king's pleasure. *AMSCO p. 156*

**Ottoman Empire — Devshirme:** The Ottomans solved the loyalty problem radically. The **devshirme** system (developed late 14th century, expanded 15th–16th centuries) recruited Christian boys aged 8–20 from conquered Balkan territories by force, converting them to Islam and training them in administration, the arts, or the military. The most elite became **Janissaries** — the Ottoman standing army, loyal to the sultan because they had no other social identity or family ties to the old order. Others became scribes, tax collectors, diplomats, or bodyguards. Since Islamic law prohibited enslaving Muslims or Jews, only Christian subjects were eligible — making devshirme a form of tribute from conquered peoples. Paradoxically, some parents even *wanted* their sons recruited, since the system offered a path of upward mobility. *AMSCO pp. 158–159*

**Mughal India — Zamindars:** Akbar created paid government officials called **zamindars** who oversaw taxation, construction, and water supply. Later they received land grants rather than salaries and kept a portion of collected taxes. This worked efficiently under Akbar, but subsequent zamindars began keeping more revenue and building private armies — a classic pattern of administrative decay. *AMSCO p. 160*

**Qing China — Kangxi:** Emperor **Kangxi** (r. 1661–1722) maintained the traditional civil service examination system, re-employing Han Chinese scholars in the bureaucracy while carefully maintaining Manchu ethnic distinctions. The exam system provided meritocratic legitimacy; the emperor kept ultimate control.

**Tokugawa Japan — Sankin-kōtai:** The Tokugawa shogunate divided Japan into 250 domains (hans), each controlled by a daimyo. To prevent rebellion, it required daimyo to maintain residences both in their home territory and in the capital, Edo. When a daimyo visited home, his family stayed in Edo — essentially as hostages. This system brilliantly neutralized the threat from powerful regional lords. *AMSCO p. 159*

> [!warning]
> A common misconception: devshirme boys were not simply enslaved laborers. They received an elite education and could rise to the highest offices in the empire. The Janissaries were the most powerful military force of their era. The system is better understood as **forced recruitment into privileged service** than as chattel slavery.

## Method 3 — Military Force and Control of Rivals

Several rulers kept power by physically neutralizing potential rivals.

**Russia — Ivan IV ("Ivan the Terrible"):** Ivan dealt with the **boyars** (noble landowners who challenged him) by confiscating their lands and forcing them to relocate to Moscow — keeping enemies close. He also created the **oprichnina**, a black-clad paramilitary drawn from lower-level bureaucrats and merchants (not boyars), giving him personal enforcers loyal to him alone. The oprichnina foreshadowed Russia's later secret police. *AMSCO p. 157*

**Russia — Peter the Great:** Peter I (r. 1682–1725) reorganized Russia into provinces with **salaried officials**, replacing the corrupt old system where local officials "fed off the land" through bribes and fees. He created a senate, modernized the military along Western European lines, and built **St. Petersburg** on newly conquered Baltic land — moving the capital from Moscow to keep the boyars under watch. *AMSCO pp. 158, 160*

**France — Louis XIV at Versailles:** Versailles was not merely a symbol — it was an instrument of control. By requiring the nobility to attend court there, Louis prevented them from building independent power in their home provinces.

## Method 4 — Monumental Architecture and Artistic Patronage

Across the globe, rulers commissioned grand buildings to demonstrate wealth, piety, and power.

| Empire | Monument | Purpose |
|--------|----------|---------|
| France | Palace of Versailles (moved court 1682) | Glorify Louis XIV; neutralize nobility |
| Mughal India | Taj Mahal (~1653) | Tomb for Shah Jahan's wife; display of wealth and artistry |
| Mughal India | Red Fort, Delhi | Administrative and military seat of Mughal power |
| Ottoman Empire | Suleymaniye Mosque, Istanbul | Suleiman's religious authority and imperial grandeur |
| Safavid Persia | Mosque of Isfahan | Shah Abbas I's Islamic legitimation |
| Russia | St. Petersburg | Peter the Great's westernization; new administrative capital |

The pattern is consistent: monumental architecture communicated that a ruler was wealthy enough to mobilize enormous resources, pious enough to honor God, and powerful enough to command thousands of workers. *AMSCO pp. 160–162*

> [!exam-tip]
> Architecture is always a strong piece of evidence in LEQ and DBQ responses about legitimizing power. Pair a specific building with the ruler's specific political need — Versailles to control nobles, the Suleymaniye Mosque to claim Islamic authority, St. Petersburg to project Western-style modernity.

## Method 5 — Taxation Systems

Empires required revenue to fund armies, bureaucracies, and construction. Each developed distinct mechanisms — with distinct weaknesses.

**France** — Tax farmers collected revenue in the provinces, keeping a portion as profit. This funded the state but enriched middlemen at the crown's expense.

**Ottoman and Mughal Empires** — Both used **tax farming**: local officials collected taxes and kept a share. Agricultural villages bore the heaviest burden, also funding the upkeep of troops. Corruption eventually undermined both empires' finances. *AMSCO p. 162*

**Ming/Qing China** — Wealthy families handled rural tax collection, collecting primarily in grain and later silver. After about 1580, wars and extravagant imperial spending drove the Ming into bankruptcy. *AMSCO p. 163*

**Russia — Peter the Great** — Peter replaced the land tax with a **head tax** (per person, 1718), increasing revenue but deepening the burden on peasants and intensifying serfdom.

**Tributes** — China, the Aztec Empire, and Songhai all collected tributes from subordinate states as a sign of political submission. Korea was a Chinese tributary state; the Aztecs stationed officials in each provincial capital to oversee tribute collection from local leaders. The [[Concept Notes/Tribute System|Tribute System]] combined economic extraction with political symbolism.

## Patterns and Exam Takeaways

All these methods share a common logic: rulers were **centralizing** power away from local elites and concentrating it in the monarch or the monarch's loyal agents. The differences lie in *how* — through bureaucrats, soldiers, hostages, divine claims, or spectacular buildings.

The limits of centralization also matter. Zamindars, tax farmers, and intendants all eventually became corrupt or semi-autonomous — a recurring pattern explaining why each empire eventually weakened. Understanding *why* these methods failed is just as important for the AP exam as understanding why they initially succeeded.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Legitimize vs. Consolidate:** The AP exam distinguishes between making people *accept* authority (legitimacy — divine right, architecture, religion) and actually *holding and exercising* control (consolidation — armies, bureaucrats, tax systems, hostages). A strong essay links both: a ruler who claimed divine right but could not fund an army would fall.
