# People, States & Movements

> [!exam-tip]
> For AP, focus less on biographical detail and more on **what each person represents** historically: Cortés = conquest and indigenous alliance dynamics; Pizarro = conquest + Andean silver; Las Casas = internal Spanish critique + source analysis; King Afonso = African rulers' agency and limits within the slave trade.

## Key People

**Hernán Cortés (1485–1547)**
Spanish conquistador who overthrew the Aztec Empire between 1519–1521 with a force of Spanish soldiers and a large coalition of indigenous allies — peoples who had been subjugated or threatened by Aztec tribute demands. Disease had already weakened the Aztec population. Cortés destroyed Tenochtitlán and founded Mexico City on its ruins, establishing the colony of New Spain. His success illustrates how conquest depended on indigenous alliance, epidemic disease, and Spanish military technology — not Spanish numbers alone.

> [!tip]
> Cortés's use of **indigenous allies** is an AP exam favorite. The Aztec Empire was not simply defeated by a handful of Spaniards; it collapsed because of epidemic disease, internal fractures, and a coalition of peoples eager to escape Aztec domination.

**Francisco Pizarro (c. 1471–1541)**
Spanish conquistador who conquered the Inca Empire beginning in 1532. Pizarro captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa, accepted a roomful of gold as ransom, and executed him anyway in 1533 — a pivotal act of treachery that paralyzed Inca resistance. Spanish control of the Inca Empire was complete by 1572. The silver of Potosí (in modern Bolivia) that Spain subsequently exploited would transform global trade.

**Moctezuma II (c. 1466–1520)**
Aztec emperor at the time of Spanish contact. His initial willingness to receive Cortés — possibly believing him to be the returning god Quetzalcóatl — and his subsequent capture and death fatally destabilized Aztec resistance. His role illustrates how leadership failure and misinterpretation of events could accelerate conquest.

**Atahualpa (c. 1500–1533)**
Last ruling Inca emperor. Captured by Pizarro at Cajamarca (1532); paid a massive gold ransom; executed regardless. His death decapitated Inca leadership and opened the Andean highlands to Spanish exploitation. His story is used on the AP exam to illustrate the brutal pragmatism of conquest.

**Bartolomé de las Casas (c. 1484–1566)**
Spanish priest, later Dominican friar, who served as a colonist in the Caribbean before becoming a sharp critic of Spanish colonial abuses. His *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (published 1552 in Seville) documented mass killings, forced labor, and the depopulation of indigenous populations. His advocacy contributed to the **New Laws of 1542**, which outlawed forcing indigenous people into slavery and attempted to ban the [[Concept Notes/Encomienda|encomienda]] system — with limited effectiveness, especially on New Spain's northern frontier.

Las Casas is critical for **AP sourcing skills**: he wrote for a Catholic Spanish audience, intending to shame the crown into reform. His purpose shapes how historians evaluate the account's accuracy and bias.

> [!warning]
> Las Casas is not simply a hero. He initially suggested importing African enslaved labor as a substitute for indigenous labor (though he later recanted). His writing must be read in light of his purpose and audience — a key AP sourcing skill.

**Prince Henry the Navigator (1394–1460)**
Portuguese royal who financed early Portuguese exploration along Africa's Atlantic coast and around the Cape of Good Hope. His patronage laid the groundwork for Portuguese trading post networks in Africa and the Indian Ocean. (See [[Unit-4.2/Exploration: Causes & Events Overview|Exploration: Causes & Events Overview]] for fuller context.)

**Vasco da Gama (c. 1460–1524)**
Portuguese explorer who reached the Swahili Coast of East Africa and India by sailing around Africa. His armed fleets seized control of Swahili city-states like Kilwa and Mombasa, devastating their commercial prosperity by force. (See [[Unit-4.2/Exploration: Causes & Events Overview|Exploration: Causes & Events Overview]].)

**King Afonso I of Kongo (r. 1509–1543)**
King of the Kingdom of the Kongo who initially permitted Portuguese slave trading but grew increasingly alarmed as the trade destabilized his kingdom. He protested in letters to the Portuguese king, arguing that slave raids were impossible to control: his subjects traded enslaved people for European goods without royal involvement, undermining his authority and draining his kingdom's population. His case illustrates the complex agency of African rulers — neither simply victims nor willing collaborators, but political actors navigating forces that ultimately exceeded their control.

**Pedro Menéndez de Avilés (1519–1574)**
Spanish explorer who founded **St. Augustine, Florida** in 1565 — the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in what would become the United States. His founding illustrates Spanish imperial ambitions in North America beyond Mexico and South America.

## Key States & Empires

**Spanish Empire (New Spain and Peru)**
The largest colonial empire in the Americas, comprising the former territories of the Aztec and Inca empires. Administered through viceroyalties directly subordinate to the Spanish crown. Its economy depended on silver mining (Zacatecas, Potosí) and coerced indigenous labor (encomienda, adapted mit'a). The Spanish Empire exemplifies the AP theme of **state building through maritime expansion** and **[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilism]]**.

**Portuguese Empire**
Portugal built a trading-post empire rather than a settler-colony empire. It controlled coastal enclaves in Africa (including the Kingdom of the Kongo trade), India (Goa), and Brazil, plus a chain of armed trading posts around the Indian Ocean. Portugal pioneered the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] by first bringing enslaved Africans to Europe as domestic servants in the late 1400s. By the era of large-scale plantation agriculture, Portuguese colonies (especially Brazil) received the largest share of enslaved Africans — 39% of the total.

**British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC)**
A [[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|joint-stock company]] that began as a commercial trading enterprise with the Mughal Empire in the 17th century. It eventually expanded inland using sepoys and political alliances, transforming from a trading company into a governing body. Britain's Seven Years' War victory (1763) drove France from India, leaving the EIC dominant. This trajectory — commercial enterprise becoming colonial government — is a key pattern for AP.

**Kingdom of the Kongo**
Central West African kingdom that engaged deeply with Portuguese missionaries and traders. King Afonso I initially converted to Christianity and permitted trade, but the slave trade ultimately undermined royal authority and destabilized the kingdom. A key example of African agency, adaptation, and the limits of resistance within the Atlantic system.

**Asante Empire**
West African state that grew in power and influence through participation in Atlantic trade networks, including selling enslaved people to Europeans. Its story illustrates how some African polities gained short-term strength from the Atlantic trade even as the broader trade devastated the continent.

## Key Movements

**Spanish Catholic Missions**
Religious conversion was one of Spain's stated justifications for conquest and colonization. Spanish missionaries worked to Christianize indigenous populations throughout the Americas, often coercively. The mission system was intertwined with the encomienda: indigenous people were supposed to receive Christian instruction in exchange for their labor — a justification that masked exploitation. Las Casas challenged this framework from within Catholic theology.

**The Atlantic Slave Trade**
Not a single moment but an escalating system spanning roughly 1500 to the mid-19th century. Began with Portuguese traders taking enslaved Africans to Europe in the late 1400s; expanded massively as plantation agriculture in Brazil and the Caribbean created insatiable labor demand. African kingdoms along the coast participated by raiding inland neighbors, selling captives for European goods. The trade forcibly transported millions of Africans and caused demographic, social, and cultural upheaval across Africa and the Americas. (See [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview|Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview]] for the full context.)

**Iroquois Confederacy and the Great Peace of Montreal (1701)**
The Iroquois were a powerful confederacy in northeastern North America that played European powers against each other. Initially allied with the British against the French, they came to see Britain as the greater threat and signed the **Great Peace of Montreal** with France in 1701 — a reminder that indigenous peoples were political actors, not passive bystanders in colonial rivalries.
