# Practice & Review

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 4.4 is a high-frequency exam topic. Expect MCQs on labor system comparisons, the causes of African enslavement, and sourcing Las Casas. SAQs and LEQs may ask you to explain how colonial empires were built and what economic systems fueled them.

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO AP World History, printed pp. 229–230*

**Questions 1–3** refer to a painting (c. 1760) by Juan Patricio Morlete Ruiz depicting a Spanish colonist, an indigenous American woman, and their child. The painting is an example of the *casta* genre — Spanish colonial paintings categorizing racial/social categories produced by colonial mixing.

---

**Question 1.** One difference between Spanish and British colonies reflected in this painting is that in Spanish colonies men were more likely to

A. be a farmer
B. be married to a woman much younger than himself
C. have a child with a Native woman
D. have accumulated considerable wealth

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The *casta* painting tradition reflects the distinctive Spanish colonial pattern of racial mixing between Spanish men and indigenous women — producing mestizo offspring. This was shaped by the demographics of conquest: most conquistadores were men, and the encomienda/hacienda system put Spanish men in close daily contact with indigenous labor. British colonies had far less intermarriage, partly because British settlement brought more families and partly because British colonial ideology maintained stricter racial separation. A, B, and D are not specifically contrasts between Spanish and British colonial patterns.

---

**Question 2.** In which region is this painting most likely set?

A. Hudson Bay
B. Gulf of Mexico
C. James River
D. Great Lakes

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Gulf of Mexico region (Mesoamerica / New Spain) is where Spanish colonization was most intensive. The *casta* painting tradition was centered in New Spain (Mexico). Hudson Bay was British/French territory; the James River (Jamestown) was British; the Great Lakes region was primarily French and later British — none of which produced the Spanish *casta* art tradition.

---

**Question 3.** Which of the following best describes the cultural exchange represented in the painting?

A. The man has adopted aspects of the culture of the woman.
B. The child is dressed in native-style clothing.
C. The plants are mostly of European origin.
D. The woman has adopted aspects of the culture of the man.

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> In *casta* paintings, indigenous women are typically depicted in European-style dress, reflecting the colonizers' cultural dominance and the expectation that indigenous people adopt Spanish customs. This is cultural exchange that flows predominantly in one direction — from colonizer to colonized. A is incorrect because the Spanish man retains his European identity and dress. B is the opposite of what *casta* paintings typically show. C may be true of Columbian Exchange botany but is not the primary cultural exchange represented in the figures' appearance.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO AP World History, printed pp. 230–231*

### SAQ 1 — Primary Source Analysis

**Passage:**
> "The widespread use of slavery was a systematic Spanish adaptation on the north Mexican frontier wherever nomadic Indians were encountered. Almost at the very moment that the New Laws [1542] made slavery illegal in Mesoamerican Mexico, the Mixtón War [1541–1542] in Nueva Galicia provided the initial reason for retaining the practice legally on the frontier for generations. The discovery of silver at Zacatecas sealed the fate of Spanish-Indian relations not only on the Gran Chichimeca, but throughout most of the North. The inevitable resistance by hunter-gatherers to Spanish domination, the shortage of labor, and the semiautonomous political power wielded by provincial and local authorities insured the survival of slavery and encomienda in northern New Spain into the eighteenth century."
>
> — José Cuello, *The Persistence of Indian Slavery and Encomienda in the Northeast of Colonial Mexico, 1577–1723* (1988)

**Part A:** Identify Cuello's argument in the passage.

> [!tip] Model Response — Argumentation
> Cuello argues that indigenous slavery and the encomienda system persisted in northern New Spain well beyond the New Laws of 1542 because of a combination of factors: the demand for labor driven by silver mining at Zacatecas, armed resistance by indigenous hunter-gatherers (used as justification for frontier enslavement), and the semiautonomous power of local and provincial Spanish authorities who could ignore or circumvent royal law. In short, economic incentives and decentralized power defeated legal reform.

**Part B:** Explain ONE way in which the treatment of enslaved people in Mexico was different from the treatment of people taken in the Indian Ocean trade in the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Enslaved people in Mexico (and the broader Atlantic system) were subject to **chattel slavery** — they were legally defined as property, their status was hereditary, and they had virtually no legal protections. By contrast, enslaved people in the Indian Ocean trade who ended up in Islamic societies had certain recognized rights, including the right to marry. Additionally, Indian Ocean enslaved people were more likely to work in urban environments as sailors, port laborers, or household servants — conditions that allowed somewhat more opportunity to form communities and work alongside free laborers — rather than the brutal plantation or mine labor characteristic of the Americas.

**Part C:** Explain ONE historical situation in the period 1450–1750, other than the one illustrated in the passage, in which European states transformed the lives of colonized states.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization/Causation
> The Portuguese seizure of the Swahili city-states of East Africa beginning with Vasco da Gama's 1498 invasion transformed the lives of populations in trading centers like Kilwa and Mombasa. These had been thriving commercial centers in the Indian Ocean trade network. The Portuguese took control through heavily armed ships and fortresses, replacing the cooperative, fee-based trading system with coerced military dominance. This devastated the economic prosperity of East African coastal communities and disrupted centuries-old commercial relationships — transforming thriving city-states into declining outposts of Portuguese commercial empire.

### SAQ 2 — Document-Based Reasoning

**Part A:** Identify ONE economic impact that the mit'a system had on Europe in the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The Spanish adaptation of the Inca mit'a — compelling Andean villages to send men to work in the silver mines of Potosí — produced massive quantities of silver that flowed to Spain and into the global economy. This silver funded Spain's military buildup and foreign wars, fueled inflation in Europe (the "Price Revolution"), and enabled European merchants to purchase luxury goods from China and India. In short, the coerced labor of Andean workers under the mit'a system transferred enormous wealth to Europe and made possible the first truly global commodity trade network.

**Part B:** Explain ONE similarity between trade in the Indian Ocean networks before the arrival of the Portuguese and trade after their arrival in the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity
> Both before and after Portuguese arrival, merchants in the Indian Ocean trade paid fees to states controlling key ports and sea lanes. Before the Portuguese, merchants operating through religious and ethnic trading networks paid port fees to the rulers of trading centers. After the Portuguese arrived and established their armed trading posts, merchants continued to pay for access to ports and passageways — the Portuguese simply extracted these fees through military coercion rather than cooperative agreement. The basic economic logic of paying tolls for access to trade routes persisted even as the power enforcing those payments changed.

**Part C:** Explain ONE difference between the economic development of Portugal and the economic development of China in the period 1450–1750.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Portugal pursued an **outward, expansionist economic strategy** during this period — financing long-distance exploration, establishing armed trading posts around the Indian Ocean, and building a colonial empire that extracted wealth from Brazil and West Africa through the slave trade. China's Ming Dynasty, by contrast, moved toward **economic restriction and inward consolidation** — prohibiting private foreign trade, destroying dockyards, limiting the size of ships, and reasserting Confucian cultural values over the commercial expansion associated with the preceding Mongol Yuan Dynasty. While Portugal was integrating itself into a global trade network, China was partially withdrawing from one.

## Think As a Historian: Purpose and Audience

*Source: AMSCO AP World History, printed p. 231*

**Primary Source:** Bartolomé de las Casas, *A Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (Seville, 1552):

> "In the Year 1509, the Spaniards sailed to the Islands of St. John and Jamaica . . . with the same purpose and design they proposed to themselves in the Isle of Hispaniola, perpetrating innumerable Robberies and Villanies as before whereunto they added unheard of Cruelties . . . . and Finally by afflicting and harassing them with un-exampled Oppressions and torments in the Mines, they spoiled and unpeopled this Contrey [country] of these Innocents. These two Isles containing six hundred thousand at least, though at this day there are scarce two hundred men to be found in either of them, the remainder perishing without the knowledge of Christian Faith or Sacrament."

> [!exam-tip]
> **Sourcing** is one of the seven AP historical thinking skills. On the DBQ, you must explain how a document's historical situation, purpose, point of view, or audience affects its usefulness for a given argument — not just identify these features.

**Question 1.** For what audience did Las Casas write these accounts? What does that audience suggest about his purpose for writing?

> [!tip] Model Response — Sourcing
> Las Casas wrote for a Spanish Catholic audience — particularly the Spanish crown and church officials. This audience shapes his purpose: he was not writing a neutral chronicle, but an indictment designed to shame Catholic rulers into reform. By framing indigenous suffering in explicitly Catholic terms (dying "without the knowledge of Christian Faith or Sacrament"), he appealed to his audience's religious guilt. His goal was to use moral suasion — within the Catholic framework his audience shared — to force policy change, as he had done successfully with the New Laws of 1542.

**Question 2.** Suppose these accounts had been written by a visitor from a competing European power eager to remove the Spanish from the Americas and take over the land for itself. What might be that author's audience and purpose?

> [!tip] Model Response — Perspective/Purpose
> A Protestant Dutch or English author writing for a Protestant European audience would have a very different purpose: not to reform Spanish colonialism, but to *delegitimize* Spain entirely in order to justify taking over its colonial territories. Where Las Casas wanted Spain to rule *better*, this hypothetical author would want readers to conclude that Spain should not rule *at all* — and that Protestant powers would make superior colonizers. This is precisely how Las Casas's accounts were weaponized in Protestant Europe to construct the "Black Legend" of uniquely Spanish brutality, a narrative that conveniently served English and Dutch imperial ambitions.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

**In one to three paragraphs, explain how the empires of European states were established between 1450 to 1750 and what economic and labor systems fueled them.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Synthesis
> European maritime empires between 1450 and 1750 were built through a combination of naval technology, political exploitation of existing rivalries, and epidemic disease. In the Americas, Spain conquered the Aztec and Inca empires using superior military equipment (steel, gunpowder), indigenous allies who resented Aztec and Inca domination, and the devastating advantage of diseases like smallpox that had already weakened indigenous populations. Portugal built a different kind of empire — a network of armed trading posts along the coasts of Africa, India, and Asia — using naval firepower to coerce existing merchants rather than establishing large settler colonies. Both models reflected the mercantilist economic logic of the era: empires existed to extract wealth for the mother country, whether through silver, spices, or enslaved labor.
>
> The economic systems that fueled these empires evolved as the demand for labor outpaced the supply. Spain initially imposed the encomienda system — compelling indigenous people to work for Spanish landowners in exchange for Christian instruction — but indigenous population collapse from disease made this insufficient. The Spanish adapted the Inca mit'a into coerced mine labor at Potosí, driving the silver production that funded Spain's global power. As indigenous labor proved inadequate, European powers turned to African enslaved labor — a system already practiced on a smaller scale by Arab merchants and within Africa itself, but now massively expanded into the chattel slavery system of the Atlantic world, in which enslaved people were legally property and their status passed to their children.
>
> The consequences were transformative and devastating. The Atlantic slave trade forcibly transported millions of Africans to the Americas over three centuries, causing demographic collapse in African home societies, creating new racial hierarchies in the colonies, and generating enormous wealth for European elites. The demographic, social, and cultural changes produced by conquest and slavery shaped the societies of the Americas, Africa, and even Europe in ways that persisted long after the formal colonial period ended.
