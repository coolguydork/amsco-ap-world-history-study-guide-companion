# Maritime Empires Link Regions

**Essential Question:** How were the empires of European states established between 1450 to 1750, and what economic and labor systems fueled them?

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 4.4 is one of the most heavily tested in Unit 4. Expect questions on **encomienda**, **chattel slavery vs. indentured servitude**, **[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilism]]**, **the Middle Passage**, and **how indigenous population collapse drove African enslavement**. Know how the Atlantic system compares to the Indian Ocean slave trade.

## Big Picture

European states, driven by political, religious, and economic rivalries, built maritime empires after 1450. They administered trading posts in Africa and Asia and established full colonies in the Americas. These empires depended on **coercive labor systems** — [[Concept Notes/Encomienda|encomienda]], chattel slavery, indentured servitude, and an adapted mit'a — to generate wealth, especially through silver and plantation agriculture. (AMSCO pp. 218–228)

## Trading Posts in Africa and Asia

Portugal led the earliest expansion into Africa. Prince Henry the Navigator financed expeditions along Africa's Atlantic coast and around the Cape of Good Hope. With local rulers' cooperation, Europeans set up **trading posts** along African coasts. Some African rulers sold enslaved people to Europeans in exchange for gunpowder and cannons — a military advantage that intensified raiding of neighboring villages.

The Kingdom of Dahomey grew powerful by raiding other villages to capture enslaved people for sale to European merchants. Portuguese explorers also pushed into the Kingdom of the Kongo and Benin, leaving traces of European cultural influence in West and Central African artwork. The Asante Empire and Kingdom of the Kongo grew in influence through participation in trade networks.

On East Africa's Swahili Coast, Vasco da Gama's Portuguese forces (see [[Unit-4.2/Exploration: Causes & Events Overview|Exploration: Causes & Events Overview]]) invaded thriving city-states like Kilwa and Mombasa in 1498, seizing control through armed ships and fortresses — devastating the region's prosperity.

In India, several European powers established coastal trading posts competing for Mughal trade:

- **Portugal** controlled Goa on India's southwestern coast
- **Britain's East India Company (EIC)** began trading relations with the Mughals in the 17th century, eventually expanding inland using Indian private forces called **sepoys** and exploiting Hindu-Muslim tensions
- **France** controlled Pondicherry in southeastern India

Britain's victory in the **Seven Years' War** (ended 1763) drove France from India; Portugal held Goa until the mid-20th century.

## East Asian Responses: Isolation and Restriction

Japan and China both moved toward restricting outside influence — a sharp contrast to European expansion.

**Japan** tolerated Portuguese and Dutch traders and missionaries in the mid-16th century, and thousands converted to Christianity. When Christian converts began destroying Buddhist shrines, the Japanese government banned Christian worship in 1587. Over the following decades Japan expelled nearly all foreigners, banned most foreign books, and prohibited Japanese from traveling abroad. Only a small Dutch community was permitted on an island in Nagasaki harbor. Some trade with China continued through regional lords with sea access to Korea, Taiwan, and Okinawa.

**China's Ming Dynasty** restricted trade after Zheng He's voyages (15th century): it prohibited private foreign trade, destroyed dockyards, limited ship sizes, and rebuilt the Great Wall. These policies reflected a broader conservatism aimed at undoing Mongol Yuan influence — the Ming reemphasized Confucianism and reinvigorated the civil service exam system. Many trade restrictions were eventually reversed, and China resumed a central role in global commerce.

> [!tip]
> Japan and China's inward turn is a classic **continuity** to contrast with Europe's outward expansion. Both reactions were driven by internal political concerns, not economic weakness.

## Europeans in the Americas

Before European arrival, the **Aztec Empire** (Mexico) and **Inca Empire** (South America) each held 10–15 million people. European diseases caused catastrophic population collapse, fatally weakening both empires.

**Conquest of the Aztecs:** Hernán Cortés, allied with peoples the Aztecs had conquered, overthrew the Aztec by 1521. The Spanish destroyed Tenochtitlán and built **Mexico City** on its ruins, establishing the colony of **New Spain**.

**Conquest of the Incas:** Francisco Pizarro captured Inca emperor Atahualpa, accepted a room of gold as ransom, then executed him anyway in 1533. By 1572, Spain had completed its conquest of the Inca Empire.

**Treaty of Tordesillas (1494):** Spain and Portugal divided the Americas — Spain claimed all lands west of a meridian through eastern South America; Portugal claimed all lands east (Brazil). This diplomatic carve-up shaped colonial geographies for centuries.

Spain also explored North America. Pedro Menéndez de Avilés founded **St. Augustine, Florida** in 1565 — the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in what would become the United States.

**France vs. Britain in North America:** British settlers allied with the Iroquois (who had long clashed with French traders), but over time the Iroquois recognized the greater British threat. The Iroquois and French signed the **Great Peace of Montreal (1701)**. Britain ultimately drove France from Canada in the same global conflict (the Seven Years' War / French and Indian War) that expelled France from India.

## Economic Systems: Mercantilism and Silver

European powers adopted **mercantilism** — an economic system emphasizing government control through high tariffs, colonial acquisition, and the goal of exporting more than importing. Colonies existed primarily to supply wealth (especially silver and gold) to the mother country.

**Silver** transformed the Spanish Empire. After gold proved scarce in the Caribbean, silver discoveries in **Zacatecas** (Mexico) and especially **Potosí** (modern Bolivia) generated enormous wealth. The use of mercury to separate silver from ore dramatically increased productivity. A percentage of all silver production went directly to the Spanish crown, funding military expansion and foreign trade.

In the Indian Ocean, the Portuguese arrived with superior naval forces and disrupted existing trade networks — which had operated through religious and ethnic ties, not armed coercion. The Portuguese forced merchants to pay for access to sea lanes and ports under military threat. Despite this disruption, many Indian Ocean merchants continued operating through traditional networks. The Portuguese won a decisive naval victory at the **Battle of Diu (1509)** over a combined Gujarati-Mamluk-Venetian fleet.

> [!warning]
> Mercantilism is often confused with capitalism. Mercantilism = government-directed accumulation of bullion and trade surpluses through colonial extraction. Free-market capitalism develops later (Unit 5).

## Labor Systems

The colonial economy demanded labor. Different regions used different systems, often simultaneously:

| Labor Type | Region | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Encomienda** | Spanish Americas | Landowners (encomenderos) compelled indigenous labor in exchange for food/shelter; notorious brutality; Bartolomé de las Casas documented abuses; limited by New Laws (1542) |
| **Mit'a (adapted)** | Andes/Peru | Spanish transformed Inca communal labor obligation into coerced mine labor; villages compelled to send men to Potosí mines for low wages |
| **Hacienda system** | Spanish Americas | Agricultural estates using coerced indigenous labor for wheat, fruit, vegetables, sugar |
| **Chattel slavery** | Americas (plantations) | Enslaved people treated as property; bought and sold; no rights; predominantly African after indigenous populations collapsed |
| **Indentured servitude** | Primarily North America | Europeans contracted to work 5–7 years for passage; became free laborers afterward — made them less "ideal" for landowners than enslaved Africans |

**Why African enslaved labor?** Indigenous people died in massive numbers from European disease. Survivors knew the land and could escape more easily. Indentured servants became free after 7 years. Enslaved Africans could not escape as readily and remained enslaved for life — and their enslaved status passed to children under chattel slavery.

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the **continuity** here: slavery existed in Africa (especially enslaved women as household workers) and the Arab-Indian Ocean trade before Europeans arrived. But the **[[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] was uniquely large-scale and brutal**, creating a racially defined hereditary system of chattel slavery.

## The Atlantic Slave Trade and the Middle Passage

Africans were captured through raids — often by African coastal kingdoms raiding inland neighbors — and held in coastal **barracoons** ("slave castles"). From there, captives endured the **Middle Passage**: the grueling Atlantic crossing (roughly six weeks) in the dank cargo holds of ships with minimal food, water, or space. Up to half of captives died on some voyages; overall, 10–15% perished before reaching the Americas. Rebellions were common but typically suppressed.

King Afonso of Kongo initially permitted the slave trade but grew alarmed when it became uncontrollable — depleting his population, undermining his authority, and allowing subjects to trade enslaved people for European goods without his involvement.

**Destination of enslaved Africans:** Portuguese colonies received the largest share (39%), followed by British West Indian and Spanish colonies (18% each), French colonies (14%), and British mainland colonies (6%).

## The Indian Ocean Slave Trade

Distinct from the Atlantic trade, East Africans were sold to buyers in northern Africa, the Middle East, and India — often as port laborers, household servants, sailors, or soldiers. Those in Islamic communities had some legal rights (e.g., the right to marry). The trade peaked in the 18th–19th centuries and left African cultural traces (words, music, customs) across Oman and India.

## Demographic, Social, and Cultural Changes

The plantation economy and Atlantic slave trade reshaped populations, social structures, and cultures across three continents:

- **Demographic:** African home countries suffered century-long population declines from slave raids; enslaved populations in the Americas grew
- **Social:** Slavery created rigid social hierarchies based on race; families were separated; more men than women were taken, making polygyny more common in some African societies
- **Cultural:** African-descended people shaped language, music, religion, and culture in the Americas; racial mixing produced new groups (mestizos, mulattos)
