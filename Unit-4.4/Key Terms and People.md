# Topic 4.4 – Key Terms and People

**Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections | Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)**

## Labor Systems (Most Testable)

**Chattel Slavery**
A system in which enslaved people are treated as property — bought, sold, inherited, and with essentially no legal rights. *Significance*: The dominant labor system on sugar and tobacco plantations in the Americas; the basis of the transatlantic slave trade. Distinguished from other forms of slavery or coerced labor by the *property* status of the enslaved person.

**Encomienda**
A Spanish colonial system in which Spanish landowners (encomenderos) were granted the right to extract labor from a set number of indigenous people in exchange for providing them food, shelter, and Christian instruction. *Significance*: The first major Spanish colonial labor system; notorious for its brutality despite its theoretical protections. Gradually replaced by the hacienda system.

**Encomenderos**
Spanish landowners who held encomiendas — the right to compel indigenous labor. They were often conquistadors rewarded for conquest.

**Hacienda System**
Large agricultural estates in Spanish America where indigenous and later mestizo workers performed coerced labor to produce wheat, vegetables, sugar, and other crops. *Significance*: Replaced the encomienda as the dominant labor system over time; created a landed elite class that would dominate Latin American politics for centuries.

**Mit'a System**
An Incan labor obligation system requiring young men to devote time to public works projects. Spanish authorities in Peru adapted it into a coerced labor system, compelling villages to send quotas of male workers to silver mines at Potosí and elsewhere. *Significance*: Example of how Europeans adapted existing indigenous labor systems for colonial exploitation.

**Indentured Servitude**
A system in which a person contracted to work for a specified period (typically 7 years) in exchange for passage to a new location, room, and board. After completing the indenture, the person was free. *Significance*: Used primarily in North American English colonies to recruit European workers, especially for tobacco cultivation. Less efficient than chattel slavery from a plantation owner's perspective (workers became free; could resist by surviving their contract).

**Coercive Labor System**
Any system that compels people to work under threat of violence or legal sanction, without free choice. Includes chattel slavery, the encomienda, hacienda, and mit'a systems. All were used in the Americas to extract wealth for European colonizers.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to compare labor systems. Know these key distinctions:
> - Chattel slavery: people ARE property
> - Encomienda: people owe labor but are not technically owned
> - Mit'a: community obligation converted to colonial extraction
> - Indentured servitude: time-limited contract labor with eventual freedom
> All were coercive; all served European economic interests.

## Colonial Administration Terms

**Viceroys**
Spanish royal appointees who served as administrators and representatives of the Spanish crown in colonial territories (e.g., Viceroy of New Spain, Viceroy of Peru). *Significance*: The key mechanism of Spanish colonial governance; viceroys had enormous local power, partially checked by audiencias.

**Audiencias**
Royal courts established in Spanish colonies where colonists could appeal viceroys' decisions. *Significance*: A check on viceroy power and part of Spain's attempt to maintain direct royal control over colonies from a great distance.

**Colonies**
Lands settled by immigrants from a home country, claimed by and administered for the benefit of the home country. Distinguished from trading posts (no large-scale settlement) and from protectorates (indirect control).

**Sepoys**
Indian soldiers trained and equipped in European military style, employed by the British East India Company. *Significance*: The EIC used sepoys to project military power inland in India without deploying European troops, enabling expansion of British commercial and political influence.

## Key Political Concepts

**Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**
Agreement between Spain and Portugal dividing the world along a north-south meridian through eastern South America. Spain claimed all lands to the west; Portugal claimed all lands to the east. *Significance*: Put Brazil under Portuguese rule while giving Spain the rest of the Americas; explains why Brazil is Portuguese-speaking.

**New Spain**
The Spanish colony established after Cortés's conquest of the Aztec Empire (1521). Included modern Mexico and much of Central America. Capital: Mexico City (built on the ruins of Tenochtitlan). *Significance*: The heart of Spain's American empire and the source of enormous silver wealth.

**Seven Years' War (1756–1763)**
A global conflict fought between Britain and France (and their allies) on five continents. Britain's victory drove France out of both India and Canada, establishing Britain as the dominant colonial power. *Significance*: A turning point in the competition between European maritime empires.

## Key African States and Concepts

**Kingdom of Dahomey**
A West African kingdom that grew powerful by raiding neighboring villages, enslaving captives, and selling them to European merchants. *Significance*: Example of an African state that actively participated in and benefited from the slave trade — at the expense of neighboring societies.

**Asante Empire**
A powerful West African empire that participated in Atlantic trade and grew in wealth and influence during the slave trade era, though it also limited European direct presence (the British trading posts in West Africa were constrained by Asante power).

**Kingdom of the Kongo**
Central West African kingdom that initially cooperated with Portuguese traders. King Afonso I attempted to control slave trading but found that European goods enabled his subjects to trade independently of his authority, undermining royal power. *Significance*: Illustrates the political disruption the slave trade caused even for African rulers who initially participated.

**Middle Passage**
The ocean crossing from West Africa to the Americas — the middle segment of the triangular trade voyage. Conditions were horrific: enslaved Africans were packed into ships with minimal food, water, and space; disease was rampant; 10–15% of captives died during the crossing. *Significance*: The Middle Passage represents one of the greatest human rights atrocities in history.

**Barracoons**
Coastal holding pens (sometimes called "slave castles") where captured Africans were held before being loaded onto ships. Located in West Africa at places like Île de Gorée (Senegal) and the Gold Coast (modern Ghana). *Significance*: These sites, now preserved as historical memorials, represent the Points of No Return for millions of Africans.

## Key People

**Hernán Cortés**
Spanish conquistador who overthrew the Aztec Empire (1521). See [[Unit-4.3/Key Terms and People]] for detail.

**Francisco Pizarro**
Spanish conquistador who captured and killed Inca ruler Atahualpa (1533) and completed the conquest of the Inca Empire. See [[Unit-4.3/Key Terms and People]] for detail.

**Atahualpa**
Last ruling Inca emperor. Captured by Pizarro in 1533; offered a room full of gold as ransom; was killed anyway. *Significance*: Symbol of the duplicity and violence of Spanish conquest.

**King Afonso of Kongo**
Ruled the Kingdom of the Kongo in the early 16th century. Initially cooperated with Portuguese slave traders, then tried unsuccessfully to limit slave raiding as it destabilized his kingdom and undermined his authority.
