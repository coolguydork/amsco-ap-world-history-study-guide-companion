# Key Terms & Concepts

> [!exam-tip]
> Labor system terminology is heavily tested. Know the **differences** between encomienda, mit'a, chattel slavery, indentured servitude, and the hacienda system — and the specific regions/contexts for each.

## Governance: African States

**Asante Empire**
West African state that grew in power through participation in Atlantic trade networks, including the slave trade. Its involvement with Europeans increased its regional influence. Also appears in Topic 6.3 (19th-century imperialism).

**Kingdom of the Kongo**
Central West African kingdom that engaged with Portuguese explorers, traders, and missionaries. King Afonso initially allowed the slave trade but eventually protested its destabilizing effects — his subjects bypassed royal authority to trade enslaved people for European goods, draining his kingdom's population. A key case study in African rulers' complex responses to European contact.

**Kingdom of Dahomey**
West African kingdom that grew powerful by raiding neighboring villages to capture enslaved people and selling them to European merchants in exchange for gunpowder and cannons — a military advantage that perpetuated further raiding.

**Barracoons**
Coastal holding pens ("slave castles") in West Africa where captured Africans were imprisoned before being loaded onto ships. The modern country of Ghana preserves several of these sites as "Points of No Return." The House of Slaves on Gorée Island (Senegal) is another surviving example.

## Governance: East Asian States

**Ming Dynasty**
Chinese dynasty (1368–1644) that responded to Zheng He's voyages by restricting foreign trade — prohibiting private foreign trade, destroying dockyards, limiting ship sizes, and rebuilding the Great Wall. These conservative policies reflected anti-Mongol sentiment and a reassertion of Confucian values. Many restrictions were eventually reversed. (See [[Concept Notes/Civil Service Examination|Civil Service Examination]] for Ming exam system context.)

## Governance: Americas

**Aztec Empire**
Mesoamerican empire of 10–15 million people before Spanish arrival. Overthrown by Cortés and indigenous allies by 1521. Its capital Tenochtitlán was destroyed and replaced by Mexico City. (See [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|The Americas Overview]] for pre-contact context.)

**Inca Empire**
South American empire of 10–15 million people before Spanish arrival. Conquered by Pizarro beginning 1532; Spanish control completed by 1572. Its mit'a labor system was adapted into coerced silver mine labor at Potosí.

**New Spain**
Spanish colonial territory established in Mexico after the conquest of the Aztec Empire (1521). Governed through a viceroyalty system directly subordinate to the Spanish crown.

**Mexico City**
Capital of New Spain, built by the Spanish on the ruins of the Aztec capital Tenochtitlán.

**Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**
Diplomatic agreement between Spain and Portugal dividing the Americas: Spain received all lands west of a meridian through eastern South America; Portugal received Brazil and lands to the east. Set the framework for colonial empire-building in the Western Hemisphere.

**Hispaniola**
Island (now Haiti and Dominican Republic) where Columbus first believed gold was plentiful. The site of early Spanish colonization and the first large-scale enslaving of indigenous Taíno people.

**Conquistadores**
Spanish soldier-explorers who carried out the conquest of the Americas. Many were rewarded with land grants (encomiendas or haciendas) and became encomenderos. Their violence and the diseases they brought devastated indigenous populations.

**Viceroyalties**
Administrative units of the Spanish colonial empire (e.g., Viceroyalty of New Spain, Viceroyalty of Peru). Each was governed by a viceroy directly representing the Spanish crown. A key mechanism of colonial governance.

## Economy: Products

**Silver**
The primary source of Spanish colonial wealth after gold proved scarce in the Caribbean. Mined at Zacatecas (Mexico) and especially Potosí (modern Bolivia). Mercury amalgamation made extraction more profitable. Silver flowed to Spain (funding military and trade), to China (which demanded silver for its economy), and into global commerce — making it the first truly global commodity.

## Economy: Economic Systems

**Mercantilism**
The dominant economic theory of European colonial powers in this period. Key principles: government control of the economy through high tariffs; colonies exist to supply raw materials and precious metals to the mother country; goal is to export more than import (favorable balance of trade). Spain used American silver to build its military and fund foreign trade.

**Colonies**
Lands settled by immigrants from a home country and subordinated to that country's political and economic control. In mercantilism, colonies primarily serve the mother country's economic interests. Distinct from trading posts, which don't require large settler populations.

**Trading Posts**
Small forts or commercial stations established on the coasts of Africa, India, and Asia. Unlike colonies, they focused on commerce rather than settlement. The Portuguese string of armed trading posts in the Indian Ocean disrupted existing ethnic/religious trading networks by adding military coercion to commercial exchange.

## Economy: Labor Systems

**Indentured servitude**
A labor system in which individuals (primarily Europeans in the early colonial period) contracted to work for a specified period — typically 5–7 years — in exchange for passage, room, and board. Upon completion, they became free laborers. Planters considered them less desirable than enslaved workers because they earned freedom and struggled with American climates and conditions.

**Chattel slavery**
A labor system in which enslaved people are legally defined as property to be bought, sold, and inherited. Distinguished from other forms of slavery by its hereditary nature (children of enslaved mothers were born enslaved) and its racial basis in the Americas. The foundation of plantation agriculture across the Atlantic world.

**Encomienda**
A Spanish colonial labor system granting a landowner (encomendero) the right to compel indigenous labor in exchange for food, shelter, and Christian instruction. Notorious for extreme brutality; often functioned as forced labor with no meaningful compensation. Criticized by Bartolomé de las Casas; theoretically limited by the New Laws (1542), though abuses continued — especially on the northern frontier of New Spain into the 18th century.

**Encomenderos**
Spanish colonial landowners who held encomienda grants, giving them authority over indigenous labor. Many were conquistadores rewarded for their role in conquest.

**Coercive labor system**
A broad term for any labor arrangement based on force rather than free consent. Encomienda, the adapted mit'a, and chattel slavery are all coercive labor systems. The AP exam uses this term to group them conceptually.

**Hacienda system**
Spanish colonial agricultural estates on which landowners developed wheat, fruit, vegetable, and sugar production using coerced indigenous labor. Unlike encomienda (which was tied to labor grants), haciendas involved actual land ownership. The mixing of Spanish men and indigenous women on haciendas produced significant mestizo populations — a key demographic distinction from British colonies.

**Mit'a system (adapted)**
The Inca mit'a was a communal labor obligation in which young men gave a portion of labor to public works. Spanish colonial authorities in Peru transformed it into coerced mine labor: villages were required to send a percentage of adult men to work in the Potosí silver mines for minimal wages and in dangerous conditions. A classic example of how Europeans adapted existing indigenous institutions for colonial exploitation.

**Middle Passage**
The middle leg of the triangular Atlantic trade: the ocean crossing from West Africa to the Americas. Captive Africans were packed into ship cargo holds with minimal food, water, or space. The journey took approximately six weeks; up to half of captives died on some voyages. Overall, 10–15% of all enslaved Africans transported died during the Middle Passage. Rebellions were common but almost always suppressed.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse the **Middle Passage** (ocean crossing, Africa → Americas) with the full triangular trade route. The "middle" refers to the second leg: Europe → Africa → Americas → Europe.

**Sepoys**
Indian soldiers trained and employed by the British East India Company to help extend British political control inland in India. They represented a key mechanism by which a commercial company (not just a government) expanded European territorial power.

**Mestizos**
People of mixed Spanish and indigenous American ancestry, a new demographic group produced by the colonial encounter. Their emergence reflected both the gendered nature of conquest (most conquistadores were men) and the social mixing that made Spanish colonies demographically distinct from British ones.

**Mulattos**
People of mixed African and European ancestry in the colonial Americas. Like mestizos, they formed a distinctive social stratum in colonial racial hierarchies.

## AP Historical Reasoning

**Purpose and Audience (Sourcing)**
A key AP document analysis skill: identifying the **intended audience** of a source helps reveal its **actual purpose**, which may differ from its stated purpose. Example: Bartolomé de las Casas wrote *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* (1552) for a Spanish Catholic audience — suggesting his purpose was to shame the Spanish crown into reform, not simply to chronicle events.
