# Topic 4.4 – Maritime Empires Link Regions

**Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections | Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)**

## Learning Objectives

- Explain how European states established maritime empires and trading posts in Africa, Asia, and the Americas.
- Identify the economic and labor systems that fueled maritime empires.
- Understand the causes and consequences of the transatlantic slave trade.
- Analyze how European expansion affected existing trade networks and political structures.

## The Big Picture

By 1600, European maritime powers had established three overlapping types of overseas presence:

1. **Trading Posts in Africa and Asia**: Small fortified outposts at strategic coastal locations, controlling trade without governing large territories (the Portuguese model)
2. **Colonial Empires in the Americas**: Large-scale territorial conquest and settlement, with systems of forced labor extracting silver and agricultural wealth (the Spanish model)
3. **Plantation Systems**: Agricultural enterprises using enslaved African labor to produce cash crops for European markets (the Portuguese-Brazilian and later Caribbean model)

All three depended on state power, military force, and new economic systems that linked regions across the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans.

## Trading Posts in Africa and Asia

### West Africa

Portuguese explorer Prince Henry the Navigator financed expeditions along Africa's Atlantic Coast beginning in the 1430s. Portuguese and later other European traders set up posts along Africa's coasts **with the cooperation of some local rulers** — a crucial point. Some African rulers saw trade with Europeans as an economic and military opportunity:

- Local rulers traded enslaved people to Europeans in exchange for **gunpowder and cannons**, giving them military advantages over neighboring groups
- The **Kingdom of Dahomey** grew powerful by raiding neighboring villages, enslaving people, and selling them to European merchants
- The **Kingdom of the Kongo** and the **Asante Empire** participated in Atlantic trade, initially growing in wealth and influence as a result

**The dual nature of African involvement** is important: some African states were willing participants in the slave trade for their own political and economic reasons. But this does not diminish the catastrophic harm the slave trade inflicted on African societies as a whole.

In East Africa, the Portuguese arrived with heavily armed ships and forcibly took over the flourishing Swahili city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa) in 1498, destroying thriving commercial centers that had long been part of the [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Trade]] network.

### Asia: Japanese Resistance

Japan tolerated the first Portuguese and Dutch traders and missionaries from the mid-16th century. But resistance to foreign influence grew:
- **1587**: Japanese government banned Christian worship services
- **1600s–1630s**: Japan expelled nearly all foreigners, banned most foreign books, and prohibited Japanese from traveling abroad
- **Exception**: Dutch merchants were permitted to live on a small island in Nagasaki harbor in near-total seclusion; Japan also continued some trade with China through regional lords

This policy of **sakoku** (near-isolation) lasted over two centuries. Europeans and Americans would not force their way back in until the mid-19th century.

> [!exam-tip]
> Japan's deliberate near-isolation is a great example to use in comparison essays. Japan *chose* to limit European contact; China partially did as well (the Ming restricted foreign trade). Compare these choices with the Indian Ocean world, where existing merchants adapted rather than excluding Europeans.

### Asia: Ming China's Response

After the voyages of Zheng He (1405–1433), the Ming Dynasty tried to limit outside influence by restricting trade:
- Prohibited private foreign trade
- Destroyed some dockyards
- Limited ship sizes
- Began reconstructing the Great Wall

These policies reflected the Ming's **conservative Confucian reaction** against Mongol influence (the Ming wanted to undo the cosmopolitan Yuan Dynasty). Many trade restrictions were eventually reversed, and China resumed its important role in global trade — particularly as a massive consumer of American silver.

### European Rivalries in India

Multiple European powers competed for footholds in India:
- **Britain**: British East India Company (EIC) established commercial relationships with the Mughal Empire in the 17th century; set up small coastal forts
- **Portugal**: Controlled Goa on India's southwest coast
- **France**: Controlled Pondicherry in southeastern India

The EIC initially operated as a simple trading company, limited by Mughal power. But it gradually expanded by exploiting tensions between Muslim and Hindu populations, making treaties with local rulers, and deploying **sepoys** (European-trained Indian soldiers). By the 18th century, the EIC controlled much of the subcontinent — a process of creeping political control that would ultimately lead to formal British colonial rule.

> [!tip]
> The British East India Company is a fascinating example of a **joint-stock company** that gradually became a governing power. It started as a profit-driven commercial enterprise and ended up running an empire. This blurring of commercial and political power is characteristic of the period.

The **Seven Years' War (1756–1763)** — fought on five continents — resulted in Britain driving France out of both India and Canada.

## Europeans in the Americas

### Conquest of the Aztec and Inca Empires

Before Europeans arrived, the **Aztec Empire** (Mexico) and **Inca Empire** (South America) each had populations of 10–15 million. European diseases had already devastated these populations before military conquest.

**Aztec Conquest**: Hernán Cortés's forces overthrew the Aztec by 1521, aided by indigenous allies (groups conquered by the Aztec who resented their rule), smallpox, and superior weaponry. Spain established **New Spain**, destroying Tenochtitlan and building Mexico City on its ruins.

**Inca Conquest**: Francisco Pizarro captured Inca ruler Atahualpa in 1533, demanded gold as ransom, then killed him anyway. Full conquest was complete by 1572.

**Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**: Spain and Portugal divided the Americas between them along a north-south meridian — Spain got everything to the west, Portugal got everything to the east (including Brazil).

### Spain and Silver

The discovery of enormous **silver deposits** in Mexico (Zacatecas) and Peru (Potosí) transformed Spanish colonialism. Silver was separated from ore using **mercury**, increasing profitability. By the late 16th century, Potosí was one of the largest cities in the world — a mining metropolis in the Andes.

Silver flowed from the Americas to Spain and onward to China (via Manila galleons across the Pacific). The Chinese government made silver its main currency. This made silver the first truly global commodity and integrated the Americas into the world economy.

**Labor for silver**: Spanish authorities adapted the traditional Incan **mit'a system** — a labor obligation requiring young men to work on public projects — into a coercive labor system. Villages were forced to send a percentage of their male population to do dangerous work in the mines for minimal wages.

### Colonial Administration

Spanish royalty appointed **viceroys** as administrators and representatives of the crown in colonial territories. To prevent viceroys from becoming too independent, Spain established **audiencias** (royal courts) where colonists could appeal viceroys' decisions. But slow communication between Europe and the Americas made direct royal control difficult in practice.

## Labor Systems

The colonial economy relied on multiple forms of coerced labor:

| Labor System | Description | Who Used It |
|-------------|-------------|------------|
| **Chattel Slavery** | Enslaved people treated as property; bought and sold | Americas (especially sugar and tobacco plantations) |
| **Encomienda** | Indigenous people required to work for Spanish landowners in exchange for food, shelter, and (supposed) Christian instruction | Spanish Americas, early colonial period |
| **Hacienda System** | Large agricultural estates using coerced indigenous labor for crops like wheat, sugar, and vegetables | Spanish Americas |
| **Mit'a System** | Adapted Incan labor obligation; villages sent quota of workers to mines | Spanish Peru |
| **Indentured Servitude** | European workers contracted to work 7 years in exchange for passage; then became free | North American colonies, especially English |
| **Serfdom** | Peasants bound to land; not legally free | Europe, Russia; see [[Unit-4.6/Challenges Overview]] |

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse encomienda and chattel slavery. Under encomienda, indigenous people were compelled to work but were not technically "owned" — they were supposed to receive food, shelter, and Christian instruction. Chattel slavery meant enslaved people were legally property with no rights. In practice, both systems were brutal, but they are legally and theoretically distinct. The AP exam sometimes asks about this difference.

## The Transatlantic Slave Trade

### Why Africans?

Several factors converged to make Africans the target of slave raids after 1450:
1. **Indigenous labor collapse**: Disease killed potential indigenous workers; survivors could escape because they knew the terrain and could blend into native populations
2. **European indentured servants**: Lasted only 7 years; not enough workers; not suited to tropical conditions
3. **African immunity**: Africans had some immunity to tropical diseases like malaria, making them better able to survive plantation conditions than Europeans
4. **Existing African slave trade**: Arab merchants had bought enslaved East Africans for centuries; some African states already participated in slave raiding
5. **Commercial opportunity**: Some African coastal states (Dahomey, Kongo) found slave trading profitable

### The Middle Passage

Captured Africans were first held in coastal fortifications called **barracoons** (or "slave castles"). Then they were crammed into ships for the **Middle Passage** — the Atlantic crossing to the Americas, taking about six weeks. Conditions were horrifying: minimal food, water, and space; disease rampant; up to half of a ship's captives might die.

Over the centuries of the trade, 10–15% of all African captives died before reaching the Americas.

### Demographic Consequences

The slave trade transported about 10–15 million Africans across the Atlantic. More than 90% went to the Caribbean and South America (primarily for sugar cultivation). Only about 6% went to British North America.

This demographic shift had cascading effects:
- Some African communities lost population for a century
- The loss of predominantly male captives created gender imbalances; polygyny increased
- The trade made some African states (Dahomey, Oyo) economically dependent on European goods, stunting more complex economic development
- New communities of enslaved Africans in the Americas created the African Diaspora

### The Indian Ocean Slave Trade

Alongside the Atlantic slave trade, a long-running slave trade operated in East Africa. Slaves from eastern Africa were sold overland or by sea to buyers in North Africa, the Middle East, and India. This trade differed from the Atlantic trade: Indian Ocean enslaved people were more likely to work as urban laborers, household servants, sailors, or soldiers — often with some legal rights in Islamic contexts — rather than on plantation agriculture.

## AP Themes

**Economic Systems**: The colonial economy created new integrated global markets connecting Europe, the Americas, Africa, and Asia through silver, sugar, and enslaved labor.

**Governance**: European states exercised direct control (viceroys, audiencias) over American colonies; in Asia and Africa, they typically operated through trading posts and alliances with local rulers rather than territorial administration.

**Social Structures**: New racial hierarchies formed in the Americas based on the encounter of Europeans, Africans, and indigenous peoples. See [[Unit-4.7/Social Hierarchies Overview]].

**Human Migration**: The forced migration of millions of Africans was the defining human movement of this era. The African Diaspora permanently altered the Americas' demographic and cultural landscape.
