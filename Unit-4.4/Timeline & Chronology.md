# Timeline & Chronology

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam rarely asks for precise dates, but it does expect you to sequence events and understand causation across time. Focus on the **logical order**: exploration → conquest → encomienda → indigenous collapse → African enslavement → plantation economy.

## Key Dates

| Date | Event | Significance |
|------|-------|--------------|
| **1494** | Treaty of Tordesillas | Spain and Portugal divide the Americas along a meridian; Portugal gets Brazil, Spain gets the rest. First diplomatic framework for Western Hemisphere colonization. |
| **1509** | Battle of Diu | Portuguese defeat combined Gujarati-Mamluk-Venetian fleet in the Arabian Sea; establishes Portuguese naval dominance in the Indian Ocean. |
| **1519–1521** | Cortés conquers Aztec Empire | Indigenous allies + epidemic disease + Spanish steel enabled conquest; New Spain established. Tenochtitlán destroyed; Mexico City built on its ruins. |
| **1532** | Pizarro begins conquest of Inca Empire | Atahualpa captured at Cajamarca; executed 1533. Spanish control completed by 1572. Silver of Potosí subsequently fuels global trade. |
| **c. 1500s** | Encomienda system established | Forced indigenous labor tied to land grants; justified by Christianization. Brutal in practice; modeled loosely on European manorial serfdom. |
| **1542** | New Laws passed | Las Casas's advocacy leads Spain to officially limit (not end) encomienda abuses and prohibit indigenous enslavement. Frequently ignored on New Spain's frontier. |
| **1552** | Las Casas publishes *A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies* | Documents Spanish cruelty for a Catholic Spanish audience. Fuels the "Black Legend" in Protestant Europe. Key sourcing text for AP. |
| **1565** | St. Augustine, Florida founded | Pedro Menéndez de Avilés establishes the oldest continuously occupied European settlement in what became the United States. Demonstrates Spanish reach into North America beyond Mexico. |
| **1587** | Japan bans Christian worship | Beginning of Japan's century-long move toward near-total isolation; expulsion of most foreigners completed by the 1630s. |
| **1600s** | Japan completes isolation | Foreigners expelled (except Dutch at Nagasaki); foreign books banned; Japanese prohibited from traveling abroad. Only limited trade with China via regional lords continues. |
| **17th c.** | British East India Company expands | EIC begins as a trading enterprise with the Mughal Empire; gradually expands inland using sepoys, treaties, and exploitation of Hindu-Muslim tensions. |
| **1701** | Great Peace of Montreal | Iroquois and French sign peace treaty after Iroquois shift away from British alliance; illustrates indigenous political agency in colonial rivalries. |
| **1763** | Britain wins Seven Years' War | France driven from India and Canada; Britain becomes dominant colonial power in both regions. EIC gains ascendancy in India. |

## Sequencing: How the Labor System Evolved

Understanding the **logic of transition** between labor systems is more important than memorizing dates:

1. **Conquest phase (1490s–1530s):** Spanish force indigenous people into labor through encomienda — a grant system modeled on European serfdom
2. **Andean adaptation (mid-1500s):** The Inca mit'a is transformed into coerced mine labor at Potosí — adapting an existing institution rather than inventing a new one
3. **Indigenous collapse:** Epidemic disease and brutal working conditions devastate the indigenous labor force; enslaved indigenous people escape more easily than Africans
4. **Indentured servitude proves inadequate:** European indentured servants struggle with climate; gain freedom after ~7 years; not a permanent solution
5. **African enslavement scales up (late 1500s onward):** Chattel slavery — already practiced by Portuguese in Europe and Atlantic islands — is massively expanded for plantation labor in Brazil and Caribbean
6. **Atlantic slave trade peak (17th–18th centuries):** 10–15 million Africans transported; 10–15% die during Middle Passage; plantation economies generate enormous European wealth

## Parallel Timelines: Asian Responses

| Period | Japan | China (Ming) |
|--------|-------|------|
| Mid-1500s | Tolerates Portuguese/Dutch traders and missionaries | Restricts trade after Zheng He voyages; bans private foreign trade |
| 1587 | Bans Christian worship | Rebuilds Great Wall; limits ship sizes |
| 1630s | Near-total isolation; Dutch allowed only in Nagasaki | Trade restrictions partially reversed; China resumes role in global commerce |
| Mid-19th century | Reopened by force (U.S. Commodore Perry, 1853) | Ongoing trade; later forced open by Opium Wars |

## AP Periodization Note

Topic 4.4 sits within **AP Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)** — the era of intensified global connectivity. The maritime empires built in this period created the conditions for:
- **Period 3** transformations: Industrial Revolution (Unit 5), Latin American revolutions (**Topic 5.2**), 19th-century imperialism (**Topic 6.2**)
- The global silver economy that connects Americas, Europe, and China
- The racial slavery system whose legacies extend through the 19th century and beyond
