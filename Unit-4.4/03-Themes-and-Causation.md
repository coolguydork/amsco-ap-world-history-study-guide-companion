# Themes and Causation — Unit 4.4: (4.4): Maritime Empires Established

#apwh #unit-4 #period-4

---

## Governance

The establishment of maritime empires required European states to develop new administrative tools for governing distant, unfamiliar territories. Spain created the Council of the Indies (1524) and the Casa de Contratación to regulate colonial commerce, and organized the Americas into viceroyalties governed by crown-appointed officials. In practice, however, the distance from Madrid meant that colonial elites — encomenderos, local officials, church authorities — exercised enormous de facto power. Portugal's trading-post model required less administrative apparatus: its feitorias were essentially fortified commercial outposts managed by small garrisons. Both systems struggled to balance the Crown's desire for control with the autonomy demanded by the people actually running the colonies.

---

## Economic Systems

The Spanish and Portuguese empires were built on fundamentally different economic logics. Spain extracted tribute and precious metals from conquered populations through coerced labor (encomienda, mita), with silver at [[Potosí]] being the cornerstone of the colonial economy. Portugal controlled the terms of trade at key commercial nodes in the Indian Ocean, taxing merchants and controlling the flow of spices without directly producing them. Both models were mercantilist in assumption — colonies existed to generate wealth for the metropole — but they achieved this through radically different means. The Spanish model created a colonial society of millions; the Portuguese model maintained a relatively thin European presence.

---

## Social Structures

The establishment of empires immediately created new social hierarchies. In Spanish America, peninsulares (Spanish-born) sat at the top; creoles (American-born Europeans), mestizos, Indigenous peoples, and enslaved Africans filled lower tiers. In Portuguese India, a small European class governed through local intermediaries, and intermarriage with local populations created a mixed Luso-Asian community. In both cases, race and place of birth determined social status and legal rights — a new form of hierarchy that differed from the class-based and religious hierarchies of medieval Europe.

---

## Cultural Developments

Empire-building created conditions for intense cultural exchange and suppression simultaneously. Spanish missionaries documented Indigenous languages, histories, and religious practices — often while actively destroying them. Indigenous peoples incorporated Catholic imagery into traditional ritual contexts, producing the syncretic religious cultures (Virgin of Guadalupe, Día de los Muertos) that persist today. In Asia, Jesuit missionaries attempted a more accommodating approach, adapting Christian practice to local cultural norms (the "Chinese Rites Controversy" reflects this tension). European colonial art — the colonial baroque of Latin America — blended European and Indigenous aesthetics in distinctive new forms.
