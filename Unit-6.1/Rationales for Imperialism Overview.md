# Rationales for Imperialism

**Topic 6.1 | AP Period 3 | AMSCO pp. 367–374**

Between roughly 1750 and 1900, industrial powers—especially in Western Europe, but also Japan—aggressively expanded overseas empires. They justified this imperialism through four overlapping sets of motives: nationalist competition, cultural and religious mission, scientific racism, and economic interest. These were not separate theories but reinforcing layers of the same imperial project.

## Nationalist Motives

The 19th century was the age of nationalism in Europe. With strong national identities came a desire for international prestige, and building overseas empires became one way for states to assert greatness on the world stage.

**Britain** led the way, expanding into Australia (New South Wales, 1788), the Indian subcontinent (complete British control by 1857), Sri Lanka, Burma, the Malay States, and parts of Borneo. **France**, humiliated by its defeat in the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), compensated by expanding into Algeria, Senegal, New Caledonia, and Indochina. **Italy and Germany**, newly unified in the late 19th century, each sought colonies for prestige and strategic advantage, beginning acquisition in the mid-1880s. **Spain**, once dominant in the first wave of colonialism, was greatly diminished by the 19th century.

**Japan** offers the most important non-European case. Through incursions into Korea, Japan provoked the Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) with China. Japan's decisive victory gave it control of Korea and Taiwan—known as **Formosa** since Portuguese colonization in the 16th century—signaling that imperial ambition was not limited to Europe.

> [!exam-tip]
> Japan's role as an imperialist power is frequently tested. Contrast it with China's failure to industrialize rapidly ([[Unit-5.8/Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview\|Topic 5.8]]). The Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) shows that Asian powers could be imperialists, not just victims—and that industrialization was the key variable separating the two.

## Cultural & Religious Motives

Imperialists framed expansion as a benevolent "civilizing mission." Rudyard Kipling's poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899) epitomized this view, describing colonized peoples as "half-devil and half-child" in need of European guidance. For France, this ideology was called the **mission civilisatrice** (civilizing mission). For Britain, missionaries like **David Livingstone** promoted the "3 Cs"—Christianity, Commerce, and Civilization—linking religious motives to commercial ones.

**Scientific racism** reinforced these attitudes. **Phrenologists** falsely claimed skull measurements proved the intellectual inferiority of Africans, Asians, and indigenous Americans. **Charles Darwin's** legitimate theory of evolution by natural selection was distorted into **Social Darwinism**—the idea that "survival of the fittest" applied to races and civilizations, making European dominance natural and inevitable. Darwin himself never endorsed this application of his work.

> [!warning]
> Social Darwinism is a *misapplication* of Darwin's theory. Darwin's work concerned biological species over millions of years; Social Darwinists falsely applied it to human races to justify conquest. The AP exam expects you to know this distinction clearly.

## Economic Motives

Industrialization created both the drive and the capacity for empire. Industrial nations needed raw materials—cotton, rubber, wool, jute, vegetable oils—and food supplies—wheat, tea, coffee, cocoa—for their factories and swelling cities. They also needed overseas markets for manufactured goods.

Britain had dominated global trade since the Napoleonic Wars, using settler colonies like Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa as both suppliers and buyers. As the **Second Industrial Revolution** progressed in the 1870s–1890s, Germany, France, the United States, and Japan challenged Britain's lead, intensifying competition for territories in Africa and Asia.

The **East India Company (EIC)** was the institutional vehicle for British economic dominance in India until 1857–58, when the British Crown dissolved it and assumed direct control. The earlier **Dutch East India Company (VOC)** had played the same role in Southeast Asia. These corporate colonial structures became models for later imperial governance worldwide.

> [!tip]
> Connect economic motives directly to Unit 5: industrialization (Topics 5.1–5.4) created the *need* for empire, while military and transport technology [[Unit-5.5/Technology in the Industrial Age Overview|Technology in the Industrial Age Overview]] created the *ability* to seize it. Causation runs directly from the Industrial Revolution into the Scramble for Africa.
