# People, States & Movements

**Topic 6.1 | AMSCO pp. 367–374**

**Rudyard Kipling (1865–1936)**
British novelist and poet raised in colonial India. His poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899) urged Western powers to accept the supposed duty of governing "inferior" peoples, describing the colonized as "half-devil and half-child." Whether Kipling personally endorsed this view is debated by historians, but the poem became the defining statement of cultural and paternalistic justifications for imperialism and the "civilizing mission" ideology across the English-speaking world.

> [!exam-tip]
> "The White Man's Burden" is a primary source the AP exam uses regularly. Always identify it as evidence of **cultural/paternalistic justifications** for imperialism—not primarily economic or nationalist ones. It also serves as evidence of the condescending racial attitudes that generated resistance (Topic 6.3).

**Charles Darwin (1809–1882)**
British naturalist who published *On the Origin of Species* (1859), establishing evolution by natural selection. His work was later distorted by Social Darwinists who applied "survival of the fittest" to racial hierarchies to justify conquest and colonial rule. Darwin never endorsed this application. On the AP exam, always distinguish Darwin's actual biological theory from Social Darwinism, which misuses it.

**David Livingstone (1813–1873)**
Scottish missionary-explorer who made three expeditions into central Africa, mapping the continent and calling for an end to the Arab slave trade. He promoted the "3 Cs"—Christianity, Commerce, and Civilization—as the framework for legitimate European engagement in Africa, linking religious duty to commercial expansion. His disappearance in 1869 sparked Henry Morton Stanley's famous search expedition, generating enormous European public interest in Africa and accelerating the push to colonize it.

**Jules Ferry (1832–1893)**
French prime minister (1880–81, 1883–85) who championed French colonial expansion. In his 1884 parliamentary speech, he argued that France had both the right and duty to civilize "inferior races" (cultural motive), that France needed colonies for trade routes and markets (economic motive), and that a great navy required overseas supply stations (strategic motive). His speech is a model AP primary source because it demonstrates how **multiple rationales operated simultaneously in one imperialist's argument**.

> [!exam-tip]
> Jules Ferry's 1884 speech appears on AP exams as a primary source. Practice identifying all three motives in the document: cultural superiority, economic necessity, and strategic/nationalist need. Ferry also explicitly invokes the Spanish conquistadors as a negative example—he argues that French imperialism is more humane—which itself reveals the self-justifying logic of the civilizing mission.

**Cecil Rhodes (1853–1902)**
British mining magnate and politician in southern Africa. Envisioned a continuous band of British territory from "Cape to Cairo." Founded the colony of Rhodesia (modern Zimbabwe and Zambia) and used diamond and gold mining profits to fund British expansion. Rhodes embodies the fusion of economic ambition and nationalist imperial vision—he is useful evidence when an AP essay asks you to illustrate the intersection of economic and nationalist motives.

**Japan as Imperial Actor**
Japan's role as an imperialist power—not just a target of Western imperialism—is a key comparative point for Unit 6. Fueled by nationalist ideology and rapid industrialization after the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration\|Meiji Restoration]] ([[Unit-5.8/Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview\|Topic 5.8]]), Japan fought and won the Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), acquiring control of Korea and Formosa (Taiwan). Japan's imperialism drew on the same nationalist and economic logic as European imperialism, demonstrating that these drives were not exclusively Western phenomena. This makes Japan essential for any AP compare or causation essay on 19th-century imperialism.
