# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 6.1 | AMSCO pp. 367–374**

**imperialism**
Policy by which one country extends its power over other territories through military force, economic control, or political dominance. The late 19th-century wave—often called "New Imperialism"—differed from earlier colonialism in its ideological intensity, industrial backing, and global scope, culminating in the Scramble for Africa and consolidation of Asian colonies.

**nationalism**
Ideology that a people sharing language, culture, and history should form their own state. In 19th-century Europe, nationalism drove both the unification of Germany and Italy and the competition for overseas empire: colonies became proof of national greatness. In Asia, nationalism equally fueled Japanese imperial ambitions and, later, anti-colonial resistance.

**Social Darwinism**
Misapplication of Darwin's evolutionary theory to human societies; argued that European "races" were biologically superior and naturally destined to dominate others. Used to justify conquest, racial segregation, and forced assimilation. Darwin never endorsed this use of his ideas, which are incompatible with the actual science of natural selection.

**Sino-Japanese War**
War fought 1894–95 between Japan and China over control of Korea. Japan's decisive victory ended Chinese dominance in Korea and gave Japan Taiwan (Formosa), demonstrating that Asian powers could be imperial aggressors as well as victims. A key signal of Japan's industrial and military rise relative to a weakening Qing China.

**Formosa**
Portuguese colonial name for Taiwan, in use from the 16th century until after World War II. Seized by Japan from China after the Sino-Japanese War (1895). Its succession of colonizers—Portuguese, Chinese, Japanese—illustrates multiple waves of imperialism across the early modern and modern periods.

**phrenologists**
Pseudoscientists who claimed to measure intelligence and character through skull size and shape; used their false findings to assert the intellectual inferiority of Africans, Asians, and indigenous Americans. A key example of "scientific racism"—deploying the language of science to justify racial hierarchy and imperial conquest.

**Charles Darwin**
British naturalist (1809–1882) whose theory of evolution by natural selection (*On the Origin of Species*, 1859) was later distorted by Social Darwinists to justify European racial superiority and imperial conquest. Darwin himself never endorsed this application. On the AP exam, always distinguish between Darwin's actual science and Social Darwinism.

**David Livingstone**
Scottish missionary-explorer (1813–1873) who traveled throughout central Africa promoting the "3 Cs"—Christianity, Commerce, and Civilization—as the framework for European engagement in Africa. His expeditions generated enormous public interest in Africa back in Europe and provided a moral-humanitarian framing for colonization as a duty rather than a conquest.

**[[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC)**
British joint-stock trading company (chartered 1600) that gradually acquired military and administrative control over the Indian subcontinent. After the Indian Rebellion (Sepoy Mutiny) of 1857, the British Crown dissolved the EIC and assumed direct rule of India in 1858. Demonstrates how corporate economic structures became instruments of formal imperial governance.

**Dutch East India Company (VOC)**
Dutch trading company (*Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie*, chartered 1602) that dominated the Southeast Asian spice trade and established colonial control over present-day Indonesia. One of the world's first multinational corporations; its joint-stock structure and blend of commercial and political power became a model for later European colonial enterprises.
