# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Topic 6.1 | AMSCO pp. 367–374**

## AP Themes in This Topic

**GOV — Governance and Imperial Ideology**
[[Concept Notes/Imperialism\|Imperialism]] required ideological legitimacy for domestic audiences. Imperialist governments justified expansion through nationalism (colonies = national prestige), economic necessity (markets and raw materials), and civilizing duty. Jules Ferry's 1884 parliamentary speech models how imperial policy was rationalized in democratic systems. The [[Concept Notes/Berlin Conference\|Berlin Conference]] (1884–85) institutionalized the Scramble for Africa through a diplomatic framework, showing how imperial *competition* was itself governed through European-only negotiation.

**CDI — Cultural Developments & Interactions**
The "civilizing mission" ideology created a specific cultural framework: Europeans presented their religion (Christianity), economic practices (capitalism, commerce), and cultural norms as inherently superior and their imposition as beneficial. Social Darwinism and scientific racism (phrenologists) gave these attitudes a pseudo-scientific veneer. These ideologies were not one-directional—they provoked resistance and cultural adaptation among colonized peoples, connecting this topic directly to Topic 6.3 (Indigenous Responses).

**ECN — Economic Systems**
Industrial economies generated demand for empire: factories needed raw materials, cities needed food, and manufacturers needed markets. The [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC) and [[Concept Notes/Dutch East India Company\|Dutch East India Company]] (VOC) demonstrate how corporate economic structures became instruments of imperial governance. The Second Industrial Revolution intensified competition among Britain, France, Germany, the United States, and Japan, accelerating the scramble for territory.

## AP Historical Reasoning: Causation

Topic 6.1 is the foundational **Causation** topic for Unit 6. The AP exam asks students to identify, explain, and evaluate the multiple causes of 19th-century imperialism. These causes reinforced each other rather than operating independently.

| Cause | Core Logic | Key Evidence |
|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Nationalism | Empire = national prestige; competition among European states | Newly unified Germany and Italy seeking colonies; France post–Franco-Prussian War |
| Cultural superiority / civilizing mission | Europeans have a duty to govern and "uplift" non-Europeans | Kipling's poem; Jules Ferry's speech; missionary activity (Livingstone) |
| Scientific racism / Social Darwinism | Biological "proof" of European superiority justifies conquest | Phrenologists' skull studies; Darwin misappropriated |
| Economic demand | Industrial nations need raw materials, food, and markets | EIC in India; Second Industrial Revolution intensifying competition |
| Military-technological advantage | Industrial weapons and transport make conquest feasible | Repeating rifles, steamships, telegraphs (see Topic 5.5) |

> [!tip]
> On a DBQ or LEQ evaluating causes of imperialism, note that these motives are *not mutually exclusive*—they reinforced each other. A single imperialist like Jules Ferry simultaneously held nationalist, cultural, and economic motives. The strongest essays explain *how* causes interacted, not just list them separately.

## Think As a Historian: The Berlin Conference (1884–85)

The **Berlin Conference** was convened by European powers to establish rules for partitioning Africa. No African representatives attended. The General Act of the Conference (1885) articulates all three motivational contexts from Topic 6.1:

- **Nationalist/political context**: concern about "disputes which might arise from new acts of occupation" — imperial rivalry among European powers required regulation
- **Economic context**: promoting "development of trade" and "free navigation" on African rivers — raw materials and commercial access were explicit goals
- **Ideological context**: commitment to the "moral and material well-being of the native populations" — the civilizing mission written into official diplomatic language

> [!warning]
> The Berlin Conference is often misunderstood as the moment Europeans *began* colonizing Africa. In fact, colonization was already underway—the Conference formalized rules for a process already in motion. It is better analyzed as evidence of *nationalist competition* among European powers than as evidence of cooperation or humanitarian concern.

## Historical Perspectives

Historians debate the primary driver of New Imperialism, and this debate shapes how primary sources are interpreted:

The **economic/Marxist interpretation** (J.A. Hobson, 1902; Lenin, 1916) argues that imperialism was the inevitable product of industrial capitalism—surplus capital sought investment abroad when domestic markets were saturated. On this reading, cultural and nationalist justifications were rationalizations of fundamentally economic drives.

The **political/nationalist interpretation** argues that interstate competition and strategic rivalry, not economic necessity, drove imperial expansion. Many colonial territories were economically marginal; what mattered was denying them to rivals.

The **cultural/ideological interpretation** holds that the "civilizing mission" was not mere rationalization but a genuine belief system held by most European imperialists, shaping policy in ways not reducible to economic interest.

The AP exam does not require adjudicating this debate, but recognizing it helps with primary source analysis: a Jules Ferry speech can be read through economic *or* nationalist *or* cultural lenses, and your argument is stronger when you acknowledge the overlap.
