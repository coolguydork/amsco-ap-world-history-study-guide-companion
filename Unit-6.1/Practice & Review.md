# Practice & Review

**Topic 6.1 | AMSCO pp. 367–374**

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage.*

> "The English in India had always been somewhat more detached from the indigenous environment than the Dutch in Indonesia. After the 1780s, their isolation gradually intensified and became obvious with the decline in status of Eurasian Anglo-Indians. . . . The club became the center of British social life in India and the other Asian colonies during the Victorian era. In clubs, one could feel like a gentleman among other gentlemen while being served by a native staff. . . . The large clubs of Calcutta remained closed to Indians until 1946. This type of color bar was especially disturbing because it excluded from social recognition the very people who had carried their self-Anglicizing the furthest and loyally supported British rule. . . .
>
> In most regions of Africa . . . the Europeans saw themselves as foreign rulers separated from the African cultures by an abyss. . . . A process of great symptomatic significance was the rejection of the highly educated West Africans who had worked with the early mission. They had envisioned the colonial takeover as an opportunity for a joint European-African effort to modernize and civilize Africa. Instead, they were now, as 'white Negroes,' despised by all."
>
> — Jürgen Osterhammel, *Colonialism*, 1997

---

**1.** Which theory did Europeans use most directly to justify the social patterns described in the passage?

A. Social Darwinism
B. Pan-Africanism
C. Popular sovereignty
D. Laissez-faire capitalism

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Social Darwinism provided the pseudo-scientific ideological framework for racial hierarchy—the belief that Europeans were biologically superior justified excluding educated Africans and Indians from social recognition, even those who had most closely adopted European norms. Pan-Africanism (B) was a resistance ideology, not a justification for imperialism. Popular sovereignty (C) and laissez-faire capitalism (D) are unrelated to racial social stratification.

---

**2.** Which statement best provides the context for the racial policies described in the passage that shaped imperialism in India and Africa?

A. In both places, the English did not encourage highly educated native people to prepare for self-rule.
B. In both places, a smooth transition of power helped the highly educated native people gain political power.
C. In both places, social clubs were the meeting places for native people planning to fight for self-rule.
D. In both places, the colonizers finally began to respect educated natives, thus weakening their own colonial rule.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage emphasizes that even the most Anglicized, educated Indians and West Africans were excluded and "despised"—the colonial system had no path to self-rule for native peoples regardless of their education or loyalty. Options B and D contradict the passage directly. Option C misreads the clubs described, which were exclusionary European institutions, not places of native political organizing.

---

**3.** The context for the European attitudes noted in the passage was that

A. most Americans told the British that "all men are created equal"
B. some scientists claimed Europeans were a biologically superior race
C. most Indians and Africans preferred to create non-British clubs
D. some Europeans wanted native people to leave India and Africa

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Scientific racism—including Social Darwinism and phrenology—provided the pseudo-scientific "proof" of European biological superiority that underpinned the racial hierarchies described. This is the ideological context the passage describes as operating in both India and Africa. The other options are either unsupported by the passage or historically inaccurate as contextual claims.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Question 1 refers to the following passage.*

> "Gentlemen, we must speak more loudly and more honestly! We must say openly that indeed the higher races have a right over the lower races. . . . I repeat, that the superior races have a right because they have a duty. They have the duty to civilize the inferior races. . . . In our time, I maintain that European nations acquit themselves with generosity, with grandeur, and with sincerity of this superior civilizing duty. I say that French colonial policy, the policy of colonial expansion . . . that this policy of colonial expansion was inspired by . . . the fact that a navy such as ours cannot do without safe harbors, defenses, supply centers on the high seas . . . ."
>
> — Jules Ferry, speech on French colonial expansion, 1884

**1A.** Identify ONE motive Ferry offers for imperial expansion other than an economic motive.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Ferry offers a **cultural/ideological motive**: he argues that "superior races" have a duty to "civilize inferior races." This is the classic "civilizing mission" (*mission civilisatrice*) argument—that European imperialism is morally justified, even obligatory, because it brings civilization to peoples Ferry characterizes as inferior.

**1B.** Explain ONE way in which Ferry's argument is similar to other arguments of the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Ferry's argument mirrors Rudyard Kipling's "White Man's Burden" (1899), which similarly framed colonial rule as a duty borne by Western peoples toward "half-devil and half-child" colonized peoples. Both arguments use the language of obligation and benevolence to justify conquest, masking economic and strategic interests behind a humanitarian veneer. Social Darwinism provided the pseudo-scientific foundation for both claims.

**1C.** Explain ONE way in which the French Revolution influenced French imperialism in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The French Revolution's ideals of liberty, equality, and the "civilizing" power of reason created a self-contradiction in French imperialism. France justified colonial rule partly by arguing it was spreading Enlightenment values and rational governance to "backward" peoples—echoing the revolutionary belief that French civilization was universal and progressive. This "republican imperialism" allowed Ferry and others to frame conquest as consistent with, rather than contradictory to, French revolutionary ideals.

---

**2A.** Identify ONE economic motivation behind European imperialism in the 19th century.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> European industrialists needed **raw materials**—cotton, rubber, vegetable oils, jute—for their factories, and **markets** for manufactured goods. As the Second Industrial Revolution accelerated in the 1870s–1890s, competition among Britain, France, Germany, and Japan intensified, driving each power to secure overseas territories before rivals could.

**2B.** Explain ONE reason, besides religious conversion, missionaries believed they were helping colonized lands.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Missionaries like David Livingstone argued that introducing **Western commerce** would end the East African slave trade by giving Africans a legitimate economic alternative. This "3 Cs" framework—Christianity, Commerce, and Civilization—held that capitalist trade and European economic practices would bring material prosperity and social order, replacing what missionaries viewed as destructive indigenous economic practices.

**2C.** Explain ONE effect imperialism had on the culture of colonized people.

> [!tip] Model Response — Effect
> Imperial "civilizing mission" ideologies created **cultural displacement** in colonized societies. Colonial administrations often banned or suppressed indigenous languages, religious practices, and political institutions, replacing them with European languages (used in schools and courts), Christian missions, and Western legal codes. In India, for example, British policies promoted English education and common law, which partially displaced Persian and Sanskrit scholarly traditions—producing a Western-educated elite that was culturally caught between two worlds, as described in the Osterhammel passage above.
