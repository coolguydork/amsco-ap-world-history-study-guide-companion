# Topic 5.7 — Practice & Review

**Unit 5: Revolutions | Period 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900)**

## Multiple Choice Practice

**1.** The development of the corporation as a business structure in the 19th century most directly contributed to industrialization by

- A) giving governments greater control over the means of production
- B) allowing large amounts of capital to be pooled from many investors with limited personal risk
- C) eliminating competition between businesses through the creation of monopolies
- D) replacing the putting-out system with centralized factory production

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> A is wrong — corporations were private entities that actually *reduced* government control over economic activity; laissez-faire ideology pushed governments *out* of production decisions.
> **B is correct** — Limited liability was the corporation's transformative innovation: investors could back large ventures (railroads, steel mills, mines) without risking personal bankruptcy. This dramatically expanded the pool of available capital and made industrial-scale enterprise possible.
> C is wrong — corporations could *lead to* monopolies over time, but this was a consequence, not the mechanism by which they contributed to industrialization. Monopolies also did not eliminate competition immediately.
> D is wrong — the shift from putting-out to factory production preceded and occurred independently of the corporate form; early factories were often sole proprietorships or partnerships.

**2.** The operations of companies like De Beers, HSBC, and Unilever in the late 19th century best illustrate which of the following?

- A) The spread of socialist economic principles across industrial nations
- B) The decline of European imperial power in Asia and Africa
- C) The connection between industrial capitalism and colonial resource extraction
- D) The success of laissez-faire policies in raising living standards globally

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> A is wrong — De Beers, HSBC, and Unilever were private capitalist corporations, not examples of socialist economic organization. If anything, they represent the opposite.
> B is wrong — the late 19th century was the *height* of European imperial power in Asia and Africa, not its decline. These companies were made possible by imperial control.
> **C is correct** — These transnational companies extracted resources (diamonds, palm oil) from colonized regions, paid local workers minimal wages, and repatriated profits to Europe — a classic pattern of economic imperialism. Unilever sourced palm oil from British West Africa and the Belgian Congo; De Beers mined diamonds across southern Africa; HSBC financed imperial trade in Asia.
> D is wrong — colonial workers and subject populations gained little from laissez-faire capitalism; the system raised living standards primarily among the European and American middle classes, not globally.

**3.** Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) represented a major shift in economic thought primarily because it

- A) argued that colonial empires were necessary to sustain industrial economies
- B) provided an intellectual justification for government ownership of key industries
- C) replaced mercantilism with a framework based on free markets and individual self-interest
- D) called for an international system of tariffs to protect domestic industries

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> A is wrong — Smith actually criticized many aspects of the mercantilist-colonial system; he argued free trade was more efficient than colonial monopolies.
> B is wrong — Smith argued the *opposite*: that governments should stay out of economic life and allow free markets to operate. This is the core of laissez-faire philosophy.
> **C is correct** — *The Wealth of Nations* dismantled mercantilism's logic (that national wealth came from accumulating gold and controlling trade) and replaced it with the argument that free markets, guided by the "invisible hand" of self-interest and competition, allocate resources more efficiently than any government could. This laid the philosophical foundation for industrial capitalism.
> D is wrong — protective tariffs were a mercantilist policy that Smith specifically criticized. Laissez-faire opposed tariffs as market distortions.

## Short Answer Question (SAQ) Practice

**Prompt:** Using specific evidence, briefly explain ONE way that the development of corporations changed economic systems in the period 1750–1900.

**Model Response:** The corporation's introduction of **limited liability** fundamentally changed how capital was invested in the 19th century. Before limited liability, an investor in a failing business could lose not only their investment but their personal assets. Under the corporate model, losses were capped at the amount invested. This dramatically lowered the risk of investing, encouraging far more people to put money into industrial ventures. The result was an unprecedented flow of capital into railroads, steel mills, and transnational companies — enabling the industrial scale of production that defined the late 19th century. Companies like De Beers and HSBC, which operated across multiple continents, could only exist because the corporate structure allowed them to raise more capital than any individual owner could provide.

## Document-Based and Long Essay Connections

**For DBQs**: Topic 5.7 commonly appears in documents about:
- Business charters, company prospectuses, and descriptions of corporate structure
- Arguments for and against laissez-faire and government regulation of business
- Accounts of transnational company operations in Africa and Asia (economic imperialism)
- Advertisements, leisure catalogs, and descriptions of consumer culture in the late 19th century

**For LEQs**: Common prompts connected to 5.7:
- "Evaluate the extent to which economic systems changed in the period 1750–1900."
- "Evaluate the extent to which the development of capitalism in the period 1750–1900 benefited all social classes equally."
- "Compare the causes and effects of economic change in TWO regions in the period 1750–1900."

> [!exam-tip]
> When writing about 5.7, pair the corporation's *mechanism* (limited liability → more investment capital) with its *consequence* (transnational companies → economic imperialism). The best AP essays link the domestic business innovation to its global effects. For example: "The corporation's limited liability structure enabled companies like Unilever to raise enough capital to operate factories on three continents by 1890, sourcing palm oil from the Belgian Congo and selling soap products across Europe — a pattern that made corporate expansion and imperial expansion inseparable."

## Common Misconceptions

> [!warning]
> **Laissez-faire did not mean no government involvement.** Governments still *chartered* corporations, built infrastructure, passed tariff laws, and waged imperial wars on behalf of corporate interests. "Laissez-faire" meant governments stayed out of regulating *wages and working conditions* — not that they were absent from economic life altogether.

> [!warning]
> **Monopolies did not eliminate the market entirely.** Rockefeller's Standard Oil and Krupp's steel empire were monopolies within specific sectors, but competition continued across the broader economy. The AP exam tests whether you understand *monopoly* as control of a *specific* market, not the entire economy.

> [!warning]
> **Consumer culture was not universal.** Rising wages and affordable goods reached the middle class — not industrial workers living in poverty. The bicycle, music hall ticket, and leisure sport were markers of *middle-class* prosperity. Industrial workers often labored sixty-hour weeks in dangerous conditions during the very same decades.

## Quick Review: Key Economic Innovations, 1750–1900

| Innovation | Key Example | Significance |
|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Laissez-faire capitalism | Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations* (1776) | Replaced mercantilism; justified free markets and minimal government regulation |
| Corporation + limited liability | Railroad and steel companies | Spread investor risk; enabled massive capital accumulation for industrial projects |
| Monopoly | Krupp (steel), Rockefeller (oil) | Concentrated wealth and political power; eliminated competition in key sectors |
| Transnational company | De Beers, HSBC, Unilever | Linked industrial production to colonial resource extraction across continents |
| Insurance market | Lloyd's of London | Spread financial risk of global trade; enabled larger and riskier ventures |
| Consumer culture | Safety bicycle, music halls, sports | Created mass domestic markets; stabilized societies through rising living standards for some |
