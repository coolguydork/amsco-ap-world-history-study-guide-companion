## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: John D. Rockefeller, interview, 1880 (AMSCO p. 329)*

> "It [creating a monopoly in the oil industry] was forced upon us. We had to do it in self-defense. The oil business was in confusion and daily growing worse. Someone had to make a stand. . . . This movement was the origin of the whole system of economic administration. It has revolutionized the way of doing business all over the world. The time was ripe for it. It had to come, though all we saw at the moment was the need to save ourselves from wasteful conditions. . . . The day of combination is here to stay. Individualism is gone, never to return."

---

**Question 1.** Which individual would most likely have agreed with the views expressed by Rockefeller on business combinations?

A. James Hargreaves
B. James Watt
C. Guglielmo Marconi
D. Cecil Rhodes

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> Rhodes, like Rockefeller, believed large-scale corporate combination was the natural and superior form of business organization — and he practiced it with De Beers. Hargreaves (spinning jenny) and Watt (steam engine) were inventors, not monopolists. Marconi was a 20th-century inventor. Rhodes is the only figure who built a monopolistic transnational empire analogous to Standard Oil.

---

**Question 2.** Which statement summarizes an important difference between Rockefeller and Enlightenment thinkers?

A. The perspective that an action could be "forced upon us" by others
B. The desire to end traditional practices that led to "wasteful conditions"
C. The conclusion that an innovation could have impact "all over the world"
D. The belief that "individualism is gone."

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> Enlightenment thinkers (Locke, Rousseau, Smith) championed individual rights and individual rationality as the foundation of political and economic life. Rockefeller explicitly declares "Individualism is gone, never to return" — directly contradicting Enlightenment ideology. The other options (being forced to act, ending waste, global impact) are not distinctly un-Enlightenment ideas.

---

**Question 3.** Which example provides support for the claims made by Rockefeller?

A. The Krupp steel industry in Germany.
B. The decline of the Ottoman Empire
C. The rise of cottage industries
D. The support for the American Declaration of Independence

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Rockefeller claims monopolistic combination revolutionized business worldwide. Alfred Krupp's steel monopoly in Germany, built using the Bessemer process, is the clearest parallel — another industrialist using combination and superior technology to dominate an entire industry in another country. The Ottoman decline, cottage industries (pre-industrial), and the Declaration (political, individualist) do not support claims about corporate combination.

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Consumer Revolution (AMSCO p. 330)

*Source: John Benson, Consumption and the Consumer Revolution, 1996*

> "It is especially difficult to explain why consumers chose to use the increasing incomes which they had at their disposal for consumption rather than for saving or investment. Some historians suggest that consumption increased because consumers shared an almost instinctive desire to enjoy a higher standard of living… Others believe that consumers consumed in order to emulate those around them… Other historians maintain that consumers were manipulated by the machinations of advertisers and other commercial interests."

**Part A.** Identify ONE way in which ideas from the period 1750–1900 contributed to the development of the consumer.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Laissez-faire economic ideology, championed by Adam Smith, promoted free trade and minimal government interference, which allowed producers to sell goods across national boundaries without tariffs. This expanded the variety and availability of consumer goods, making consumption a viable option for more people. Additionally, the ideology that individual material improvement was desirable (versus older moral frameworks emphasizing thrift) legitimized spending as a social good.

**Part B.** Explain ONE way in which industrialization influenced the consumer revolution in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Factory production dramatically reduced the cost of manufactured goods such as textiles, tools, and household products, making them affordable for the working and middle classes for the first time. This lowered cost of goods, combined with rising (if modest) wages in industrial cities, gave urban workers a small degree of disposable income. Producers responded by advertising aggressively — especially to the middle class — and by creating new retail formats, which further stimulated demand and created a self-reinforcing cycle of mass production and mass consumption.

**Part C.** Explain ONE way in which capitalism influenced supply and demand in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Capitalist competition and the profit motive drove producers to continuously find cheaper ways to manufacture goods, which increased supply while lowering prices. At the same time, advertising created new demand for goods people had never previously considered necessary — from bicycles to sports equipment to branded soap. Corporations like Unilever actively shaped consumer desire through marketing, demonstrating that under industrial capitalism, "demand" was not simply a natural phenomenon but could be manufactured by producers to ensure that supply always found buyers.

### SAQ 2 — Business Growth (AMSCO p. 330)

**Part A.** Identify ONE development that promoted business growth in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> The development of the corporation with limited liability was the single most important structural innovation promoting business growth. By protecting stockholders from losses beyond their investment, the corporate form made it safe for many individuals to invest small amounts in large enterprises. This enabled industrialists to raise capital at a scale impossible for sole proprietors, funding the massive factories, railroads, and global supply chains that drove 19th-century economic growth.

**Part B.** Explain ONE way in which free-trade policies affected a cultural structure in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Free-trade policies reduced tariffs on imported goods, making foreign products available at lower prices in domestic markets. In Britain, this meant that factory-made textiles, household goods, and food staples became affordable to working-class consumers. The resulting expansion of consumption transformed urban working-class culture: instead of producing most goods at home or going without, workers became participants in a commercial market economy, defining their social status partly through purchases. Consumer goods, leisure activities, and fashion became markers of class identity — a cultural transformation directly enabled by free trade.

**Part C.** Explain ONE way in which Western European countries changed a previous economic system in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explanation
> Western European nations replaced mercantilist economic systems with laissez-faire capitalism. Mercantilism had relied on government-directed trade, protectionist tariffs, and state-chartered monopolies (like the East India Company) to accumulate national wealth. Under laissez-faire capitalism, governments reduced tariffs, allowed private competition, and let markets allocate resources. This shift enabled the rise of privately owned transnational corporations — HSBC, Unilever, De Beers — that operated globally without state direction, generating wealth through free-market competition rather than state-protected monopoly trade routes.
