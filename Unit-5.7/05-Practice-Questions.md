# Practice Questions — Unit 5.7: Economic Developments and Innovations

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

## Multiple-Choice Questions

**Use the passage below to answer Questions 1–2.**

> *"You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."*
> — William Jennings Bryan, "Cross of Gold" Speech, Democratic National Convention, 1896

---

**1.** The argument in Bryan's speech most directly reflected which of the following economic grievances?

- A) American manufacturers faced ruinous foreign competition because of insufficient tariff protection
- B) Agricultural producers were harmed by the gold standard's tight money supply, which kept commodity prices low and debts difficult to repay
- C) American workers were exploited by industrial corporations that paid wages below subsistence levels
- D) Foreign investors controlled too much of the American railroad network

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*Bryan's "cross of gold" metaphor attacks the gold standard, which limited the money supply and kept commodity prices low — devastating American farmers who needed higher prices for their crops and easier credit to repay debts.*

---

**2.** Bryan's argument is best understood as evidence of which of the following broader patterns in 19th-century economic history?

- A) The gold standard uniformly benefited all sectors of industrializing economies
- B) Global commodity market integration created winners (industrial producers) and losers (agricultural commodity producers) simultaneously
- C) Democratic politics in the United States consistently favored industrial interests over agricultural ones
- D) Free trade policies benefited agricultural exporters while harming manufacturers

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*The Long Depression of the 1870s–1890s demonstrated that commodity market integration was a double-edged sword: it lowered prices for consumers but devastated producers. Bryan speaks for those on the losing side of that integration.*

---

**3.** The development of the limited liability corporation in the 19th century most directly enabled which of the following?

- A) Government regulation of industrial monopolies that had previously operated outside legal frameworks
- B) The pooling of investment from thousands of shareholders, enabling capital-intensive industrial ventures
- C) Workers to own shares of the companies that employed them
- D) International trade free from government tariffs and restrictions

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*Limited liability made corporate stock-holding attractive to small investors by protecting them from losing more than their investment; this pooled the enormous capital required for railroads, steel mills, and other capital-intensive industrial enterprises.*

---

**4.** David Ricardo's theory of comparative advantage was most commonly used in the 19th century to justify which of the following?

- A) Protective tariffs in late-industrializing nations like Germany and the United States
- B) Free trade policies, arguing that specialization and exchange maximize global production
- C) Colonial economic policies that forced non-industrialized regions to export raw materials
- D) Social welfare legislation that would increase worker productivity

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*Comparative advantage argues each country should produce what it makes most efficiently and trade for the rest; this was the intellectual foundation for free trade advocacy — especially in Britain, which made manufactured goods most efficiently and wanted open markets for them.*

---

**5.** The Sherman Antitrust Act (USA, 1890) was most directly a response to which of the following economic developments?

- A) Foreign competition that threatened American manufacturers
- B) The concentration of industrial production in monopolistic trusts like Standard Oil
- C) Labor unions organizing to control production in key industries
- D) The gold standard's restriction on the money supply during the Long Depression

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*Standard Oil's control of ~90% of US oil refining exemplified the monopolistic concentration that critics argued violated competitive capitalism's own principles; the Sherman Act was the government's first major antitrust tool.*

---

**6.** The global commodity markets of the late 19th century most directly affected non-industrialized regions by:

- A) Providing stable, high prices for agricultural exports that funded industrial development
- B) Integrating them into the global economy as raw material suppliers while exposing them to volatile price swings
- C) Enabling their transition from agricultural to industrial production through access to cheap manufactured goods
- D) Reducing their dependence on European manufactured goods by creating local commodity exchanges

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*Global commodity market integration gave cotton farmers in Egypt, rubber tappers in Congo, and nitrate miners in Chile access to global markets — but also exposed them to price crashes they had no control over. Integration without industrial diversification created dependency, not development.*

---

**7.** J.P. Morgan's role in financing the creation of U.S. Steel (1901) most directly illustrates which of the following trends in late 19th-century capitalism?

- A) Government ownership of key industries as a strategy to prevent monopoly
- B) The growing concentration of financial power in investment banks that could organize industrial consolidations
- C) The success of small entrepreneurial firms in competing against established industrial corporations
- D) The decline of the steel industry as electricity replaced iron and steel in industrial applications

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*Morgan's ability to assemble $480 million to purchase Carnegie Steel and create the world's first billion-dollar corporation illustrates the concentration of financial power that characterized the Second Industrial Revolution — the investment banker had become as powerful as the industrialist.*

---

**8.** The "Long Depression" (1873–1896) is most significant for AP World History because it demonstrates:

- A) That industrial capitalism was inherently unstable and prone to repeated crises
- B) That the integration of global commodity markets meant economic downturns could simultaneously affect agricultural producers worldwide
- C) That free trade policies were causing permanent damage to industrialized economies
- D) That the gold standard was successfully stabilizing international currency values

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*The Long Depression's global reach — falling commodity prices simultaneously devastated farmers in the USA, Europe, India, Egypt, and Australia — illustrated that the integrated global economy meant shared vulnerability as well as shared opportunity.*

---

**9.** Britain's advocacy for free trade after 1846 is best understood as:

- A) A purely ideological commitment to Adam Smith's principles regardless of British economic interests
- B) A policy that aligned with Britain's economic self-interest as the world's most efficient manufacturer, seeking open markets for its exports
- C) A diplomatic strategy to prevent European nations from forming trade blocs against British goods
- D) A response to working-class demands for cheaper imported food

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*Britain preached free trade when British manufacturers had the lowest production costs globally and would therefore dominate open markets. As German and American manufacturers became competitive, Britain maintained free trade ideology even when it was no longer unambiguously self-serving — illustrating how economic ideology can outlast its material foundations.*

---

**10.** The global division of labor that emerged in the 19th century, in which industrialized nations manufactured goods and non-industrialized regions supplied raw materials, was:

- A) A natural result of each region's geographic endowments and inherent economic strengths
- B) Partly produced by colonial policies, tariff structures, and military power that limited non-industrial regions' ability to develop manufacturing
- C) Beneficial to all participants, as each region specialized in what it did most efficiently
- D) Reversed by the end of the 19th century as non-industrial regions began manufacturing their own goods

**✅ Correct Answer: B**
*The division of labor was not "natural" — it was produced by colonial policy (Britain imposed free trade on India), tariff structures (industrialized nations protected their manufacturing while keeping colonies open), and military power (which enforced these economic arrangements).*

## Short Answer Questions (SAQ)

### SAQ 1

**"Explain how the development of new financial institutions transformed industrial capitalism in the 19th century."**

**Part A:** Identify ONE specific financial institution or innovation that supported industrial capitalism.
**Part B:** Explain how that institution or innovation enabled a specific form of industrial development.
**Part C:** Explain ONE negative consequence of the financial institution or innovation you identified.

> [!tip] Top-Score Guidance
> - **Part A/B:** Limited liability corporation → enabled thousands of small investors to fund railroad construction; Investment banking → enabled Morgan to create US Steel; Gold standard → enabled international trade by stabilizing currency exchange.
> - **Part C:** Monopoly trusts concentrated economic power; gold standard deepened depressions by preventing monetary expansion; global commodity markets created dependency for non-industrial regions.

---

### SAQ 2

**"Compare the economic consequences of industrialization for an industrialized nation and a non-industrialized region in the period c. 1850–1900."**

**Part A:** Identify ONE specific economic benefit of industrialization for an industrialized nation.
**Part B:** Identify ONE specific economic cost of global commodity market integration for a non-industrialized region.
**Part C:** Explain ONE reason why the economic gap between industrialized and non-industrialized regions widened during this period.

> [!tip] Top-Score Guidance
> - **Part A:** Rising wages (eventually), cheap manufactured goods, high-value export manufacturing, capital accumulation.
> - **Part B:** India's deindustrialization (Lancashire cloth destroys Indian textiles); Egyptian cotton dependency (one crop → vulnerability to price crashes); Congo rubber extraction (brutal forced labor for Belgian industrial demand).
> - **Part C:** Industrial nations had the capital, technology, and military power to maintain the asymmetric relationship. Non-industrial regions couldn't build protective tariffs because colonialism prevented them from setting their own trade policy.
