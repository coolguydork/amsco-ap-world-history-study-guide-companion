# People, States & Movements

**Unit 5.7 — Economic Developments and Innovations | AMSCO pp. 325–331**

## Key Figures

### John D. Rockefeller (1839–1937)
American industrialist who founded Standard Oil and created one of history's most powerful monopolies in the U.S. petroleum industry. Rockefeller's strategy of horizontal integration — buying out competitors until Standard Oil controlled roughly 90% of American oil refining — became the model for monopoly capitalism. In his 1880 interview (the AP source document for this topic), he defended monopoly as inevitable and efficient: "The day of combination is here to stay. Individualism is gone, never to return."

> [!exam-tip]
> Rockefeller is the primary figure in the MCQ practice for this topic. Know his argument: monopoly was *forced* by market chaos and was ultimately a rationalization of business. Critics countered that it destroyed competition and concentrated political power dangerously.

### Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919)
Scottish-born American industrialist who built a steel empire using the [[Unit-5.7/Key Terms & Concepts|Bessemer process]]. Carnegie used vertical integration — controlling everything from raw materials to distribution — to dominate American steel. Later became a major philanthropist (Carnegie Hall, Carnegie libraries), embodying the "Gospel of Wealth" ideology that rich industrialists had a duty to give back. Symbol of monopoly capitalism and of the era's class contradictions.

### Cecil Rhodes (1853–1902)
British-born imperialist, founder of De Beers Diamonds, and prime minister of the Cape Colony in southern Africa. Rhodes epitomized the fusion of transnational corporate power with imperial ambition. He sought to build a Cape Town–to–Cairo railroad through British-controlled African territory, aiming to connect all British colonies in Africa into a single extractive network. The project failed because Britain never gained continuous territorial control. Colonial railroad workers in Africa were natives paid far below European wages — the economics of imperialism in miniature.

> [!tip]
> Rhodes bridges Topic 5.7 (transnational corporations) and Topic 6.3–6.5 (imperialism in Africa). On the AP, he may appear in both economic and imperialist contexts. De Beers was a corporation; the Cape-to-Cairo railroad was imperialism; both were Rhodes.

### Karl Marx (1818–1883)
German philosopher and economist whose *Das Kapital* (1867) offered the most systematic critique of the industrial capitalist system that Topics 5.3–5.7 describe. Marx argued that capitalism necessarily concentrated wealth in the hands of a bourgeoisie (capitalist class) at the expense of the proletariat (workers), and that monopoly capitalism was its most advanced — and most destructive — form. Marx's ideas directly fueled the workers' movements covered in **Topic 5.8**.

### Adam Smith (1723–1790)
Scottish economist whose *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) provided the ideological foundation for industrial capitalism. Smith argued that free markets, guided by the "invisible hand" of supply and demand, allocate resources more efficiently than government intervention. His critique of mercantilism and advocacy for laissez-faire policy shaped the economic thinking of the Industrial Revolution era. Quoted in the AMSCO opening: "Man is an animal that makes bargains."

## Key Institutions

### Lloyd's of London
Marine insurance market that grew from a London coffee house into a global institution. By spreading risk across many underwriters, Lloyd's enabled the long-distance trade that industrial capitalism required. Represents the broader maturation of financial services alongside manufacturing during industrialization.

### De Beers Diamonds
Cecil Rhodes's diamond mining company in southern Africa; one of the era's iconic transnational corporations. Used colonial land and labor to generate enormous wealth for British investors while paying African workers a fraction of European wages. A clear example of how corporate capitalism and imperialism reinforced each other.

## Movements: Capitalism and Its Critics

| Movement / Idea | Core Claim | Key Figure | AP Connection |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Laissez-faire capitalism** | Free markets allocate resources best; government should not intervene | Adam Smith | Replaced mercantilism; justified industrial monopolies |
| **Monopoly capitalism** | Large corporations dominate entire industries; competition is inefficient | Rockefeller, Krupp | Caused concentration of wealth; provoked socialist backlash |
| **Marxist critique** | Capitalism exploits workers; will collapse under its own contradictions | Karl Marx | Directly leads to Topic 5.8 (labor movements, socialism) |
| **Imperial capitalism** | Corporate expansion requires and justifies colonial conquest | Cecil Rhodes | Directly leads to Topics 6.3–6.5 (imperialism) |

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse **laissez-faire** (no government interference, free markets) with **mercantilism** (government-directed trade and protectionism). The shift from mercantilism to laissez-faire is itself an AP-testable change for this period.
