# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 5.7 — Economic Developments and Innovations | AMSCO pp. 325–331**

---

## Economy: Business Structures

**corporations**
Business entity chartered by a government and owned by stockholders; features limited liability, transferable shares, and indefinite duration. Corporations replaced high-risk sole proprietorships as the dominant form of large-scale business during industrialization, enabling the pooling of capital from many investors.

**stockholders**
Individuals who own shares of a corporation; receive dividends when the company profits; lose only the value of their investment if the company fails — not personally liable for debts. The stockholder model was central to making industrial-scale investment attractive.

**stock market**
Exchange where shares of corporations are bought and sold. Allowed industrialists to raise massive capital from dispersed investors and gave stockholders a liquid way to exit investments, making corporate ownership much more flexible than partnership.

**monopoly**
Control of an entire industry or market by a single company, eliminating competition. Classic AP examples: Rockefeller's Standard Oil (U.S.), Krupp steel (Germany). Monopolies concentrate economic and political power — a key target of socialist and labor critics in Topic 5.8.

**transnational**
Describes companies or organizations operating across national boundaries. 19th-century transnational firms like HSBC and Unilever sourced raw materials from colonies and sold goods globally, gaining wealth and influence on a scale previously impossible. Directly connected to economic imperialism (Topic 6.5).

## Economy: Key Companies

**Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation**
HSBC; British-owned bank founded in Hong Kong (1865) that focused on finance, corporate investment, and global banking. A leading example of a transnational financial institution channeling colonial capital into global markets. (AMSCO p. 326)

**Unilever Corporation**
British-Dutch transnational company producing household goods, most famously soap. Sourced palm oil from British West Africa and the Belgian Congo via large colonial plantations. Symbol of how industrial capitalism used imperial holdings as both raw material sources and labor reserves. (AMSCO p. 326)

> [!exam-tip]
> Both HSBC and Unilever are examples of how **economic imperialism** worked in practice. Even before formal conquest, corporations were extracting colonial resources. This is the bridge between Topic 5.7 and Topic 6.5.

## Culture: Population & Society

**consumerism**
Cultural and economic shift in which purchasing goods becomes central to identity and daily life. Arose as factory production lowered prices and created affordable goods for a new middle class with disposable income. Producers responded with advertising, new retail formats, and manufactured leisure goods.

**urbanization**
Rapid growth of cities driven by rural workers migrating to industrial centers for factory jobs. Transformed social structures, public health, living conditions, and class composition. A key cause of mass consumer culture — concentrated consumers in one place.

## Technology: Industry

**Bessemer process**
Industrial method (c. 1856) for mass-producing steel from pig iron by blasting air through molten metal. Developed by Henry Bessemer; dramatically reduced steel costs. Alfred Krupp used it to build a monopoly in German steel. Also essential for railroad construction and urban building. Links to [[Unit-5.5/Technology in the Industrial Age Overview\|Topic 5.5]] (industrial technology).

> [!tip]
> **Exam connection — Bessemer to Krupp to monopoly:** The AP may trace a chain of causation: new technology → cheaper production → one firm dominates the market → monopoly → political/economic power concentration. Know all three steps.
