# Topic 5.7 — Key Terms & Concepts

Master these terms to dominate the economic questions on the AP exam. (AMSCO pp. 325–328)

---

## Economic Ideologies

### [[capitalism]]
An economic system in which **private individuals** own the means of production (factories, land, capital) and make decisions based on market forces (supply and demand) rather than government direction. Capitalism replaced mercantilism as the dominant economic framework of the industrial era.

### [[laissez-faire]]
French for "let do" or "let it be." The economic doctrine that governments should **not interfere** in market activity — no price controls, no production quotas, minimal regulation. Associated with [[Adam Smith]]'s *Wealth of Nations* (1776). The idea: free markets naturally produce efficient outcomes because self-interest drives productivity and competition.

> [!tip]
> **Laissez-faire** is the economic philosophy that justifies minimal government regulation of business. Its opposite in this period would be **mercantilism** (heavy government control of trade) or **socialism** (collective ownership). On the AP exam, be able to explain *why* laissez-faire became dominant and *what its critics* (like [[Unit-5.8/Karl Marx & Socialism|Karl Marx]]) said about it.

### [[Adam Smith]] (1723–1790)
Scottish economist and philosopher; author of *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). Smith argued that humans are naturally transactional (self-interested) and that free markets, guided by the "invisible hand" of supply and demand, allocate resources more efficiently than governments can. Smith championed [[capitalism]] and [[laissez-faire]] and attacked mercantilism. His work is foundational to modern economics.

> [!exam-tip]
> *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) is a key text for the AP exam. Know that Smith: (1) published in 1776, (2) argued for free markets and against government interference, (3) provided intellectual justification for industrial capitalism, and (4) critiqued mercantilism.

### Mercantilism
The pre-industrial economic philosophy that a nation's wealth depended on accumulating gold and silver, maintaining a favorable trade balance, and using colonies to supply raw materials and buy finished goods. [[Adam Smith]]'s [[laissez-faire]] capitalism replaced mercantilism as the dominant economic framework.

### [[socialism]]
The economic and political philosophy that advocates **collective or government ownership** of the means of production, distribution, and exchange. Arose as a critique of industrial [[capitalism]]'s inequalities. Associated with [[Unit-5.8/Karl Marx & Socialism|Karl Marx]] and Friedrich Engels (*The Communist Manifesto*, 1848; *Das Kapital*, 1867). Socialism held that capitalism exploited workers (the proletariat) for the benefit of owners (the bourgeoisie).

## Business Structures

### [[corporation]]
A business entity **chartered by the government** as a legal "person" — it can own property, make contracts, and be sued, independent of its individual owners. Key feature: **limited liability** — stockholders can lose only what they invested, not their personal assets. Corporations issue **stock** (shares of ownership) to raise capital; stockholders receive **dividends** from profits.

**Four key features of a corporation:**
1. **Limited Liability** — investors risk only their investment
2. **Transferability of Shares** — stock can be bought and sold freely
3. **Legal Personality** — the corporation exists as a legal entity independent of its owners
4. **Perpetual Existence** — the corporation continues even if individual owners change

> [!exam-tip]
> The corporation was transformative because it **spread risk** across many investors, made it possible to raise far more capital than any individual could provide, and enabled large-scale enterprises (railroads, factories, mines) that sole proprietorships and partnerships could not finance.

### [[monopoly]]
When a **single corporation controls** a specific industry or market, eliminating competition. Industrial-era monopolies grew from corporations that used their advantages (technology, capital, scale) to drive out competitors.

**Key examples:**
- **Alfred Krupp** (Germany): Used the Bessemer steel process to build a near-monopoly on German steel production; supplied the German military
- **John D. Rockefeller** (United States): Built Standard Oil into a near-monopoly controlling ~90% of U.S. oil refining

### Sole Proprietorship
A business owned and operated by a single individual. Simple to set up but limited in capital, scale, and risk tolerance. Disadvantage: the owner has **unlimited personal liability** — if the business fails, creditors can seize personal assets. Could not compete with corporations for large-scale industrial activity.

### Partnership
A business owned by two or more individuals who share profits, losses, and management. More capital than a sole proprietorship but still limited; partners have unlimited liability. Like sole proprietorships, partnerships were insufficient for the massive capital needs of industrial enterprises.

## Transnational Business

### [[transnational companies]]
Corporations that **operate across national boundaries** — producing, sourcing, selling, and investing in multiple countries. A defining feature of the late industrial era and early globalization.

**Key examples:**
- **De Beers Diamonds** (Cecil Rhodes): Diamond mining empire spanning southern Africa
- **Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (HSBC)** (est. 1865): British-owned bank in Hong Kong colony; financed global trade and investment
- **Unilever** (British-Dutch): Household goods manufacturer with factories in Australia, Switzerland, and the United States by 1890; sourced palm oil from British West Africa and the Belgian Congo

### Lloyd's of London
Famous insurance market that began in a **London coffee house** where merchants gathered to insure shipping voyages. Lloyd's grew into the world's premier insurance market, creating the **modern insurance industry**. Insurance spread the financial risk of large ventures across many investors — a key enabler of global trade.

### HSBC (Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation)
Founded **1865** in Hong Kong (then a British colony). A **transnational bank** that provided financing for trade and corporate investment across Asia and the world. One of the earliest and most important global banks; still one of the world's largest today.

### Unilever
A **British-Dutch** transnational company producing household goods (especially soap). By **1890**, Unilever had factories in Australia, Switzerland, and the United States. It sourced **palm oil** — a key ingredient in soap — first from British West Africa and then from the **Belgian Congo**. Unilever's supply chain connected European consumer markets to African raw materials through imperial networks.

## Consumer Culture

### Consumerism
A **culture in which purchasing and consuming goods** becomes a central feature of social life and identity. Emerged among the working and middle classes during the late 19th century as real wages rose and more goods became affordable. Sustained by heavy advertising, especially targeting the **middle class** with disposable income.

### Leisure Culture
New forms of recreation that developed alongside industrialization:
- **Cycling**: The **penny-farthing bicycle** (1880s) evolved into the **safety bicycle** (chain-connecting gears), making cycling accessible to the masses
- **Organized sports**: Soccer (football) became dominant in Europe; baseball in the United States; class-based sports emerged (tennis and golf = upper class; some forms of rugby = working class)
- **Music halls** and **public parks** constructed in the second half of the 19th century — spaces where all social classes could mingle
- Companies encouraged **athletics** for practical reasons: self-discipline, rule-following behavior, and of course the sale of athletic equipment

> [!tip]
> Connect the rise of consumer culture to the broader story of industrialization: as workers earned wages, they could buy goods, which created demand that sustained further production. The consumer market became as important as industrial production itself.
