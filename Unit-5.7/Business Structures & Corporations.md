# Topic 5.7 — Business Structures & Corporations

**AMSCO:** pp. 325–328

---

## From Small Business to Corporation

The Industrial Revolution transformed not just how things were made — it transformed **how businesses were organized**. The scale of factories, railroads, and global trade required new forms of ownership that could pool resources from many investors.

### Three Forms of Business Ownership

| Form | Description | Limitation |
|------|------------|-----------|
| **Sole proprietorship** | Owned by one person; owner makes all decisions | Owner bears all risk; limited capital |
| **Partnership** | Small group shares ownership and decisions | Still limited capital; partners share risk |
| **Corporation** | Legal entity chartered by government; owned by stockholders | Limited liability; transferable shares; can grow to any scale |

### Four Key Features of a Corporation

1. **Limited liability** — stockholders can lose at most what they invested; not personally liable for company debts
2. **Transferability of shares** — ownership (stock) can be bought and sold freely; attracts more investors
3. **Legal personality** — the corporation can make contracts, be sued, and hold property as if it were a person
4. **Continuity** — the corporation survives changes in ownership; it does not die when founders die

Corporations **spread risk** across many investors, making large-scale, long-term projects (railroads, steel mills, global shipping) possible that no individual could finance alone.

## Monopolies

Some corporations grew so powerful they eliminated all competition:

- **Monopoly**: control of a specific industry, eliminating competition
- **Alfred Krupp** (Essen, Germany): used the Bessemer Process to dominate the German steel industry
- **John D. Rockefeller** (United States): created a monopoly in the oil industry

Monopolies generated enormous wealth and political power — a corporation's decision about where to build a factory could create or destroy thousands of jobs in a community.

## Transnational Companies

The most powerful corporations of the late 19th century **operated across national boundaries**. Their scale of wealth and influence was unprecedented.

| Company | Origin | Sector | Reach |
|---------|--------|--------|-------|
| **De Beers Diamonds** | Cecil Rhodes (British) | Diamonds | Proposed Cape Town–Cairo railroad to connect British colonies; never completed |
| **Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (HSBC)** | British | Finance, banking | Opened in Hong Kong 1865; global corporate banking |
| **Unilever** | British-Dutch | Household goods (soap) | Factories in Australia, Switzerland, U.S. by 1890; sourced palm oil from British West Africa then Belgian Congo |

> [!exam-tip]
> Transnational companies are a key example of how **economic imperialism** worked. De Beers, HSBC, and Unilever extracted resources and labor from colonized regions (Belgian Congo, British West Africa) and shipped profits back to Europe. This is New Imperialism in economic form — see [[Unit-6.1/Overview|Unit 6.1 Overview]].

### Cecil Rhodes and the Cape-to-Cairo Railroad
Rhodes planned a railroad connecting all British colonies from Cape Town (South Africa) to Cairo (Egypt) — a transportation network that would have made British imperial governance easier and military coordination faster. It was **never completed** because Britain never controlled all the necessary territory. But the project illustrates how industrial-era corporations had explicitly imperial ambitions.

> [!warning]
> Railway workers in Africa were overwhelmingly native Africans paid **far lower wages** than their European counterparts. Technology was a tool of extraction, not development.

## Banking and Insurance

As corporations grew, so did the **financial infrastructure** supporting them:

- **Lloyd's of London**: began in a coffee house where merchants and sailors exchanged shipping news; grew into the insurance industry — especially **marine insurance** (covering ships and cargo)
- **Banks** multiplied as entrepreneurs needed places to deposit capital and borrow money for new ventures

## Mass Culture and Consumerism

Industrialization didn't just change production — it created a **consumer culture**.

- Production outpaced traditional demand → manufacturers began **advertising heavily**, especially to the middle class with disposable income
- **Leisure activities** became popular: cycling (penny-farthing → safety bicycle with chain drive, 1880s), boating, sports
- Companies encouraged worker athletics (sports built self-discipline and rule-following)
- **Class-based sports**: tennis and golf (upper class, England); certain rugby styles (lower class)
- **Music halls and public parks** built for all social classes — with the explicit aim of modeling "civilized behavior" to the lower classes
- European football (soccer) dominated the continent; baseball dominated the United States

> [!tip]
> The rise of consumer culture is evidence that industrialization raised living standards for some — the middle class could afford bicycles and theater tickets. But this same period produced the horrendous conditions described in [[Unit-5.8/Labor Unions & Reform|Labor Unions & Reform]]. Both things are true simultaneously.
