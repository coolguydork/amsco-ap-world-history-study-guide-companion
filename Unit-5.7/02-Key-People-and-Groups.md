# Key People and Groups — Unit 5.7: Economic Developments and Innovations

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## John D. Rockefeller (1839–1937)
**Region:** United States | **Period:** 1860s–1910s

- Founded Standard Oil (1870) and used horizontal integration — buying out competitors — to control ~90% of American oil refining by 1880.
- Organized Standard Oil as a **trust** (shareholders transferred control of multiple companies to trustees), creating the model of industrial monopoly.
- His company was broken up by the Supreme Court in 1911 (Standard Oil Co. v. United States) under the Sherman Antitrust Act — a landmark in government regulation of industrial capitalism.

---

## Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919)
**Region:** Scotland/United States | **Period:** 1870s–1900s

- Built Carnegie Steel using **vertical integration** — controlling every stage of production from iron ore mines to steel mills to railroads that transported the product.
- Sold Carnegie Steel to J.P. Morgan in 1901 for ~$480 million (equivalent to billions today), creating U.S. Steel — the world's first billion-dollar corporation.
- His "Gospel of Wealth" (1889) argued that the wealthy had a duty to distribute surplus for public benefit — while simultaneously crushing unions and paying minimal wages.

---

## J.P. Morgan (1837–1913)
**Region:** United States | **Period:** 1870s–1910s

- America's most powerful investment banker; organized and financed industrial consolidations including U.S. Steel, General Electric, and AT&T.
- Twice rescued the US government from financial crisis (1895, 1907) by organizing private banking coalitions — illustrating the dominance of private financial power over public institutions.
- His career illustrates the Second Industrial Revolution's characteristic concentration of financial as well as industrial power.

---

## David Ricardo (1772–1823)
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** Early 19th century

- Economist who developed the **theory of comparative advantage** (1817): each nation should produce what it makes most efficiently and trade for everything else; total global production is maximized by free trade.
- His theory provided the intellectual foundation for free trade advocacy and was used to justify Britain's repeal of the Corn Laws.
- The theory has limits: comparative advantage assumes countries can actually shift between industries, which they often cannot in the short term — a limitation that protectionists used to justify tariffs.

---

## The Rothschild Family
**Region:** Europe (Britain, France, Germany, Austria) | **Period:** 18th–19th century

- The most powerful investment banking dynasty in 19th-century Europe; financed governments (including Britain's war against Napoleon) and industrial projects across the continent.
- Their ability to raise capital across national borders made them essential intermediaries for railroad construction and government bond issuance.
- Illustrate how international finance bridged national economies and facilitated the globalization of industrial capitalism.

---

## William Jennings Bryan (1860–1925)
**Region:** United States | **Period:** 1890s

- American populist politician who in his famous "Cross of Gold" speech (1896) argued that the gold standard was destroying American farmers by keeping money scarce and commodity prices low.
- Represents the political backlash against industrial capitalism's financial institutions by agricultural producers whose commodity prices fell throughout the 1870s–1890s.
- His career illustrates how global commodity markets could devastate rural producers who were not direct beneficiaries of industrialization.

---

## Karl Marx (1818–1883) and Friedrich Engels (1820–1895)
**Region:** Germany/Britain | **Period:** 1840s–1880s

- Co-authors of *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) and Marx's major work *Capital* (Vol. I, 1867); argued that industrial capitalism systematically exploited workers and would eventually collapse under its own contradictions.
- Their economic analysis of capitalism — surplus value, alienation, concentration of capital, falling rate of profit — was both a description of 19th-century industrial capitalism and a prediction of its future.
- Covered more fully in [[Unit-5.8]], but relevant here as economic critics of the developments described in 5.7.
