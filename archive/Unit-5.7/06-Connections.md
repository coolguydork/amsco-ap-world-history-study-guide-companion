# Cross-Topic Connections — Unit 5.7: Economic Developments and Innovations

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## 1. Builds On (Earlier Units)

- **[[Commercial Capitalism and Joint-Stock Companies]]** ([[Unit-4.5]]) — The limited liability corporation is the direct descendant of the joint-stock company; 19th-century investment banking built on 17th-century commercial banking institutions.
- **[[Atlantic Silver Trade]]** ([[Unit-4.4]]) — The global commodity markets of the 19th century are a more complex version of the silver trade network that connected global economies in Unit 4; both created global economic interdependence.
- **[[Enlightenment Economics]]** ([[Unit-5.1]]) — Adam Smith's free market theory and Ricardo's comparative advantage are the intellectual foundations of 19th-century free trade policy.
- **[[Industrialization Begins and Spreads]]** ([[Unit-5.3]], [[Unit-5.4]]) — The economic institutions of 5.7 were created to finance and organize the industrial production described in 5.3 and 5.4.

---

## 2. Sets Up (Later Topics and Units)

- **[[Reactions to the Industrial Economy]]** ([[Unit-5.8]]) — Marxist economic analysis, labor unions, and socialist movements are direct responses to the industrial capitalism described in 5.7.
- **[[New Imperialism]]** (Unit 6) — The global commodity markets, investment banks, and trade policy of 5.7 drove the demand for raw materials that motivated formal imperialism; British cotton mills needed Egyptian cotton; German chemical industries needed African minerals.
- **[[World War I and its Economic Causes]]** (Unit 7) — Competition among industrial capitalism's major powers for markets, raw materials, and investment opportunities contributed to the imperial rivalries that led to WWI.
- **[[Great Depression]]** (Unit 8) — The financial integration of the global economy, the gold standard's constraints, and the concentration of financial power in investment banks all contributed to the 1929 crash and its global spread.

---

## 3. Comparative Cases

- **Industrial capitalism vs. pre-industrial commercial capitalism** — Commercial capitalism (Unit 4) extracted profit from trade price differences; industrial capitalism extracted profit from production — a fundamentally different and more productive (and exploitative) mechanism.
- **Free trade vs. protectionism as development strategies** — Britain succeeded with free trade (after industrializing behind mercantilism); Germany, USA, and Japan succeeded with protectionism. The debate about which approach better promotes development continues in the 21st century.
- **Standard Oil (USA) vs. German cartels** — Both represent industrial concentration, but by different organizational forms: Rockefeller's trust (secret agreements to control prices) vs. Germany's openly organized industrial cartels (legally recognized, government-encouraged). Illustrates how different legal systems produce different industrial structures.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam rarely focuses on this topic in isolation — it's more often tested as **context** for questions about imperialism (Unit 6), labor movements (5.8), or global inequality. Practice explaining the economic structures of 5.7 as the *backdrop* that makes other historical developments comprehensible.
