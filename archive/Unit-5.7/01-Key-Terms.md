# Key Terms — Unit 5.7: Economic Developments and Innovations

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Industrial capitalism** — An economic system in which private owners invest capital in factories and machines to produce goods for profit, using wage labor; distinguished from commercial capitalism (profit from trade) by its basis in production. (The dominant economic system of the industrialized world by 1850; its contradictions — between capital and labor, between industrialized and non-industrialized nations — drove the major political conflicts of the late 19th century.)

2. **Limited liability corporation** — A business structure in which shareholders' liability for corporate debts is limited to their investment; makes large-scale industrial investment possible by protecting individual investors from total ruin. (The dominant organizational form of industrial capitalism; enabled the pooling of massive capital for railroad, steel, and mining ventures; ancestor of the modern publicly traded company.)

3. **Investment bank** — A financial institution that raises capital for corporations and governments by underwriting stock and bond issues; distinct from commercial banks that hold deposits and make loans. (Investment banks like Rothschilds (Europe), J.P. Morgan (USA), and Deutsche Bank (Germany) were essential intermediaries between industrial corporations needing capital and investors seeking returns.)

4. **Gold standard** — A monetary system in which a currency's value is fixed to a specific quantity of gold; adopted by Britain (1821) and most industrialized nations by the 1870s–1880s. (Provided currency stability that facilitated international trade and investment; but also meant governments could not expand money supply during recessions, deepening downturns — the basis of populist anti-gold-standard movements like the American Populists.)

5. **Free trade** — An economic policy eliminating tariffs and trade restrictions; championed by Britain (especially after 1846) as the globally optimal economic system. (Britain's advocacy was partly principled — Adam Smith's influence — and partly self-interested: as the world's most efficient manufacturer, Britain benefited most from open global markets. Other nations industrialized behind protective tariffs.)

6. **Commodity market** — A marketplace where raw materials (cotton, wheat, copper, rubber, oil) are bought and sold, often through standardized contracts traded on exchanges; global commodity markets integrated industrial supply chains. (The Chicago Board of Trade, London Metal Exchange, and Liverpool Cotton Exchange linked raw material producers in Asia, Africa, and Latin America to industrial processors in Europe and North America.)

7. **Price revolution** — A period of significant inflation; the "Global Price Revolution" of the 16th century (caused by American silver) is the classic AP example, but 19th-century commodity markets also created price volatility that affected agricultural producers globally. (Falling commodity prices in the 1870s–1890s [the "Long Depression"] ruined farmers across Europe and the Americas, driving political movements like American Populism.)

8. **Monopoly / Oligopoly** — A market dominated by one (monopoly) or a few (oligopoly) very large firms; characteristic of Second Industrial Revolution industries (steel, oil, railroads, chemicals). (John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil controlled ~90% of American oil refining by 1880; Carnegie Steel dominated American steel; these concentrations drove antitrust legislation.)

9. **Robber barons** — A term for American industrial capitalists of the Gilded Age (Rockefeller, Carnegie, Vanderbilt, Morgan) who accumulated enormous wealth through monopoly power, political corruption, and labor exploitation. (Their careers illustrate the extreme inequality of laissez-faire industrial capitalism; also illustrate the role of corporate consolidation in the Second Industrial Revolution.)

10. **Trust / Cartel** — A combination of corporations that coordinate to control prices or eliminate competition; Standard Oil Trust (USA) and IG Farben (Germany, chemicals) are examples. (Antitrust legislation — Sherman Antitrust Act (USA, 1890) — developed specifically to break these combinations, illustrating the tension between industrial concentration and competitive capitalism.)

11. **Global division of labor** — The 19th-century pattern in which industrialized nations produced manufactured goods while non-industrialized regions produced raw materials; each region "specialized" according to its position in the industrial system. (This division was not natural but was produced by colonial policy, tariff structures, and military power; it deepened global inequality and made non-industrialized regions economically dependent on industrial centers.)

12. **Standard of living debate** — The historical controversy over whether industrialization improved or worsened the living standards of the working class; evidence suggests early industrialization worsened conditions before eventually improving them (the "Engels pause"). (Important for AP essays that ask you to evaluate industrialization's social impact: be aware that the answer depends on *which* workers, *when*, and by *what measure*.)

13. **Credit and banking systems** — The financial infrastructure of 19th-century capitalism: central banks (Bank of England, 1694; Banque de France, 1800; US Federal Reserve, 1913), commercial banks making loans, investment banks raising capital, and insurance companies spreading risk. (Without sophisticated credit systems, the enormous capital investments required for railroads, steel mills, and mines could not have been assembled.)

> [!exam-tip]
> For AP essays on the global economy, know the **core pattern**: industrial nations export manufactured goods, import raw materials; non-industrial regions export commodities, import manufactures. This asymmetric trade relationship enriched industrial nations and impoverished commodity producers — the structural foundation of modern global inequality.
