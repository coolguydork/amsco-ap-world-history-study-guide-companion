# Timeline — Unit 5.1: The Enlightenment

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

> **Big Picture:** The Enlightenment (c. 1680–1800) is the intellectual hinge between the Scientific Revolution of the 17th century and the political and industrial revolutions of the late 18th–19th centuries. It transforms European thought from a primarily religious framework to a secular, rational one — with world-historical consequences for governance, economics, and social organization.

---

| Date | Event | Region | Significance |
|------|-------|--------|--------------|
| 1687 | Newton publishes *Principia Mathematica* | England | Establishes rational laws of the physical universe; becomes the model Enlightenment thinkers seek to apply to society |
| 1689 | Locke publishes *Two Treatises of Government* | England | Articulates natural rights (life, liberty, property) and right of revolution; foundational text for Atlantic Revolutions |
| 1690 | Locke publishes *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* | England | Argues human minds are blank slates (*tabula rasa*) shaped by experience — challenging innate hierarchy and privilege |
| 1721 | Montesquieu publishes *Persian Letters* | France | Satirizes French society through fictional Persian travelers; begins Enlightenment critique of existing institutions |
| 1748 | Montesquieu publishes *The Spirit of the Laws* | France | Develops separation of powers; comparative political analysis; directly influences U.S. Constitution (1787) |
| 1751 | *Encyclopédie* begins publication (Diderot, d'Alembert) | France | **Change:** Codifies Enlightenment knowledge; spreads rational, secular thinking; twice censored by French crown |
| 1762 | Rousseau publishes *The Social Contract* | France/Geneva | "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains" — general will concept; influences French Revolution's radical phase |
| 1762 | Rousseau publishes *Émile* | France/Geneva | Argues for natural education; paradoxically prescribes a domestic, subordinate role for women |
| 1776 | Adam Smith publishes *The Wealth of Nations* | Scotland | Articulates free-market economics and division of labor; intellectual foundation for industrial capitalism and free trade |
| 1776 | American Declaration of Independence | North America | **Change:** First major political document to translate Enlightenment ideas into a founding document; Lockean natural rights language prominent |
| 1781 | Kant publishes *Critique of Pure Reason* | Prussia | Defines the Enlightenment as "Sapere aude" (Dare to know); synthesizes rationalism and empiricism; major philosophical landmark |
| 1789 | French Revolution begins; Declaration of Rights of Man | France | **Change:** Enlightenment ideals of liberty, equality, popular sovereignty enacted in revolutionary law — but women excluded |
| 1792 | Wollstonecraft publishes *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* | Britain | **Change:** Extends Enlightenment natural rights arguments to women; founding text of Western feminism |
| 1792 | Olympe de Gouges executed after writing *Declaration of Rights of Woman* | France | **Continuity:** Despite Revolution, women's political equality remained denied; de Gouges guillotined for political radicalism |
| 1800 | Enlightenment wanes; Romanticism emerges as counter-movement | Europe | **Continuity:** Enlightenment ideas persist in political systems; but cultural counter-reaction emphasizes emotion, nature, the sublime |

---

> [!tip] Continuity vs. Change
> **Continuity:** Hereditary monarchy and aristocracy *persisted* in most of Europe through this period; Enlightenment ideas challenged but did not immediately overturn them.
> **Change:** The *legitimacy* of absolute monarchy was permanently damaged — after Locke and Rousseau, rulers had to at least *claim* to govern for the people's benefit, even when they did not.
