# Key People and Groups — Unit 5.1: The Enlightenment

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## John Locke (1632–1704)
**Region:** England | **Period:** Late 17th–early 18th century

- Argued that humans are born with natural rights to **life, liberty, and property** — not granted by kings or God, but inherent to human nature.
- Developed the **social contract**: government legitimacy depends on the *consent of the governed*; if a government violates natural rights, citizens have the right of **revolution**.
- His *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) directly influenced Thomas Jefferson's language in the Declaration of Independence and the broader Atlantic revolutionary tradition.

> [!exam-tip]
> Locke is the single most tested Enlightenment thinker on the AP exam. Know the phrase "consent of the governed" and its connection to the American Revolution.

---

## Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778)
**Region:** Geneva/France | **Period:** 18th century

- More radical than Locke: argued that civilization corrupts human nature and that private property is the root of inequality.
- Developed the concept of the **"general will"** — the collective interest of all citizens — as the true basis of political authority, not just majority rule.
- His *Social Contract* (1762) influenced the **French Revolution**, particularly its more radical phases (Jacobin democracy, Robespierre's justifications for the Terror).

---

## Voltaire (1694–1778)
**Region:** France | **Period:** 18th century

- Advocated for **religious toleration**, freedom of speech, and freedom of the press through biting satire (*Candide*, 1759).
- A sharp critic of the Catholic Church ("*Écrasez l'infâme!*" — "Crush the infamous thing!") and of arbitrary monarchical power.
- Represents the *philosophe* tradition: public intellectuals who used wit and accessibility to spread Enlightenment ideas to a broad audience.

---

## Baron de Montesquieu (1689–1755)
**Region:** France | **Period:** 18th century

- Studied political systems comparatively in *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), arguing that the best government system depends on a society's geography, climate, and culture.
- Championed **separation of powers** among executive, legislative, and judicial branches as the key mechanism for preventing tyranny.
- His direct influence on the U.S. Constitution (separation of powers, checks and balances) makes him an essential AP World History figure.

---

## Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797)
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** Late 18th century

- Wrote *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792), arguing that reason is not a male-only capacity and that women deserve equal education and civil rights.
- Directly challenged male Enlightenment thinkers who preached universal reason while excluding women from political participation.
- Her work is the founding text of modern Western feminism and a key example of how marginalized groups used Enlightenment logic *against* its own authors.

---

## Adam Smith (1723–1790)
**Region:** Scotland | **Period:** 18th century

- In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), argued that **free markets** — not mercantilism or government regulation — generate the greatest economic prosperity through the "invisible hand" of competition and self-interest.
- Introduced the concept of the **division of labor** as a driver of productivity.
- His work provided the ideological foundation for **laissez-faire capitalism** and became the intellectual bible of 19th-century liberal economics and free trade movements.

---

## Denis Diderot (1713–1784) and the *Encyclopédistes*
**Region:** France | **Period:** 18th century

- Diderot edited the *Encyclopédie* (1751–1772), a 28-volume compendium of human knowledge that implicitly challenged Church and royal authority by celebrating reason, science, and craft knowledge.
- The *Encyclopédistes* (contributors including Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu, d'Alembert) formed a loose intellectual network that collectively defined the French Enlightenment.
- The *Encyclopédie* was twice banned by French authorities — demonstrating that the authorities recognized its subversive political implications.

---

## Salonnières (e.g., Marie Thérèse Rodet Geoffrin, Madame du Deffand)
**Region:** France | **Period:** 18th century

- Elite women who hosted **salons** — intellectual gatherings that brought philosophers, artists, writers, and aristocrats together to debate Enlightenment ideas.
- Despite being excluded from formal political roles and denied the natural rights that male philosophes championed, salonnières were essential organizers and patrons of the Enlightenment's intellectual life.
- Their role illustrates the **gender paradox** of the Enlightenment: women enabled its spread while being denied its promises.

---

## Isaac Newton (1643–1727)
**Region:** England | **Period:** Late 17th century (Scientific Revolution)

- His laws of motion and universal gravitation (*Principia Mathematica*, 1687) became the model of rational explanation that Enlightenment thinkers sought to apply to human society.
- Enlightenment thinkers believed that, just as Newton had discovered the rational laws governing the physical world, reason could discover the natural laws governing human society and government.
- Newton is the *intellectual ancestor* of the Enlightenment, not an Enlightenment thinker himself — but his work made the Enlightenment possible.
