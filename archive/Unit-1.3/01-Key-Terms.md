# Unit 1.3: Key Terms — South and Southeast Asia (1200–1450)

## Vocabulary

**Delhi Sultanate** — A series of Muslim dynasties (1206–1526) ruling northern India from Delhi; the first major Muslim political power in South Asia. (Significance: introduced Persian-Islamic governance and culture to the Indian subcontinent; laid the foundations for the later Mughal Empire. See [[Unit-3.1/01-Key-Terms#Mughal Empire]].)

**Muhammad of Ghur** — Afghan conqueror (d. 1206) whose campaigns established Muslim political control over northern India and whose general Qutb-ud-Din Aibak founded the Delhi Sultanate. (Significance: marks the beginning of sustained Muslim rule in South Asia, a defining turning point in Indian history.)

**Hindu-Muslim syncretism** — The blending of Hindu and Islamic religious, artistic, and cultural practices that emerged under the Delhi Sultanate and flourished in later centuries. (Significance: produced hybrid art forms (Indo-Islamic architecture), musical traditions, and philosophical movements that define Indian cultural identity.)

**Bhakti movement** — A Hindu devotional tradition that emphasized personal, emotional devotion to a deity over ritual and caste distinctions; flourished partly in response to the Muslim presence. (Significance: challenged Brahmin authority, crossed caste lines, and produced devotional poetry and music that shaped Hinduism for centuries.)

**Khmer Empire** — A powerful Hindu-Buddhist state (802–1431) in mainland Southeast Asia (modern Cambodia), famous for the temple complex of Angkor Wat. (Significance: demonstrates how Indian cultural influence — Hinduism, Buddhism, Sanskrit — shaped state formation in Southeast Asia without direct Indian political control.)

**Angkor Wat** — The massive temple complex built by the Khmer king Suryavarman II (early 12th century) in honor of Vishnu; later converted to Buddhist use. (Significance: the largest religious monument in the world; exemplifies the political use of religious architecture to legitimize royal power — a pattern found across South and Southeast Asia.)

**Majapahit Empire** — A Hindu-Buddhist maritime empire (1293–c.1520) based in Java that dominated the Indonesian archipelago and controlled key Spice Islands trade routes. (Significance: shows how Indian cultural forms could support a powerful maritime commercial state; declined as Islam spread along Indian Ocean routes.)

**Srivijaya** — A Buddhist maritime state (7th–13th century) based in Sumatra that controlled the Strait of Malacca and dominated Indian Ocean trade in Southeast Asia. (Significance: demonstrates that Buddhist commercial states could leverage geography to build wealth and power; its decline opened space for Majapahit and eventually Islamic port states.)

**Malacca (Melaka) Sultanate** — An Islamic trading port founded c. 1400 on the Malay Peninsula that became the dominant commercial hub of Southeast Asia. (Significance: Islam spread rapidly through Southeast Asia via Malacca's merchants; demonstrates how conversion to Islam could facilitate participation in the broader Indian Ocean commercial network. Note: Samudra Pasai in northern Sumatra was an earlier Islamic polity, c. 1267.)

**Monsoon winds** — Seasonal wind patterns over the Indian Ocean that blow northeast in winter and southwest in summer, enabling reliable sailing between South Asia, Southeast Asia, East Africa, and the Arabian Peninsula. (Significance: the physical mechanism underlying all Indian Ocean trade; merchants planned entire commercial cycles around the monsoons.)

**Indian Ocean trade network** — The interconnected maritime system linking the coasts of East Africa, Arabia, Persia, India, Southeast Asia, and China through monsoon-driven commerce. (Significance: the most commercially active maritime zone in the world during this period; transmitted Islam, Buddhism, crops, technologies, and peoples across vast distances.)

**Vijayanagara Empire** — A powerful Hindu state (1336–1646) in southern India that resisted Delhi Sultanate expansion and became a center of Hindu culture and long-distance trade. (Significance: demonstrates regional resistance to Muslim political expansion; southern India maintained a distinct Hindu political tradition while the north came under Islamic rule.)

**Wet-rice agriculture** — The cultivation of rice in flooded paddies, which produces high caloric yields and supports dense populations; the agricultural foundation of most Southeast Asian states. (Significance: enabled the large populations and food surpluses that supported cities, temples, and armies across Southeast Asia.)

**Sanskrit** — The classical literary and religious language of South Asia, spread to Southeast Asia along with Hinduism and Buddhism. (Significance: served as a prestige language and cultural connector for Southeast Asian elites, much as Latin did in medieval Europe; demonstrates the reach of Indian cultural influence without direct conquest.)

**Sufism (in South and Southeast Asia)** — The mystical tradition within Islam whose practitioners were the primary agents of Islam's spread into South Asia and the Malay world. (Significance: Sufi teachers adapted Islamic practice to local cultural contexts, making conversion more accessible; their missionary role is a major reason Islam spread peacefully through trade rather than conquest in much of Southeast Asia. See [[Unit-1.2/01-Key-Terms#Sufism]].)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests the **methods of Islam's spread** in Southeast Asia: it spread primarily through **trade and Sufi missionaries**, not conquest (unlike its spread in parts of the Middle East and India). Know this distinction for SAQ and DBQ responses.

> [!exam-tip]
> When analyzing Southeast Asian states, use the phrase **"Indian cultural influence without Indian political control"** — it captures how Hinduism and Buddhism shaped governance, art, and social structure across the region while no Indian empire ever conquered Southeast Asia.
