# Unit 2.6: Environmental Consequences of Connectivity

#apwh #unit-2 #period-1

## Overview

Trade networks that connected Afro-Eurasian populations also connected their disease environments, enabling pathogens to travel at the speed of commerce across populations that had no prior immunity. The Black Death — caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis* — originated in Central Asia, traveled along Mongol-controlled Silk Road routes in the 1330s–1340s, and reached the Middle East, North Africa, and Europe by 1347, killing an estimated 30–50% of Europe's population and devastation populations across Asia and the Middle East within a decade. Beyond disease, trade networks also spread agricultural crops — cotton, citrus, rice, and sugarcane — that increased food production and population in receiving regions, demonstrating that environmental connectivity cut both ways. The long-term demographic collapse caused by the Black Death reshaped labor relations, religious authority, and political structures across Eurasia, setting the conditions for the Renaissance, the Protestant Reformation, and European expansion.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain the environmental and demographic consequences of increased connectivity through trade networks.
- Analyze how the spread of the Black Death disrupted existing social, political, and economic structures.
- Evaluate the long-term effects of demographic collapse on Eurasian societies.
