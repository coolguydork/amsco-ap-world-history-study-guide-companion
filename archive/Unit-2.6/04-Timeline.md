# Unit 2.6: Timeline — Environmental Consequences of Connectivity

#apwh #unit-2 #period-1

## Big Picture

This timeline tracks the environmental and biological consequences of the same trade networks that generated prosperity — demonstrating that connectivity brings costs as well as benefits, a pattern that recurs throughout world history. From the slow spread of useful crops to the catastrophic explosion of bubonic plague, the events below show how trade routes were arteries through which both life and death could travel.

## Timeline Table

| Date | Event | Region | Environmental/Demographic Consequence |
|------|-------|--------|---------------------------------------|
| c. 900–1000 | Champa rice (fast-ripening variety) spreads from Southeast Asia to China via trade routes | Southeast Asia → South China | Chinese agricultural productivity surges; population grows rapidly from ~60 million to ~120 million by 1200 |
| c. 1000–1200 | Cotton cultivation spreads from South Asia westward along trade routes | South Asia → Middle East → North Africa → Mediterranean | New textile industry develops; cotton becomes major trade good; agricultural landscapes transformed |
| c. 1100–1300 | Citrus fruits spread from South/Southeast Asia into the Middle East and Mediterranean | Indian Ocean → Middle East → Mediterranean | Diet improves; new agricultural commodities emerge; orchards reshape landscapes |
| c. 1200–1250 | Increasing Mongol military activity disrupts steppe ecosystems, driving rodent populations carrying *Y. pestis* into contact with trade routes | Central Asia (steppe regions) | Ecological disruption creates conditions for plague transmission from animal to human populations |
| c. 1206–1279 | Mongol conquests create Pax Mongolica — road networks, trade caravanserais, movement of people and animals across Eurasia | Central Asia, China, Persia, Russia | Trade intensification accelerates movement of pathogens as well as goods; plague reservoir connects to trade corridors |
| c. 1331 | First recorded outbreak of plague-like disease in Hebei province, China | North China | Significant mortality in Chinese population; demographic pressure on an already-stressed post-Mongol-conquest society |
| c. 1338–1339 | Plague documented among populations near Lake Issyk-Kul in Central Asia (modern Kyrgyzstan) | Central Asia | Evidence from tombstone inscriptions; Central Asian origin of the Black Death confirmed by modern DNA studies |
| 1346 | Plague reaches the Crimea (Black Sea coast); Genoese merchants at Caffa besieged by Mongol forces allegedly using plague-infected corpses | Black Sea region | Disease crosses from Central Asian trade routes to Mediterranean commercial networks via Italian merchants |
| 1347 | Genoese merchant ships arrive in Sicily carrying plague; disease spreads to southern Italy | Sicily, southern Italy | Beginning of the Black Death in Europe; port cities struck first and hardest |
| 1347–1348 | Plague spreads to major Mediterranean port cities: Genoa, Venice, Marseille, Barcelona | Mediterranean Europe | Urban populations devastated; trade networks that brought the plague now spread it rapidly |
| 1348–1349 | Plague spreads across France, England, the Holy Roman Empire, Scandinavia | Western and Northern Europe | 30–50% mortality in affected regions; Venice loses roughly 60% of its population; some villages completely depopulated |
| 1348–1351 | Flagellant movements spread across Germany and the Low Countries; pogroms against Jewish communities in Rhine Valley | Central and Western Europe | Social disorder; Church authority challenged; Jewish communities massacred in Strasbourg, Mainz, and dozens of other cities |
| 1351 | English Statute of Laborers attempts to cap wages at pre-plague levels | England | Evidence of labor shortage severity; legislation largely fails as workers demand more; seeds of Peasants' Revolt planted |
| 1370s–1380s | Labor shortages contribute to peasant uprisings: Peasants' Revolt in England (1381), Jacquerie in France (1358) | Western Europe | Feudal labor obligations increasingly challenged; serfdom declines in Western Europe over next century |
| c. 1400 | European population begins slow recovery, but remains below pre-plague levels for over a century | Europe | Demographic recovery fuels economic expansion; survivors with inherited wealth drive luxury goods demand |

> [!exam-tip]
> Note how the timeline shows the plague traveling along trade routes: Central Asia → Black Sea → Mediterranean ports → inland Europe. This geographic pattern is the evidence for the argument that trade network connectivity caused the pandemic — it didn't appear everywhere at once, it followed the routes.
