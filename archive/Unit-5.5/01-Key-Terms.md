# Key Terms — Unit 5.5: Technology in the Industrial Age

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **First Industrial Revolution (c. 1760–1850)** — The initial phase of industrialization, centered on textiles (cotton and wool), coal, and iron, powered by steam; primarily associated with Britain. (Distinguished from the Second Industrial Revolution by its focus on mechanical production and coal/steam energy rather than electrical and chemical processes.)

2. **Second Industrial Revolution (c. 1870–1914)** — The later phase of industrialization, centered on steel, organic chemicals, electricity, and the internal combustion engine; more science-based and primarily associated with Germany and the United States. (Produced the technologies that dominated the 20th century: automobiles, airplanes, synthetic chemicals, mass electrification.)

3. **Bessemer process (1856)** — A method of mass-producing steel by blasting air through molten pig iron, invented by Henry Bessemer; dramatically reduced the cost of steel relative to iron. (Made steel — stronger, more flexible, and more durable than iron — affordable for rails, bridges, ships, and buildings; foundation of the Second Industrial Revolution.)

4. **Steel** — An alloy of iron with a small percentage of carbon, stronger and more versatile than iron; produced cheaply at scale through the Bessemer process. (Enabled the construction of railroads, suspension bridges, ocean-going steamships, and eventually skyscrapers — the structural material of modern civilization.)

5. **Railroad** — A transportation system using iron or steel tracks on which steam-powered locomotives haul freight and passengers; the defining infrastructure technology of 19th-century industrialization. (Reduced freight costs by ~90%; integrated national markets; drove demand for coal, iron, and steel; reshaped settlement patterns and labor migration.)

6. **George Stephenson (1781–1848)** — British engineer who designed the *Rocket* locomotive and the Liverpool-Manchester Railway (1830), the world's first commercially successful intercity steam railroad. (His success inaugurated the global railroad age; within 20 years, railroad construction was underway across Europe, North America, and India.)

7. **Steamship** — A vessel powered by a steam engine turning a paddle wheel or propeller; replaced sailing ships for long-distance oceanic trade from the 1830s–1870s. (Reduced transoceanic voyage times from weeks to days; made regular, scheduled shipping possible; transformed global commodity markets and migration patterns.)

8. **Telegraph** — An electrical device for transmitting encoded messages over long distances through wires; developed simultaneously by Morse (USA) and Cooke and Wheatstone (Britain) in the 1830s–1840s. (First communication technology faster than human travel; transformed commerce, military coordination, journalism, and imperialism — for the first time, the speed of information could exceed the speed of goods.)

9. **Samuel Morse (1791–1872)** — American artist and inventor who developed the electromagnetic telegraph and the Morse code system (dots and dashes representing letters) in the 1830s–1840s. (The telegraph he developed became the global standard; the first transatlantic telegraph cable was laid in 1866.)

10. **Transatlantic telegraph cable (1866)** — The submarine telegraph cable connecting North America and Europe, finally successfully laid by Cyrus Field after multiple failed attempts. (For the first time, a message could cross the Atlantic in minutes rather than weeks — compressing the communication geography of the Atlantic world.)

11. **Thomas Edison (1847–1931)** — American inventor who developed the practical incandescent light bulb (1879), the phonograph, and the first commercial electrical power distribution system (Pearl Street Station, New York, 1882). (His Menlo Park laboratory pioneered the model of industrial research — systematic, team-based invention aimed at commercial applications rather than theoretical science.)

12. **Nikola Tesla (1856–1943)** — Serbian-American inventor who developed alternating current (AC) electrical systems and the AC induction motor, enabling long-distance electrical transmission. (The Tesla-Edison rivalry ("War of Currents") was resolved in AC's favor — Edison's DC systems could not transmit electricity more than a mile; Tesla's AC could cross continents.)

13. **Internal combustion engine** — An engine that burns fuel (gasoline or diesel) inside cylinders to generate power; developed by Nikolaus Otto (1876) and refined by Karl Benz and Gottlieb Daimler (1880s). (Foundation of the automobile and airplane industries; replaced steam in transportation for most purposes during the 20th century.)

14. **Suez Canal (1869)** — An artificial waterway connecting the Mediterranean Sea to the Red Sea, built by the French-Egyptian Suez Canal Company and opened in 1869; dramatically shortened the sea route from Europe to Asia. (Illustrates how engineering technology transformed global trade routes; also central to British imperial strategy — Britain purchased 44% ownership in 1875.)

15. **Panama Canal (1914)** — An artificial waterway connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans through Panama, built by the USA after France failed; opened in 1914. (Another example of engineering technology transforming global shipping; also demonstrates American imperial ambition and its connection to industrial capability.)

16. **Electricity** — The controlled flow of electrons used to power lights, motors, and communication devices; harnessed for practical use from the 1870s–1880s. (The defining energy technology of the late 19th and 20th centuries; transformed urban life through electric lighting, streetcars, and eventually industrial automation.)

> [!exam-tip]
> AP stimulus questions on technology often ask you to connect a specific technology to **broader historical patterns**. Practice explaining: railroad → market integration → industrial-scale production viable → urbanization → labor organizing → labor reform. One technology connects to dozens of historical consequences.
