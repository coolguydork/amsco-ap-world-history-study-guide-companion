# Unit 1.1: Key People and Groups — East Asia (1200–1450)

## Individuals and Groups

### Song Dynasty (960–1279)
**Period:** 960–1279 | **Region:** China

- Developed the most sophisticated economy in the world by 1200, with commercialized agriculture, urban markets, and early forms of credit
- Patronized Neo-Confucian scholars and expanded the civil service examination system, creating a literate bureaucratic class
- Produced world-leading innovations in printing, gunpowder, paper money, and naval technology that would eventually transform global history

---

### Kublai Khan
**Period:** 1260–1294 | **Region:** East Asia / China

- Grandson of Genghis Khan; founder of the Yuan Dynasty (1271) and Great Khan of the Mongol Empire
- Conquered the Southern Song Dynasty in 1279, completing Mongol control of China, and attempted (failed) invasions of Japan and Java
- Promoted trade along the Silk Roads and welcomed foreign merchants, including Marco Polo, making the Yuan court a cosmopolitan center

---

### Zhu Yuanzhang (Hongwu Emperor)
**Period:** 1368–1398 | **Region:** China

- A peasant rebel who overthrew the Yuan Dynasty and founded the Ming Dynasty in 1368; ruled as the Hongwu Emperor
- Dismantled Mongol institutions, restored the civil service examination, and strengthened centralized imperial authority — including abolishing the position of prime minister
- Implemented agrarian reforms and resettlement programs to rebuild a China devastated by decades of Mongol rule and plague

---

### Zheng He
**Period:** 1371–1433 | **Region:** China, Indian Ocean

- Muslim Chinese admiral who commanded seven massive naval expeditions (1405–1433) on behalf of the Yongle Emperor
- His fleet of "treasure ships" dwarfed contemporary European vessels; traditional accounts describe the largest ships at over 400 feet, though modern historians debate the actual dimensions — more conservative estimates place them at around 200–250 feet, still far larger than anything Europe had deployed at the time
- The voyages were ended after his death due to Confucian scholar-bureaucrats' opposition to "maritime adventurism"; China turned inward, a pivotal moment of historical contingency

> [!exam-tip]
> Zheng He is a top AP exam figure. Be ready to explain **why the voyages ended** (Confucian conservatism, cost, factional politics) and contrast this with European exploration that began just decades later.

---

### Yongle Emperor (Zhu Di)
**Period:** 1402–1424 | **Region:** China

- Third Ming emperor who moved the capital to Beijing and commissioned construction of the Forbidden City, a monument to centralized imperial authority
- Expanded the tribute system across Southeast Asia and sponsored Zheng He's voyages as diplomatic extensions of Ming prestige (see [[Unit-2.3/02-Key-People-and-Groups]] for full Zheng He coverage)
- Ordered the compilation of the Yongle Encyclopedia (~22,000 volumes), reflecting the dynasty's ambition to systematize and preserve all classical Chinese knowledge

---

### Dai Viet (Vietnamese Kingdom)
**Period:** c. 939–1804 | **Region:** Southeast Asia (modern Vietnam)

- Maintained political independence from China despite lying directly on its southern border, having expelled Chinese rule in 938 CE
- Adopted Chinese-style Confucian governance, the civil service examination, and Chinese written script while preserving the Vietnamese spoken language and distinct cultural identity — a nuanced example of Sinification
- Successfully repelled three Mongol invasion attempts (1258, 1285, 1288) through guerrilla tactics and naval ambushes, one of the few polities to do so

---

### Scholar-Gentry Class
**Period:** Song–Ming | **Region:** China

- The educated elite who passed civil service examinations based on Confucian classics; held administrative posts at all levels of government
- Served as cultural intermediaries between the imperial court and local society, maintaining Confucian moral order
- Their dominance explains both the extraordinary cultural continuity of Chinese civilization and the resistance to maritime expansion under the Ming

---

### Korean Goryeo Dynasty (918–1392)
**Period:** 918–1392 | **Region:** Korea

- Buddhism remained the dominant state religion for most of Goryeo's history; Neo-Confucianism gained influence only in the late Goryeo period (late 14th century) and became fully dominant under the successor Joseon Dynasty (1392–1897). The Goryeo court adopted Chinese-style civil service examinations and engaged Neo-Confucian ideas, but it would be an overstatement to characterize the dynasty as Neo-Confucian overall
- Developed celadon pottery and woodblock printing (the Tripitaka Koreana, 1251) reflecting the syncretic blending of Chinese and Korean traditions
- Became a vassal state of the Mongol Yuan Dynasty, demonstrating the reach of Mongol imperial power into East Asia

---

### Japanese Shogunate (Kamakura, 1185–1333)
**Period:** 1185–1333 | **Region:** Japan

- A military government (bakufu) that ruled Japan in the name of the emperor; samurai class wielded real political power
- Successfully repelled two Mongol invasions (1274, 1281), aided by typhoons the Japanese called *kamikaze* ("divine winds")
- Reflects the limits of Mongol expansion and the durability of decentralized, militarized governance in Japan

> [!warning]
> Japan's political structure (emperor as figurehead, shogun as real ruler) is a common source of confusion. The emperor had cultural/religious authority; the shogun had military/political power.
