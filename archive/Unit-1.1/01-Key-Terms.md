# Unit 1.1: Key Terms — East Asia (1200–1450)

## Vocabulary

**Song Dynasty** — Chinese imperial dynasty (960–1279) known for economic prosperity, Neo-Confucian philosophy, and technological innovation. (Significance: set the cultural and institutional template for later Chinese dynasties; its innovations spread globally via trade.)

**Yuan Dynasty** — Mongol-ruled Chinese dynasty (1271–1368) founded by Kublai Khan; first foreign dynasty to rule all of China. (Significance: integrated China into the Mongol trade network and facilitated east-west cultural exchange. See [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Kublai Khan]].)

**Ming Dynasty** — Chinese imperial dynasty (1368–1644) founded by Zhu Yuanzhang after overthrowing the Yuan; reasserted Han Chinese rule and Confucian values. (Significance: sponsor of Zheng He's voyages and builder of the Great Wall in its current form.)

**Neo-Confucianism** — A philosophical movement blending classical [[Confucianism]] with Buddhist and Daoist ideas, emphasizing moral self-cultivation and social hierarchy. (Significance: became the dominant state ideology in China, Korea, Vietnam, and Japan; reinforced patriarchal social structures.)

**Civil Service Examination** — A system of merit-based government recruitment requiring candidates to demonstrate mastery of Confucian classics. (Significance: created a scholar-gentry class loyal to the emperor; promoted social mobility in theory while favoring elites in practice.)

**Scholar-Gentry** — The educated elite class in China who passed civil service exams and held administrative positions. (Significance: served as the backbone of Chinese bureaucracy; shaped cultural values for centuries.)

**Tribute System** — A diplomatic framework in which neighboring states sent gifts and acknowledged Chinese superiority in exchange for trading rights and protection. (Significance: organized East Asian international relations; used by the Ming to extend influence without direct conquest.)

**Junks** — Large Chinese sailing vessels with watertight bulkheads and multiple masts, enabling long-distance ocean voyages. (Significance: made the Indian Ocean trade network accessible to Chinese merchants and admirals like [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Zheng He]].)

**Champa Rice** — A fast-ripening, drought-resistant rice variety imported from Vietnam to China during the Song Dynasty. (Significance: dramatically increased agricultural yields, supported population growth, and freed labor for craft production.)

**Foot Binding** — The practice of tightly binding young girls' feet to keep them small, seen as a mark of beauty and status in Song China. (Significance: reflects the intensification of patriarchal control during the Song; spread across social classes over time.)

**Gunpowder** — A Chinese invention used initially in fireworks, then adapted for military use in bombs, fire arrows, and eventually firearms. (Significance: one of the most globally consequential inventions in world history; transformed warfare across Eurasia.)

**Movable Type** — A printing technology using individual, reusable characters, refined in Song China. (Significance: accelerated the spread of literacy, Buddhism, Neo-Confucianism, and administrative documents.)

**Paper Money** — Government-issued currency (jiaozi) first used in Song China to facilitate large-scale commercial transactions. (Significance: indicates the sophistication of the Chinese market economy and prefigures modern banking.)

**Zheng He's Voyages** — A series of seven state-sponsored expeditions (1405–1433) led by the Muslim admiral Zheng He, reaching Southeast Asia, India, Arabia, and East Africa. (Significance: demonstrates Ming tribute-system diplomacy and naval capability; their cancellation reflects Confucian conservative priorities. Primary coverage: [[Unit-2.3/00-Overview|Unit 2.3: Exchange in the Indian Ocean]])

**Dai Viet** — The Vietnamese kingdom (c. 939–1804) that maintained independence from China despite centuries of cultural borrowing. (Significance: a key example of Sinification's limits — Vietnam adopted Chinese writing, Confucian governance, and the civil service exam while preserving a distinct language and national identity; successfully expelled the Mongols in three invasions, 1258–1288.)

**Great Wall of China** — A series of fortifications along China's northern border, massively expanded by the Ming Dynasty using forced labor. (Significance: reflects Ming anxiety about northern nomadic threats — particularly the Mongols — and the costs of defensive empire-building.)

**Daoism** — A Chinese philosophical and religious tradition emphasizing harmony with nature, simplicity, and the "Way" (Dao). (Significance: one of the three pillars of Chinese thought alongside Confucianism and Buddhism; influenced art, medicine, and governance.)

**Sinification** — The process by which neighboring peoples adopted Chinese cultural practices, language, writing, and governance systems. (Significance: explains how Chinese cultural influence spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam without direct conquest.)

**Khanate** — A Mongol-ruled political unit governed by a khan; after the empire split, four khanates controlled different regions including China (Yuan). (Significance: maintained a degree of Mongol coordination while allowing regional adaptation. See [[Unit-1.2/01-Key-Terms#Mongol Empire]].)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about the **continuities** in Chinese governance across dynasties (civil service exams, Confucian bureaucracy) as well as the **changes** brought by Mongol rule. Be ready to compare Song, Yuan, and Ming policies.
