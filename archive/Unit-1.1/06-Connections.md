# Unit 1.1: Cross-Topic Connections — Developments in East Asia

## Concepts This Topic Builds On

- Pre-1200 Tang Dynasty China — The Tang (618–907) established the civil service exam, spread Chinese culture to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, and opened the Silk Roads to Buddhist and Islamic exchange; the Song inherited this foundation
- Buddhism and Daoism as regional traditions — Both had spread from India and China across East Asia before 1200; Neo-Confucianism in the Song period develops partly as an intellectual response to these traditions
- The early Silk Roads — Central Asian and Arab merchants connected China to West Asia and the Mediterranean for centuries before 1200; the Song commercial revolution amplified this existing network

## Concepts This Topic Sets Up

- [[Unit-1.2/00-Overview|Unit 1.2: Developments in Dar al-Islam]] — The Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258) is the same empire that established the Yuan Dynasty in China; the Islamic and East Asian worlds are linked through Mongol politics
- [[Unit-2.1/00-Overview|Unit 2.1: The Silk Roads]] — Chinese silk, porcelain, and paper money are the premier luxury goods of the Silk Road network; Song commercial innovations (paper money, letters of credit) reshape how Silk Road trade is financed
- [[Unit-2.2/00-Overview|Unit 2.2: The Mongol Empire and the Modern World]] — The Yuan Dynasty is the eastern khanate of the Mongol Empire; understanding the Song-to-Yuan transition is essential to understanding the Pax Mongolica
- [[Unit-2.3/00-Overview|Unit 2.3: Exchange in the Indian Ocean]] — Zheng He's voyages (1405–1433) represent the Ming state's brief engagement with the Indian Ocean network; his fleet demonstrates the scale of Chinese maritime capacity before the decision to withdraw
- Unit 3 (Land-Based Empires) — The Ming administrative model (centralized bureaucracy, civil service exam, tributary relations) directly influences how neighboring states organized governance
- Unit 4 (Transoceanic) — Chinese inventions diffused westward (gunpowder → firearms, compass → navigation) and directly enabled European maritime expansion; Chinese demand for silver drove the global silver trade

## Comparative Civilizations and Contrasts

- **Song China vs. Medieval Europe:** In the same era when European feudalism fragmented political authority, Song China had a centralized state, meritocratic bureaucracy, paper money, and a thriving commercial economy — a sharp contrast in political organization and economic development
- **Yuan Dynasty vs. Ilkhanate (Mongol Persia):** The Mongols governed China by maintaining Chinese administrative structures while staffing them with Central Asian personnel; compare with the Ilkhanate in Persia, where Mongol rulers converted to Islam within generations — showing how Mongol rule adapted to different conquered cultures
- **Ming withdrawal vs. European expansion:** Ming China had the technological capacity for sustained oceanic exploration (demonstrated by Zheng He) but chose political retrenchment after 1433; European states were simultaneously launching the Age of Exploration — a powerful example of how political decisions, not just technology, shape historical outcomes
- **Chinese tribute system vs. Islamic commercial networks:** Two competing models for long-distance relations — China's tribute system is hierarchical and state-directed (China at center); Islamic merchant networks are decentralized and commercially driven

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks why China stopped the Zheng He voyages. The answer is **political** (Confucian officials opposed maritime commerce as wasteful and un-Confucian), not technological. Know this causation distinction — it is a classic AP essay trap.

> [!exam-tip]
> When comparing Song China to other 1200–1450 civilizations, lead with **Neo-Confucianism** as the defining cultural system. It shapes gender relations (reinforcing patriarchy, associated with foot-binding), social hierarchy, and political philosophy across East Asia — and is explicitly tested on the AP exam.
