# Unit 3 Overview: Land-Based Power (c. 1450–1750)

#apwh #unit-3 #period-2

> [!exam-tip]
> Unit 3 covers AP World History **Period 2 (c. 1450–1750)**, shared with Unit 4. Together, Period 2 accounts for roughly **12–15% of the MCQ section**. Unit 3 is among the highest-yield units on the exam — expect SAQ and LEQ prompts asking you to compare how two different empires justified or maintained power. The devshirme system, mansabdar system, and civil service examination are frequently tested in stimulus-based MCQs.

---

## Unit Summary

Between 1450 and 1750, the most powerful states on earth were not the maritime empires of western Europe but the vast land-based empires of Afro-Eurasia. The Ottoman Empire controlled the eastern Mediterranean and the Middle East; the Safavid Empire dominated Persia; the Mughal Empire ruled the Indian subcontinent; the Qing Dynasty unified China; and the Russian Empire expanded eastward across Eurasia. These states used gunpowder weapons to conquer territory, then built sophisticated administrative, legal, and religious systems to hold diverse, multiethnic populations together. Each empire faced the same fundamental challenge: how to project power over vast distances with pre-industrial technology. Their solutions — slave soldiers, tax farming, religious legitimation, monumental architecture, and the co-optation of local elites — are the core content of this unit and the primary material for AP comparison essays.

## The Big Picture: Unit 3 in One Paragraph

In 1450, the Mongol Empire's collapse had left a power vacuum across Eurasia. Over the next century, a new category of state filled that vacuum: the **gunpowder empire**. Firearms and cannons — originating in China, diffused through the Islamic world — gave well-organized states an overwhelming advantage over nomadic cavalry, city walls, and smaller neighbors. The Ottoman Empire capitalized first, seizing Constantinople in 1453 and eventually controlling a swath of territory from North Africa to Mesopotamia. The Safavids, Mughals, Qing, and Russians followed different paths to the same end: vast, multiethnic empires governed by sophisticated bureaucracies and legitimized by religious authority. But military conquest was the easy part — *administration* was the enduring problem. How do you extract taxes, maintain loyalty, and project authority across millions of square kilometers without telephones, railways, or modern bureaucracies? Unit 3 is the answer to that question. By 1750, these empires were beginning to show the strains — internal fragmentation, succession crises, fiscal pressure, and growing European commercial competition — that would accelerate into crisis in Unit 5.

## AP Thematic Synthesis Across Unit 3

### Governance
Every empire in Unit 3 combined **military dominance** with **administrative legitimation**. The Ottomans used the devshirme system (enslaved Christian boys trained as loyal Janissary soldiers and administrators), separating military power from hereditary nobility. The Mughals used the mansabdar system (ranked military-administrative appointments tied to revenue grants) to manage a diverse nobility. The Qing maintained Manchu ethnic dominance through the Eight Banners while co-opting Han Chinese through the civil service examination. The Safavids relied on a Shia clerical class (ulama) to legitimize the shah's authority. Russia under Peter the Great and Catherine the Great used Western-style bureaucratic reforms to centralize power over the nobility. The common thread: **loyalty engineering** — finding ways to make powerful subjects dependent on the ruler rather than on independent power bases.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to compare the administrative methods of **two** land-based empires. High-yield pairs: Ottoman (devshirme) vs. Mughal (mansabdar); Qing (examination + Eight Banners) vs. Ottoman (devshirme); Russian (Table of Ranks) vs. Qing (civil service examination).

### Economic Systems
Land-based empires extracted wealth primarily through **taxation of agricultural production** and **control of trade routes**, not oceanic commerce. The Ottoman timar system granted cavalry soldiers tax rights over defined territories in exchange for military service — a feudal-like arrangement that later gave way to tax farming as the empire's fiscal needs grew. The Safavids created a royal monopoly on Persian silk — the empire's primary export — selling it through Armenian merchant networks to European buyers, deliberately bypassing Ottoman middlemen. The Mughal state extracted revenue through a sophisticated land assessment system (the *zabt*) that attempted to tax actual agricultural output rather than fixed assessments. The Qing inherited and expanded the Ming fiscal system, supplementing it with tribute from frontier regions. All five empires struggled with the **fiscal-military cycle**: wars required revenue; revenue required taxation; taxation required a functioning bureaucracy; a functioning bureaucracy required stability that wars disrupted.

### Cultural Developments and Religion
Religion was both the most powerful tool of legitimation and the most dangerous source of conflict. The Ottoman sultans claimed the title of Caliph (defender of Sunni Islam) and used the ulama to administer religious law across the empire. The Safavid state made **Shia Islam** the official religion of Persia — a deliberate distinction from Sunni Ottoman neighbors that created a lasting sectarian frontier still visible today. Mughal Emperor Akbar attempted a radical experiment in **religious syncretism** (the Din-i-Ilahi), while his grandson Aurangzeb reversed course and imposed strict Sunni orthodoxy, triggering the Maratha revolts that would fragment the empire. In Europe, the **Protestant Reformation** shattered Christian unity and produced a century of religious warfare, ultimately forcing a separation of church and state sovereignty (Peace of Westphalia, 1648). The **Scientific Revolution** challenged the Catholic Church's intellectual authority, shifting the epistemological foundation of European thought from revealed scripture to empirical observation.

### Social Structures
All five empires governed **multiethnic, multi-religious** populations and developed systems to manage diversity. The Ottomans used the **millet system** — allowing non-Muslim communities (Greek Orthodox Christians, Jews, Armenians) to govern themselves under their own religious laws in personal matters. The Mughals tolerated Hindu practices under Akbar but reversed this under Aurangzeb. The Qing imposed the queue hairstyle on Han Chinese as a visible marker of submission while otherwise allowing considerable cultural continuity. Women's roles were formally subordinated across all empires — the Ottoman harem, Mughal purdah, and Confucian gender norms all restricted elite women's public roles — yet powerful women (Roxelana, Nur Jahan, Cixi's later predecessor regents) exercised significant influence through household and family networks.

### Technology and Environment
Gunpowder — the defining technology of Unit 3 — originated in China (Unit 1), diffused through the Islamic world, and was deployed most aggressively by these five empires. The **cannon** made medieval fortifications obsolete and allowed relatively small forces to defeat much larger armies sheltering behind walls. Equally important was the **printing press** (Gutenberg, c. 1440), which the Ottoman Empire initially banned to protect scribal guilds — a decision that contributed to the empire's later technological lag relative to Europe. The Scientific Revolution produced new instruments (telescope, barometer, microscope) and new mathematical frameworks (calculus, Newtonian mechanics) that would eventually give European states decisive technological advantages in the 19th century. Environmentally, the period saw significant deforestation driven by imperial demand for shipbuilding timber, charcoal for metallurgy, and agricultural expansion to feed growing urban populations.

### Human Migration
The period's most significant forced migrations were internal to these empires: the devshirme moved Balkan Christian boys to Istanbul; the Qing relocated Han populations away from Manchurian homelands; the Russian Empire forcibly settled nomadic peoples across Siberia. The **Safavid-Ottoman wars** created refugee flows across the Persian-Anatolian frontier. Religious persecution drove Huguenots from France (after the Edict of Fontainebleau, 1685) into Protestant Europe. These migrations transmitted skills, languages, and cultural practices across imperial borders — a less dramatic but equally consequential form of exchange compared to the oceanic migration of Unit 4.

## Comparative: Sunni vs. Shia — The Ottoman-Safavid Divide

One of the AP exam's most reliable comparison points is the **Ottoman-Safavid sectarian rivalry**:

| Feature | Ottoman Empire | Safavid Empire |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| Islamic branch | Sunni | Shia |
| Caliph claim | Yes (after 1517) | No (Shah as quasi-divine figure) |
| Relation to ulama | Ulama as state administrators | Ulama as independent power; sometimes rivals to the Shah |
| Key conflict | Chaldiran (1514) — Ottoman victory; established frontier | Chaldiran loss → Safavid capital moved inland |
| Long-term consequence | Sunni-Shia frontier still defines Iran-Turkey-Iraq boundary | Shia Islam becomes permanent identity of Persian/Iranian civilization |

> [!exam-tip]
> The Sunni-Shia divide is **NOT primarily a theological dispute** for AP purposes — treat it as a **political and identity marker** that empires used to distinguish themselves from rivals and to legitimate internal religious policies.

## AP Exam: Unit 3 High-Frequency Topics

These topics appear disproportionately on AP exams — know them cold:

1. **Devshirme system** — what it was, why it created loyal soldiers/administrators, how it changed over time
2. **Sunni-Shia split** — Ottoman vs. Safavid rivalry; religion as political identity marker
3. **Akbar's religious policies** — Din-i-Ilahi, ibadat khana, tolerance as political strategy
4. **Protestant Reformation** — causes, key figures (Luther, Calvin), consequences (religious wars, Peace of Westphalia)
5. **Qing administration** — Eight Banners, civil service exam continuation, Manchu-Han relations
6. **Mansabdar system** — Mughal administrative rankings, how it differed from Ottoman devshirme
7. **Scientific Revolution** — Copernicus→Galileo→Newton sequence; challenge to Church authority
8. **Ottoman decline** — Janissary interference, timar decay, failed Siege of Vienna (1683)
9. **Safavid collapse** — Shah Abbas I's reforms vs. Afghan invasion (1722); over-reliance on one capable ruler
10. **Peace of Westphalia (1648)** — state sovereignty; *cuius regio, eius religio* originates at Augsburg (1555); Westphalia extends it to include Calvinism

> [!warning]
> **Common AP exam trap:** Do not use European maritime exploration or the Columbian Exchange to explain developments in the Ottoman, Mughal, Safavid, or Qing empires. These are **land-based** stories. European oceanic expansion is Unit 4's story.
