# Unit 1.7: Connections — What Came Before and After

#apwh #unit-1 #period-1

## What This Topic Builds On (Topics 1.1–1.6)

Topic 1.7 is a **synthesis topic** — it has no new content of its own. Instead, it draws on the specific regional knowledge from the six previous topics and asks you to make comparisons across them. Here is what each prior topic contributes:

- **[[Unit-1.1]]** *(Song Dynasty China):* The civil service exam, paper money, commercialized agriculture, Neo-Confucianism, and the tributary system are the core of any comparison involving East Asia in this period. Song China's economic sophistication is the benchmark against which other regions are often compared.

- **[[Unit-1.2]]** *(Mongol Empires and the Making of the Modern World):* The Mongol disruption and the Pax Mongolica are the most important macro-level forces of the period. Any comparison of cross-regional trade or political disruption must account for the Mongols.

- **[[Unit-1.3]]** *(Developments in South and Southeast Asia):* The Delhi Sultanate shows how Islam entered South Asia through conquest; Southeast Asian kingdoms show how trade and religion (Hinduism, Buddhism, then Islam) shaped political culture. These are useful comparison points with Africa's Islamic adoption through trade.

- **[[Unit-1.4]]** *(Developments in the Islamic World):* The fragmentation of the caliphate and the continued vitality of Islamic commerce and culture despite political disruption is the central story. Dar al-Islam as a unified cultural sphere despite political fragmentation is the key AP concept.

- **[[Unit-1.5]]** *(Developments in Africa):* Mali and the Swahili Coast show two different models of African integration into world trade — overland trans-Saharan (Mali) and maritime Indian Ocean (Swahili). Both adopted Islam, but in different ways and through different mechanisms.

- **[[Unit-1.6]]** *(Developments in Europe and the Americas):* Feudal Europe and the emerging Aztec/Inca states provide the bookend comparisons — Europe's decentralized political structure and the Americas' isolation from Old World networks are important contrast points with the more connected regions.

## What This Topic Sets Up (Unit 2 and Beyond)

The patterns established in Unit 1 become the launching pad for the transformations of Unit 2 (Networks of Exchange) and Unit 3 (Land-Based Empires):

- **Unit 2 (c. 1200–1450 Networks):** The trade networks described comparatively in 1.7 — Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan, and later trans-Atlantic — are the subject of Unit 2. Understanding the regional bases established in Unit 1 helps explain who the major players in these networks were and why.

- **Unit 3 (Land-Based Empires, c. 1450–1750):** The states that become the gunpowder empires of Unit 3 — Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal — emerge from the political vacuum left by Mongol collapse. The [[Unit-3.4]] comparison of empires directly parallels the 1.7 comparison but one period later.

- **The Mongol Legacy:** The Pax Mongolica (Unit 1/2 overlap) creates the conditions for the Black Death, which reshapes European and Islamic societies entering the 1450–1750 period. It also sets up China's Ming Dynasty restoration and its brief Indian Ocean dominance (Zheng He voyages).

- **The Spread of Islam:** The Islamic expansion into Southeast Asia and West Africa documented in Unit 1 becomes the foundation for the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires' Islamic legitimation strategies in Unit 3.

## Key Comparative Pairings

### Pairing 1: Song China vs. Mali Empire — Commercial Wealth as the Basis of Power

Both Song China and Mali derived their power from commercial wealth rather than purely agricultural tribute or military conquest. Song China's internal market — powered by paper money, fast-ripening rice surpluses, and porcelain manufacturing — and Mali's control of trans-Saharan gold flows both demonstrate that **economic dominance could sustain political power**. However, they differed fundamentally in structure: Song China had a diversified, internally generated commercial economy, while Mali's wealth was almost entirely dependent on controlling external trade flows as a middleman. This distinction matters because when the trade routes shifted (as they did after the Portuguese opened Atlantic routes in the late 1400s), Mali's economic model was far more vulnerable than China's.

### Pairing 2: Feudal Europe vs. Feudal Japan — Parallel Structures Without Contact

The simultaneous emergence of feudal systems in medieval Europe and Kamakura/Muromachi Japan — with no contact between them — is one of the AP curriculum's most striking parallel developments. Both featured: a warrior elite (knights/samurai) bound to lords through land grants; a nominally supreme ruler (king/emperor or shogun) with limited practical power over autonomous local lords; agricultural surpluses extracted from a peasant base to support the warrior class; and a religious institution (Catholic Church / Buddhism and Shinto) that provided cultural legitimacy outside the feudal hierarchy. The key difference is that Japan had a dual power structure (emperor + shogun) while Europe had a Church-state competition for supremacy, giving each system a different axis of tension.

### Pairing 3: Swahili Coast vs. Italian City-States — Commercial City-States at Network Nodes

The Swahili Coast city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa) and Italian city-states (Venice, Genoa) were both commercially driven, politically independent urban centers that derived their wealth from positioning themselves at key nodes in larger trade networks. Both were cosmopolitan, multicultural, and prosperous far beyond their small geographic footprint. The Swahili cities connected the African interior to the Indian Ocean network; Venice and Genoa connected Northern Europe to the Mediterranean and, through it, to the Islamic world and ultimately the Silk Roads. Neither was a conventional territorial empire — both derived power from **control of trade flows** rather than control of agricultural land. This makes them useful as a comparison type that cuts across geographic region.

### Pairing 4: The Americas vs. Sub-Saharan Africa — State-Building Without Old World Connections

The Americas and Sub-Saharan Africa (outside the Swahili Coast) both underwent significant state-building processes in this period **outside** the main Eurasian trade networks. The Aztec predecessors and Inca predecessors in the Americas were building tributary empires; the Mali Empire and Great Zimbabwe were consolidating large states in Africa. Both regions relied on local agricultural surpluses and regional trade rather than Silk Road or Indian Ocean connections. A key difference: West Africa (Mali) was connected to the Islamic world via the trans-Saharan network, giving its rulers access to Islamic administrative and commercial tools; the Americas remained entirely isolated, meaning their state-building was fully independent and used different administrative technologies (quipu, pictographic writing, mit'a labor).

> [!exam-tip]
> When an AP LEQ asks you to "evaluate the extent" of change or similarity, these comparative pairings are your core evidence. Always frame comparisons with an explicit signal phrase: "Similarly to Song China, the Mali Empire..." or "Unlike feudal Europe, feudal Japan..."

> [!warning]
> Do not argue that the Americas were "less advanced" because they lacked writing or metal tools in the European sense. The AP exam values understanding different systems on their own terms. The Inca mit'a system and quipu records were sophisticated administrative solutions to specific Andean conditions.
