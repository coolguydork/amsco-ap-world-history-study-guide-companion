# Unit 3.3: Empires: Belief Systems

#apwh #unit-3 #period-2 #belief-systems

## Topic Summary

Unit 3.3 examines how the religious landscape of Afro-Eurasia was transformed — and challenged — between 1450 and 1750. Four major developments dominate this chapter: the **Protestant Reformation** fractures Western Christianity; the **Ottoman-Safavid rivalry** institutionalizes the Sunni-Shia divide; new **syncretic religious movements** emerge at the intersection of traditions (above all **Sikhism**); and **missionary activity** spreads Christianity and Islam into new regions, interacting with indigenous beliefs in complex ways. Crucially, Unit 3.3 shows that religious change was not just a spiritual phenomenon — it was inseparable from political power, social identity, and imperial competition.

> [!warning]
> **Scope note:** Vodou and Candomblé are Atlantic-world syncretic traditions that emerged from the forced displacement of enslaved Africans — they belong in Unit 4 (consequences of the Atlantic slave trade and maritime empire), not here. This file covers belief systems as tools of **empire** within the 1450–1750 Afro-Eurasian land-based empire context. Similarly, Rumi (1207–1273) predates this unit's 1450 start date; he appears only as historical background to Sufi orders active in 1450–1750, not as a featured figure.

## The Orthodox Church and Russia

The Eastern Orthodox Christian tradition, centered in Constantinople until its fall in 1453, found its new institutional home in Russia after the Ottoman conquest. Moscow's prince Ivan III articulated the "Third Rome" ideology — the claim that Moscow had inherited the spiritual mantle of Rome and Constantinople as the center of true Christianity. This gave the Russian tsars a religiously grounded claim to imperial authority distinct from both Catholic Europe and the Islamic empires to their south. The Russian Orthodox Church became deeply intertwined with the tsarist state, legitimizing autocracy through religious ritual and theological argument. Importantly, Orthodox Christianity's survival and Russian expansion are a major counter-narrative to the Protestant-Catholic drama of Western Europe: there were **three** major branches of Christianity competing and defining themselves in this period, not two.

## The Wars of Religion

The Protestant Reformation did not produce peaceful coexistence — it produced over a century of devastating warfare in Europe. The **French Wars of Religion** (1562–1598) pitted Huguenots (French Protestants) against Catholics, culminating in the Edict of Nantes (1598), which granted limited toleration. The **Thirty Years' War** (1618–1648) began as a religious conflict in the Holy Roman Empire and became a general European war involving every major power. The **Peace of Westphalia** (1648), which ended the Thirty Years' War, is one of world history's most consequential diplomatic settlements: it established the principle of **state sovereignty** — that rulers, not the Church, had authority over their territories — and implicitly recognized that Europe would remain permanently divided between Catholic and Protestant states. This was both a religious settlement and the founding document of the modern international state system.

## The Scientific Revolution

The Scientific Revolution (c. 1543–1700) is one of AMSCO 3.3's explicitly required topics and one of the most frequently overlooked. Beginning with Copernicus's heliocentric model (*De Revolutionibus*, 1543), a series of European thinkers — Galileo, Kepler, Bacon, Descartes, and above all **Newton** — developed a new way of understanding the natural world based on observation, mathematics, and experimentation rather than scriptural authority or Aristotelian tradition. Galileo's conflict with the Catholic Church over heliocentrism illustrates the direct collision between the new empirical method and religious authority. Francis Bacon's inductive method and Descartes' rationalism provided philosophical foundations for empirical science. Newton's *Principia Mathematica* (1687) synthesized this tradition into a mathematical model of the universe. The Scientific Revolution's long-term significance for AP World History is its contribution to the **Enlightenment** (Unit 5) and its role in Europe's eventual technological and military advantages — it is a cause of later European global dominance.

## Big Picture

Unit 3.3 connects Unit 3's land-based empires to Unit 4's transoceanic connections by showing that **religion moved along the same routes as trade and conquest**. The Jesuits followed European ships. Sufi missionaries followed Indian Ocean trading networks. Islam spread into West Africa along trans-Saharan caravan routes. No religious tradition in this period remained isolated — all were transformed through contact, competition, and synthesis with other traditions.
