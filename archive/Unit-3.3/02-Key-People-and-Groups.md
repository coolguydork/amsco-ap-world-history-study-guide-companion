# Unit 3.3: Key People and Groups

#apwh #unit-3 #key-people

## Protestant Reformation Figures

### Martin Luther — 1483–1546 | Holy Roman Empire (Germany)
- Augustinian monk and theology professor whose 95 Theses (1517) attacked the sale of indulgences and broader corruption in the Catholic Church
- Argued that salvation comes through faith alone (sola fide), not through the purchase of indulgences or good works authorized by the church — a direct challenge to clerical authority
- Translated the Bible into German, making it accessible to ordinary literate people; the printing press spread his ideas across Europe within weeks — a landmark example of technology enabling religious revolution

> [!exam-tip]
> Luther is the single most important figure in the Protestant Reformation for AP purposes. Know: what he argued (faith alone, scripture alone), why it spread (printing press), and its political consequences (princes using Lutheranism to challenge Holy Roman Emperor authority).

### John Calvin — 1509–1564 | Geneva / France
- French theologian who developed a systematic Protestant theology emphasizing predestination: God has already determined who will be saved (the "elect") and who will be damned
- Turned Geneva into a model Reformed Protestant city-state with strict moral governance
- Calvinist churches spread to France (Huguenots), the Netherlands (Dutch Reformed), Scotland (Presbyterian), and New England Puritans — making Calvinism more internationally influential than Lutheranism

### Henry VIII — 1491–1547 | England
- King of England who broke with the Catholic Church in 1534 primarily to obtain an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon; Parliament passed the Act of Supremacy making him head of the Church of England
- His break was politically rather than theologically motivated — he was not a Protestant reformer by conviction
- The Anglican Church he created became a distinct tradition that influenced the British Empire's religious character globally

## Catholic Counter-Reformation Figures

### Ignatius of Loyola — 1491–1556 | Spain/Rome
- Spanish soldier turned spiritual mystic; founded the Society of Jesus (Jesuits) in 1540 with a military-style organizational structure and emphasis on obedience to the Pope
- The Jesuits became the Catholic Church's most important missionary order, operating in Asia, Africa, and the Americas
- His Spiritual Exercises became one of the most influential works of Catholic devotional practice

### Matteo Ricci — 1552–1610 | China
- Italian Jesuit priest who reached China in 1582 and adopted Chinese dress, language, and intellectual culture to make Christianity more appealing to Chinese elites
- Presented himself as a "Western Confucian" — arguing that Christianity was compatible with Confucian ethics; introduced European mathematics and astronomy to the Chinese court
- His method of cultural accommodation ("inculturation") was eventually condemned by the Pope in the Chinese Rites Controversy — illustrating the tension between missionary pragmatism and theological orthodoxy

### Francis Xavier — 1506–1552 | India, Japan, East Asia
- Co-founder of the Jesuits; the first major Catholic missionary to Asia; converted thousands in Goa (India), Malacca, and Japan before dying on the Chinese coast
- His work in Japan introduced Christianity to an estimated 300,000 Japanese converts before the Tokugawa shogunate suppressed Christianity (1597 onward) and expelled missionaries
- Demonstrates both the reach and the limits of Catholic missionary expansion in Asia

## Islamic Reform and Sufi Figures

### Guru Nanak — 1469–1539 | Punjab (Indian subcontinent)
- First Guru of Sikhism; born into a Hindu family in the Punjab amid Hindu-Muslim encounter (the Mughal Empire did not begin until 1526, after his birth); rejected the caste system and the formal rituals of both Hinduism and Islam
- Preached that there is one God, accessible to all people regardless of caste or religion; "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim"
- His movement eventually developed into the Sikh Khalsa (community), a distinct religious and later martial identity — a direct product of Hindu-Muslim cultural encounter in the Mughal Empire

### Rumi (Jalal al-Din Rumi) — 1207–1273 | (Persia/Anatolia)
- (Slightly predates this chapter, but his influence peaks in this period) Persian Sufi poet and mystic; his followers, especially his son Sultan Walad, organized the Mevlevi order ("Whirling Dervishes") in Konya (Anatolia) after Rumi's death
- His Masnavi is one of the great works of Persian literature; his poetry emphasizes love, longing, and the mystical union with the divine
- The Mevlevi order was patronized by the Ottoman sultans; Rumi's influence illustrates how Sufi mysticism softened and enriched orthodox Islamic practice

### Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab — 1703–1792 | Arabia
- (Late in Period 3) Arabian Islamic scholar who advocated a strict return to the Quran and early Islamic practice, rejecting Sufism, Shia practices, and what he saw as innovations (bidah)
- Allied with tribal ruler Muhammad ibn Saud in 1744; their partnership laid the foundation for the modern Saudi state
- Wahhabism's later global influence makes this an important bridge to modern world history (Units 7–9)

## Groups and Communities

### Huguenots (French Protestants)
- French Calvinist Protestants who gained significant political and military power in France by the mid-16th century, triggering the French Wars of Religion
- The St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre (1572) saw thousands of Huguenots killed on the orders of the Catholic monarchy
- The Edict of Nantes (1598) granted them limited tolerance, revoked by Louis XIV in 1685 — causing a Huguenot exodus to Protestant countries that transferred skilled artisans and merchants

### Japanese Christian Community (Kakure Kirishitan)
- Japanese converts to Christianity, numbering perhaps 300,000 by the late 1500s; after the Tokugawa banned Christianity (1597–1650), some communities practiced secretly for generations ("Hidden Christians")
- Their survival and eventual re-emergence in the 19th century illustrates how religious identity can persist underground under persecution
- Contrast with China: Christianity never reached mass popular levels in China, partly because the Chinese Rites Controversy ended Jesuit accommodation strategies

### Sikh Khalsa
- The community of initiated Sikhs formally established by Guru Gobind Singh (the 10th and last Guru) in 1699; members were given new names, a code of conduct, and the Five Ks (uncut hair, iron bracelet, wooden comb, cotton undergarment/breeches, sword)
- The Khalsa transformed Sikhism into not just a spiritual but a martial community, largely in response to Mughal (especially Aurangzeb's) persecution of Sikh Gurus
- Illustrates how imperial religious policy (Aurangzeb's orthodoxy) shaped the development of a religious tradition
