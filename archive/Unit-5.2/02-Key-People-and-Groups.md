# Key People and Groups — Unit 5.2: Nationalism and Revolutions

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## Toussaint Louverture (1743–1803)
**Region:** Saint-Domingue (Haiti) | **Period:** 1790s–1800s

- Formerly enslaved, self-educated man who became the principal military and political leader of the Haitian Revolution.
- Organized enslaved workers into a disciplined army that defeated French, Spanish, and British forces; explicitly invoked Enlightenment natural rights language to justify freedom.
- Captured by Napoleon through treachery in 1802, died in a French prison; became a martyr for the Haitian independence movement.

> [!exam-tip]
> Toussaint Louverture is a perennial AP stimulus subject. Know his connection to Enlightenment ideas, why Napoleon feared him, and why his revolution terrified slaveholders across the Americas.

---

## Simón Bolívar (1783–1830)
**Region:** Venezuela/South America | **Period:** 1810s–1820s

- Creole military leader who led independence movements in Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia.
- Invoked Enlightenment ideals while also acknowledging in the *Jamaica Letter* (1815) that Latin America's social complexity made direct democracy impractical.
- His failure to unite Latin America into a single republic after independence illustrates the limits of nationalist idealism against regional economic and political interests.

---

## José de San Martín (1778–1850)
**Region:** Argentina/South America | **Period:** 1810s–1820s

- Led the liberation of Argentina, Chile, and Peru; coordinated with Bolívar to drive Spain out of South America.
- Unlike Bolívar, San Martín did not seek personal political power after military victory — he resigned leadership and returned to Europe.
- His career illustrates the role of military organization and strategic thinking (not just ideology) in revolutionary success.

---

## Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–1821)
**Region:** France/Europe | **Period:** 1799–1815

- Rose from the French Revolution to become Emperor; spread Enlightenment-derived legal codes (the Napoleonic Code) across occupied Europe while suppressing democratic institutions.
- Sent an army to reconquer Haiti (1802), which galvanized Haitian resistance and accelerated independence.
- His conquests inadvertently spread **nationalist ideology** — by occupying Spain, he triggered Latin American independence movements; by humiliating German princes, he sparked German nationalism.

---

## Maximilien Robespierre (1758–1794)
**Region:** France | **Period:** 1793–1794

- Leader of the Committee of Public Safety during the **Reign of Terror**; executed thousands in the name of revolutionary virtue.
- His career illustrates Rousseau's "general will" taken to totalitarian extremes — the claim that terror in the name of the people is justified.
- Executed in the Thermidorian Reaction (1794); his fate became a warning about revolutionary radicalism and mob rule.

---

## The Enslaved People of Saint-Domingue
**Region:** Caribbean | **Period:** 1791–1804

- The most radical revolutionary actors of the era: enslaved Africans (about 500,000 in Saint-Domingue) who organized a mass uprising beginning in 1791.
- Fought simultaneously against French colonists, Spanish invaders, and British occupiers — demonstrating extraordinary military capability and political determination.
- Their revolution was driven by both Enlightenment ideas (appropriated and applied to their own condition) and a prior tradition of African and African-derived resistance (maroon communities, vodou).

---

## Creole Elites of Latin America
**Region:** Latin America | **Period:** 1810s–1820s

- Spanish-born Americans (not peninsulares — those born in Spain) who resented being excluded from top colonial offices, paying high taxes, and being forced to trade exclusively with Spain.
- Led independence movements across the continent but generally sought to preserve the racial and social hierarchy beneath them — independence for creoles, not for Indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans, or mestizos.
- Their selective application of revolutionary ideals illustrates a recurring pattern: revolutions tend to benefit those with resources to organize them.

---

## Edmund Burke (1729–1797)
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** Late 18th century

- British statesman who wrote *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), condemning the French Revolution as a reckless destruction of tradition and social order.
- Founded modern **conservatism**: the argument that society is an organic community inherited from the past, not a contract to be redesigned by rational abstraction.
- His work is significant for the AP exam as the intellectual counter-reaction to Enlightenment radicalism.

---

## Olympe de Gouges (1748–1793)
**Region:** France | **Period:** 1789–1793

- French playwright and political activist who wrote the *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Female Citizen* (1791), directly challenging the exclusion of women from the Rights of Man.
- Executed by guillotine (1793) during the Reign of Terror — illustrating the Revolution's violent suppression of feminist dissent.
- Her death is a powerful example of the contradictions of revolutionary universalism.
