# Unit 2.4: Key People and Groups — Trans-Saharan Trade Routes (c. 1200–1450)

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently uses Mansa Musa as a primary source stimulus — his pilgrimage is documented in Islamic sources and is excellent evidence of both trans-Saharan trade wealth and West African Muslim identity. Know the details of his 1324–1325 hajj and what it reveals about Mali's place in the global economy.

---

## Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337)
**Time Period:** Early 14th century
**Region:** Mali Empire (West Africa) → North Africa → Mecca

- The most powerful ruler of the Mali Empire, controlling the gold-producing regions of Bambuk and Bure and the trans-Saharan routes that brought that gold to North Africa and the Mediterranean Islamic world
- His pilgrimage (*hajj*) to Mecca in 1324–1325 became legendary: he traveled with an enormous entourage (estimates range from 8,000 to 60,000+ people, with 500+ servants and 80–100 camels carrying gold) and distributed so much gold in Cairo and along his route that he caused gold-market inflation in Egypt lasting over a decade
- His journey placed Mali on Islamic and European world maps — the 1375 Catalan Atlas depicts him on a golden throne, demonstrating how his hajj reshaped the Mediterranean world's understanding of African wealth; he also brought back an Andalusian architect who redesigned Timbuktu's mosque and royal palace

---

## Sundiata Keita (r. c. 1235–1255)
**Time Period:** Mid-13th century
**Region:** West Africa (founder of Mali Empire)

- The legendary founder of the Mali Empire, who overthrew the Sosso king Sumanguru Kante at the Battle of Kirina (c. 1235) and established Mali's control over the former Ghana Empire's gold trade routes
- His story is preserved in the *Epic of Sundiata*, an oral epic maintained by griots — one of Africa's greatest works of oral literature and a primary source for understanding both the historical events and the cultural values (honor, lineage, spiritual power) of Mande-speaking West Africa
- His state-building model — military conquest of trade route chokepoints, followed by taxation of commerce rather than direct production — became the template for West African empire-building through Songhai

---

## Griots (Jelis)
**Time Period:** c. 1200–1450 and ongoing
**Region:** West Africa (Mali Empire and successor states)

- Hereditary oral historians, musicians, praise-singers, and genealogists who served as the living memory of West African courts and communities, preserving political histories, dynastic legitimacy claims, and cultural values through performance
- Their role was essential to governance: kings relied on griots to recite their lineage to establish legitimacy; griots mediated disputes, announced royal decisions, and served as diplomats
- The griot caste occupied a socially ambiguous position — simultaneously indispensable and somewhat set apart (in some traditions, griots could not be buried in the same earth as others due to their special spiritual status)

---

## Berber and Tuareg Caravan Leaders
**Time Period:** c. 1200–1450 and beyond
**Region:** Sahara Desert and northern Sahel

- Berber-speaking and Tuareg peoples of the Sahara organized, guided, and protected the caravans that crossed the desert, providing essential knowledge of water sources (*wadis* and oases), safe routes, and timing
- Tuareg clans controlled key desert passages and often levied tolls on caravans — making them commercially indispensable and politically powerful in their own right, neither fully subject to Mali's authority nor fully independent of its economic orbit
- Their role illustrates that the trans-Saharan network was not simply a two-point exchange (West Africa to North Africa) but a layered system involving multiple intermediary communities with their own interests and power

---

## Diula (Dyula) Merchants
**Time Period:** c. 1200–1450 and beyond
**Region:** West Africa — Sahel and forest zone

- Muslim West African merchants who traveled between the Sahel (where trans-Saharan routes terminated) and the forest zone (where gold was produced and kola nuts were grown), serving as the commercial link between interior producers and Saharan traders
- Carried Islam with them as they traded, building mosques and Islamic schools in market towns throughout West Africa — a primary mechanism by which Islam spread beyond the immediate trans-Saharan corridor
- Their commercial networks were based on kinship and shared Islamic identity, creating trust relationships that enabled long-distance credit and deferred payment across vast distances

---

## North African and Arab Merchants
**Time Period:** c. 1200–1450
**Region:** Maghreb, Egypt, the Arabian Peninsula → trans-Saharan routes

- Merchants from the Islamic heartland (Maghreb, Egypt, Arabia) were the northern counterparts of West African traders, supplying manufactured goods — copper, textiles, glassware, horses, and Islamic manuscripts — in exchange for gold and enslaved people moving northward
- Arab and Berber merchant families often established permanent agents in Sahelian cities like Timbuktu and Jenne, creating the diasporic commercial communities that turned these cities into cosmopolitan Islamic centers
- Their involvement ensured that trans-Saharan trade was deeply integrated into the wider Islamic commercial world, not an isolated regional exchange

---

## Enslaved Peoples in the Trans-Saharan Trade
**Time Period:** c. 1200–1450
**Region:** Sub-Saharan Africa → Sahara → North Africa, the Mediterranean, Arabia

- Enslaved people were a significant commodity in trans-Saharan trade, moving northward alongside gold; they were captured in raids on stateless societies south and east of Mali and sold into domestic service, military service (slave soldiers were common in North African armies), and craft production across the Islamic world
- The trans-Saharan slave trade long predated the Atlantic slave trade; it was smaller in total volume but operated for centuries longer, shaping social structures across the Sahara and Sahel
- This dimension of trans-Saharan commerce is important for AP World History's emphasis on labor systems: coerced labor moved through the same routes as gold, demonstrating that trade networks carried human suffering alongside commercial wealth

> [!warning]
> Do not conflate the **trans-Saharan slave trade** with the **Atlantic slave trade** (Unit 4). The trans-Saharan trade was older, smaller in scale, predominantly moved enslaved men into military/administrative roles, and was not racially systematized in the same way as the Atlantic trade. They are related but historically distinct phenomena.
