# Unit 2.4: Key Terms — Trans-Saharan Trade Routes (c. 1200–1450)

> [!exam-tip]
> AMSCO 2.4 is exclusively about **Trans-Saharan trade and West African empire**. The AP exam tests this through stimulus questions involving West African gold, Mansa Musa's hajj, and the spread of Islam. Know the gold-salt exchange, the role of the Mali Empire, and how trade built political power in the Sahel.

---

## The Network

**Trans-Saharan Trade Routes** — The overland exchange system crossing the Sahara Desert, connecting the gold-producing savannas of West Africa (the Sahel) to the markets of North Africa, the Maghreb, and the Mediterranean Islamic world. (Significance: the economic foundation of West African empire-building from Ghana through Mali to Songhai; transmitted Islam southward along the same routes that gold moved northward; one of the three major long-distance trade networks of the period alongside the Silk Roads and Indian Ocean.)

**Sahel** — The semi-arid grassland belt south of the Sahara Desert and north of the tropical forest zone, home to the major West African empires. (Significance: the Sahel's position between the Sahara and the forest zone made it ideal for controlling trade; the region where caravans transitioned from desert travel to savanna roads; home to cities like Timbuktu, Jenne, and Koumbi Saleh.)

**Camel Caravans** — The primary transport technology of Trans-Saharan commerce, using the **dromedary camel** (one-humped, adapted to hot desert conditions) rather than the Bactrian camel of the Central Asian Silk Roads. (Significance: the dromedary can carry heavy loads 25–30 miles per day without water, making regular Saharan crossings possible; without camel transport, consistent long-distance trade across the Sahara would be impossible; introduced from Arabia into North Africa in the early centuries CE.)

**Gold-Salt Exchange** — The foundational economic relationship of the Trans-Saharan trade: West African gold (from the Bambuk and Bure goldfields) was exchanged for Saharan rock salt (from mines at Taghaza and Timbuktu), both of which were scarce and precious in the other's home region. (Significance: West African rulers monopolized gold access and taxed its export; salt was as economically vital as gold for preserving food and physiological health; the asymmetry of these resources drove a centuries-long commercial relationship.)

**Taghaza** — A salt-mining settlement deep in the Sahara, operated primarily by enslaved workers who mined large slabs of rock salt for export southward to West African markets. (Significance: the primary source of the salt that West Africans received in exchange for gold; its isolation and dangerous conditions illustrate the brutal human cost of sustaining trans-Saharan commerce; control of Taghaza was strategically significant.)

**Sijilmasa** — A major trading city at the northern terminus of the trans-Saharan routes in present-day Morocco; the point where Saharan caravans connected to Mediterranean and Islamic markets. (Significance: the commercial gateway through which West African gold entered North Africa and the wider Islamic world; Berber and Arab merchants organized caravans from Sijilmasa southward.)

## West African Empires

**Ghana Empire** — The earliest of the major West African empires (c. 300–1200 CE), controlling the upper Niger River and the southern terminals of trans-Saharan gold routes; fell to the Almoravid movement in the 11th century. (Significance: the model on which Mali and later Songhai were built; demonstrated that controlling the interface between gold-producing regions and trans-Saharan routes was the foundation of West African imperial power; predecessor state to Mali.)

**Mali Empire** — The dominant West African empire from the 13th to 15th centuries, founded by Sundiata Keita (c. 1235) after defeating the Sosso; controlled the gold-producing regions of Bambuk and Bure and the major Saharan trade routes. (Significance: at its height one of the wealthiest states in the world; patron of Islamic scholarship in Timbuktu; Mansa Musa's hajj put Mali on global maps; successor to Ghana and precursor to Songhai.)

**Mansa Musa (r. c. 1312–1337)** — The most powerful ruler of the Mali Empire, whose pilgrimage (*hajj*) to Mecca in 1324–1325 with thousands of attendants and enormous quantities of gold became legendary. (Significance: his distribution of gold in Egypt and Arabia caused inflation in regional gold markets for over a decade — concrete evidence of Mali's economic weight; his patronage of Islamic architects and scholars transformed Mali's urban culture; his pilgrimage was recorded on the 1375 Catalan Atlas, one of Europe's most famous medieval maps.)

**Sundiata Keita (r. c. 1235–1255)** — The legendary founder of the Mali Empire who united Mande-speaking peoples, defeated the Sosso king Sumanguru Kante, and established the political framework the empire would use for two centuries. (Significance: subject of the *Epic of Sundiata*, the foundational oral narrative of Mali preserved by griots; illustrates how West African rulers combined military power, lineage legitimacy, and spiritual authority — both Islamic and indigenous — to build empire.)

**Timbuktu** — A city in the Mali Empire, located at the junction of trans-Saharan caravan routes and the Niger River bend, that became the Islamic world's preeminent center of scholarship in sub-Saharan Africa. (Significance: home to the Sankore Mosque-University and other institutions; private and institutional libraries accumulated extensive manuscript collections; demonstrates that trans-Saharan trade transmitted scholarship and Islamic intellectual culture, not just material goods.)

**Songhai Empire** — The West African empire that succeeded Mali as the dominant power in the Sahel from the late 15th century, eventually controlling an even larger territory; centered on the city of Gao on the Niger River. (Significance: under Sunni Ali (r. 1464–1492) and Askia Muhammad (r. 1493–1528), Songhai became the largest empire in West African history; though just beyond the 1200–1450 scope, it provides the forward context for where Mali's trade networks led.)

## People and Cultural Life

**Berber and Tuareg Traders** — North African and Saharan peoples who organized, guided, and participated in trans-Saharan caravans; the Tuareg in particular were indispensable desert guides and caravan leaders. (Significance: without Berber and Tuareg expertise in desert navigation, water sources, and caravan logistics, the trans-Saharan network could not have functioned; they formed a distinct commercial and cultural layer between North African markets and West African producers.)

**Diula (Dyula) Merchants** — West African Muslim merchants who traveled throughout the Sahel and forest zones, connecting trans-Saharan routes to interior markets and spreading Islam alongside commerce. (Significance: a key mechanism by which Islam spread beyond the immediate trans-Saharan corridor into the forest zone and across West Africa more broadly; their trade networks extended the reach of the gold-salt exchange into regions caravans could not directly access.)

**Griots** — West African oral historians, musicians, and keepers of cultural memory who preserved and transmitted political history, genealogies, and epic traditions through performance rather than written texts. (Significance: primary source for Mali's history, including the *Epic of Sundiata*; demonstrates that African societies maintained sophisticated historical consciousness without writing; griots held a unique, hereditary, and somewhat ambiguous social position — honored for their knowledge, yet set apart from other social categories.)

**Islam in West Africa** — Islam was introduced to West African elites and merchants via trans-Saharan trade contacts beginning in the 8th century; by 1200 it was the religion of the ruling class and urban merchants across the Sahel, while rural populations often maintained indigenous practices. (Significance: West African "elite Islam" — where rulers converted for commercial and diplomatic advantages while their subjects retained traditional beliefs — is a key AP pattern; Mansa Musa's hajj represents its fullest expression; demonstrates that Islam spread primarily through trade in this region, not conquest.)

> [!exam-tip]
> A common AP trap: do not confuse the **Trans-Saharan** network (this topic) with the **Indian Ocean** network (Unit 2.3). They served different regions and different goods, though they were linked through East African gold reaching the Indian Ocean via the Swahili Coast. West Africa = trans-Saharan; East Africa = Indian Ocean. Mali and the Swahili Coast are different ends of a connected but distinct system.
