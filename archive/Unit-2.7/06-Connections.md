## Comparative Pairings

- **Silk Roads vs. Indian Ocean — political vs. commercial self-organization:** The Silk Roads required a dominant empire (Mongol) to function; the Indian Ocean network was sustained by Islamic commercial law and merchant trust. When the Mongol Empire fragmented, Silk Road commerce collapsed; when no single power controlled the Indian Ocean, trade continued. This difference in political dependency is one of the most important comparative arguments in Unit 2.

- **Indian Ocean vs. Trans-Saharan — two vehicles of Islamic spread:** Both networks transmitted Islam into new regions (East/Southeast Africa and West Africa respectively), but through somewhat different mechanisms: Indian Ocean Islam spread through merchant example and commercial integration; Trans-Saharan Islam spread through merchant activity combined with Sufi scholarly prestige and elite conversion. The results differed too: East African coastal Islam was syncretic and urban-focused; West African Islam was court-centered and gradually penetrated rural society more slowly.

- **All three vs. the Columbian Exchange (Unit 4.6):** The period's trade networks moved goods and culture deliberately; the Columbian Exchange after 1492 moved pathogens accidentally and catastrophically. Compare the Black Death (plague via Silk Roads, ~30–50% European mortality) with smallpox in the Americas (via European contact, ~50–90% Indigenous mortality) — same mechanism, different scale, different historical context.

- **Merchant status across networks:** Islamic world (Indian Ocean, Trans-Saharan) → merchants are honored; Confucian China (Silk Roads endpoint) → merchants are stigmatized below farmers. This ideological difference shaped which civilizations organized sustained commercial networks and which relied on state tribute systems — with lasting consequences for who dominated global trade in the early modern period.
