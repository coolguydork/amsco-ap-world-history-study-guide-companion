# Unit 2.7: Themes and Causation — Comparison of Economic Exchange

#apwh #unit-2 #period-1

## Governance

All three networks had different relationships with state power. The Silk Roads were heavily state-dependent: without Mongol political authority and the Pax Mongolica, the overland routes were too dangerous for sustained commerce, and when the Mongol Empire fragmented after 1350, Silk Road trade declined sharply. The Indian Ocean network was the opposite — decentralized and merchant-driven, operating through Islamic commercial law and diasporic community trust rather than state protection. The Trans-Saharan network fell between these extremes: West African states like Mali taxed and regulated trade without monopolizing it, using gold revenue to fund state power while leaving the actual desert crossing to specialized Berber traders. These differences mean that state collapse had very different effects: the Mongol collapse devastated Silk Road commerce, barely affected Indian Ocean commerce, and only moderately disrupted Trans-Saharan trade.

> [!exam-tip]
> "State involvement" is a recurring AP comparison axis. Practice articulating the contrast: Silk Roads = **politically dependent**; Indian Ocean = **commercially self-organizing**; Trans-Saharan = **state-taxed but merchant-operated**.

## Economic Systems

The goods traded on each network reflect the comparative advantages of the regions they connected. The Silk Roads carried the highest-prestige luxury goods of the Eurasian world — Chinese silk and porcelain, Central Asian horses, Persian metalwork, Indian spices — because these were the goods that justified the enormous overland transport costs. The Indian Ocean carried a broader range: spices from the Spice Islands commanded premium prices, but Indian cotton textiles, East African gold and ivory, and Southeast Asian tropical products moved in large volume; the maritime medium made bulk goods more economically viable than overland routes. The Trans-Saharan network was built on two essential commodities — West African gold (which financed Mediterranean economies) and Saharan salt (which was essential for food preservation in the Sahel) — with enslaved people as a significant third category. All three networks operated primarily through credit and trust instruments rather than coin, reflecting the impracticality of moving heavy metal across vast distances.

## Cultural Developments

Islam was the most powerful cultural consequence of all three networks, but it spread through different mechanisms on each. On the Indian Ocean, Islam spread primarily through the example and social integration of Arab and Persian Muslim merchants — conversion was often voluntary and driven by commercial advantage, as Islamic commercial law made it easier to do business with the Muslim-dominated network. On the Trans-Saharan, Sufi missionaries and the prestige of the Islamic scholarly tradition attracted West African rulers, while the commercial advantages of literacy (Arabic) and legal frameworks reinforced conversion among elites. On the Silk Roads, Islam spread through Central Asian populations who were incorporated into the Mongol Empire; Buddhism also spread eastward through these routes. The Indian Ocean network was the most religiously diverse, carrying Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism simultaneously, with conversion dependent on local political decisions rather than merchant pressure alone.

## Social Structures

The social status of merchants differed dramatically across these networks, with significant consequences for how trade was organized. In the Islamic world — which dominated both the Indian Ocean and Trans-Saharan networks — the Prophet Muhammad had been a merchant, and trade was explicitly honored in Islamic tradition; merchant communities were prestigious and well-integrated into urban social structures. In Confucian China, by contrast, merchants occupied the lowest rung of the four-tiered social hierarchy (scholar-farmer-artisan-merchant), which created persistent tension between China's commercial dynamism and official ideology. This difference partly explains why the Chinese state could shut down Zheng He's voyages through bureaucratic disapproval — no equivalent ideological brake existed in the Islamic commercial world. On all three networks, women participated minimally in long-distance trade itself but were central to local market economies and often managed household commercial operations while husbands traveled.

## Technology and Environment

The physical environments of each network determined what technologies were essential. The Silk Roads required camel technology (the Bactrian two-humped camel for Central Asian steppes) and caravanserai infrastructure; the Trans-Saharan required the dromedary (one-humped camel), whose fat-storage and water-conservation adaptations are uniquely suited to Saharan conditions. The Indian Ocean required maritime technology — the lateen-sail dhow optimized for monsoon conditions, navigational instruments (astrolabe, compass), and knowledge of monsoon wind patterns. Across all three networks, paper and paper money (originating in China and diffusing westward) reduced the weight burden of commercial documentation. The compass, also Chinese in origin, eventually transformed the maritime network's reach.

## Human Migration

All three networks produced diasporic communities — merchants who settled permanently in foreign ports or oasis cities to maintain commercial relationships. Indian Ocean merchant diasporas (Arab, Indian, Chinese, Swahili) were the most extensive and long-lived, creating hybrid cultures throughout the coastal world: Swahili culture is itself a product of Bantu-Arab-Persian synthesis. Silk Road diasporas included Sogdian Central Asian merchants who established communities throughout East Asia before the Mongol period, and later Chinese and Muslim merchant communities in Yuan Dynasty China. Trans-Saharan migration patterns were more constrained by the physical barrier of the Sahara, but Berber and Tuareg communities maintained semi-permanent presences at key Saharan oasis towns. In all cases, these diasporic communities were the human infrastructure that made long-distance trade possible.
