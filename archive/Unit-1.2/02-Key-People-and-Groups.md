# Unit 1.2: Key People and Groups — Dar al-Islam (1200–1450)

## Individuals and Groups

### Abbasid Caliphate
**Period:** 750–1258 | **Region:** Baghdad / Middle East

- At its height, the Abbasid court in Baghdad was the intellectual center of the world, sponsoring translations of Greek, Persian, and Indian texts in the House of Wisdom
- By 1200, the Abbasid caliph was largely a symbolic religious figurehead while real power was held by Turkish and Persian military rulers (sultans)
- The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 — in which the last caliph was executed — ended the caliphate and traumatized the Islamic world; it is a key turning point in Islamic history

---

### Hulagu Khan
**Period:** 1217–1265 | **Region:** Middle East (Ilkhanate)

- Grandson of Genghis Khan; led the Mongol campaign that sacked Baghdad in 1258 and killed the last Abbasid caliph
- Founded the Ilkhanate in Persia; initially hostile to Islam, but later Ilkhanate rulers converted, demonstrating the pattern of Mongol cultural assimilation
- His invasion forced many Islamic scholars, artists, and merchants to flee westward, spreading Islamic culture (and the trauma of conquest) across the Middle East

---

### Mamluks
**Period:** 1250–1517 | **Region:** Egypt and Syria

- A dynasty of enslaved soldiers (originally Turkic and Caucasian) who overthrew their Ayyubid rulers and established their own sultanate in Egypt
- Defeated the Mongols at the **Battle of Ain Jalut** (1260) — the first major Mongol military defeat, halting westward Mongol expansion
- Became the protectors of the Abbasid caliphate in exile (in Cairo) and of the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, giving them enormous prestige in the Islamic world

> [!exam-tip]
> The Battle of Ain Jalut (1260) is highly significant — it is one of the AP exam's favorite "limits of Mongol expansion" examples. The Mamluks halted Mongol advance into Africa and preserved Islamic civilization in the Middle East.

---

### Ibn Battuta
**Period:** 1304–c.1368/1369 | **Region:** Morocco / Global Islamic World

- A Moroccan Muslim scholar who undertook one of history's greatest journeys, traveling over 75,000 miles across the Islamic world from Morocco to Mali, East Africa, the Middle East, Central Asia, India, Southeast Asia, and China
- His *Rihla* (Journey) describes Islamic societies across three continents, documenting trade, governance, religious practices, and social customs
- His travels demonstrate the extraordinary connectivity of the 14th-century Islamic world and the degree to which a Muslim traveler could find familiar legal, religious, and commercial institutions from Mali to China

---

### Mansa Musa
**Period:** 1312–1337 | **Region:** Mali Empire (West Africa)

- Emperor of the Mali Empire whose 1324–1325 hajj to Mecca became legendary; his caravan allegedly included 60,000 people and so much gold that he destabilized Egyptian gold prices for a decade
- His pilgrimage introduced Mali's extraordinary wealth to the Islamic world and Mediterranean, and returned Mali with architects, scholars, and cultural influences that transformed the empire
- Demonstrates how the hajj functioned as a global exchange of ideas, goods, and influence across the Dar al-Islam

> [!tip] See also [[Unit-1.4/02-Key-People-and-Groups#Mansa Musa]] for full details.

---

### Sufi Missionaries
**Period:** 1200–1450 | **Region:** Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Asia, Southeast Asia

- Sufi orders (tariqas) sent missionaries who adapted Islamic practice to local cultures — allowing pre-Islamic customs to blend with Islamic faith, making conversion more accessible
- Key to the spread of Islam to West Africa (via trans-Saharan routes), Southeast Asia (via Indian Ocean trade), and Central Asia (among Mongol and Turkic peoples)
- Sufi emphasis on personal devotion, music, and poetry (e.g., Rumi's poetry in Persia) created a rich spiritual literature that appealed across cultural boundaries

---

### Al-Ghazali
**Period:** 1058–1111 | **Region:** Persia / Islamic World

- Islamic theologian and philosopher who reconciled Sufi mysticism with orthodox Sunni practice in *The Revival of the Religious Sciences*
- His synthesis shaped Islamic intellectual life for centuries and helped legitimize Sufism within mainstream Islam
- Though he died before 1200, his influence on Islamic scholarship during this period is fundamental

---

### Omar Khayyam / Islamic Scientists
**Period:** c. 1000–1300 | **Region:** Persia, Baghdad, Andalusia

- Islamic scholars advanced mathematics (algebra, developed by al-Khwarizmi), astronomy, medicine (Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine*), and philosophy during the Abbasid golden age
- These scholars preserved Greek learning and made original contributions; their work was later translated into Latin and became the basis of the European university curriculum
- The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 caused significant disruption to this intellectual tradition; popular accounts describe books thrown into the Tigris River, but historians note that many scholars and manuscripts survived — the impact was severe but not a total erasure of Islamic learning

---

### Rashid al-Din
**Period:** 1247–1318 | **Region:** Ilkhanate Persia

- Jewish-born convert to Islam who served as vizier (prime minister) of the Mongol Ilkhanate
- Compiled the *Compendium of Chronicles* — a universal world history that blended Mongol, Persian, Chinese, and Islamic historical traditions
- Exemplifies the cosmopolitan cultural synthesis of the Ilkhanate court, where Mongol rulers patronized scholars from multiple traditions
