# Key Terms — Unit 5.4: Industrialization Spreads

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Meiji Restoration (1868)** — The political revolution in Japan that overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate and restored imperial rule under Emperor Meiji; immediately launched a state-directed program of rapid industrialization and westernization. (The most successful example of deliberate, top-down industrialization; Japan went from feudal isolation to defeating a European great power in 35 years.)

2. **Meiji government** — The new Japanese government formed after 1868, dominated by ambitious samurai reformers who used state power to build railroads, factories, banks, a navy, and Western-style educational and legal institutions. (Illustrates how strong state action can drive rapid industrialization when private capital is insufficient or too slow.)

3. **Selective borrowing (Japan)** — Japan's deliberate policy of adopting Western technology, military organization, and legal institutions while explicitly preserving Japanese culture, imperial system, and social hierarchy. (Captured by the slogan "*Wakon Yōsai*" — "Japanese spirit, Western technology"; this selective approach distinguished Japan from wholesale Westernization.)

4. **Railroad** — A system of tracks on which steam-powered locomotives haul goods and passengers; the defining infrastructure technology of the Second Industrial Revolution. (Railroads integrated national markets, enabled coal and raw material transport, drove demand for iron and steel, and extended industrial reach into non-industrial regions.)

5. **George Stephenson (1781–1848)** — British engineer who designed the first successful steam locomotive (*Rocket*, 1829) and the Liverpool-Manchester Railway (1830), inaugurating the railroad age. (His work transformed the steam engine from a stationary factory machine into a mobile transportation technology.)

6. **Protective tariff** — A tax on imported manufactured goods, designed to make foreign products more expensive and protect domestic industries from foreign competition. (Used by Germany, the USA, Japan, and Russia to shield infant industries while they developed; opposed by Britain, which had industrial advantages and preferred free trade.)

7. **Zollverein** — The German Customs Union (established 1834 under Prussian leadership), which eliminated internal tariffs among German states and created a single German market — an economic precursor to political unification. (Illustrates how economic integration can precede and enable political unification; also illustrates government's role in market creation.)

8. **Otto von Bismarck (1815–1898)** — Prussian statesman who unified Germany through wars with Austria and France (1866–1871) and then directed Germany's rapid industrial development through protective tariffs, state banking, and social welfare legislation. (His top-down industrial policy made Germany Europe's largest industrial economy by 1900 — an alternative model to British laissez-faire.)

9. **Second Industrial Revolution** — The phase of industrialization (c. 1870–1914) characterized by steel, chemicals, electricity, and the internal combustion engine; associated with Germany, USA, and Japan as much as Britain. (Qualitatively different from the first: more science-based, more capital-intensive, and more dependent on state and corporate organization.)

10. **Transcontinental railroad (USA, 1869)** — The first railroad linking the Atlantic and Pacific coasts of the United States, built by the Union Pacific and Central Pacific railroads using land grants from Congress and labor from Chinese immigrants and Civil War veterans. (Opened the American West to settlement and industrial exploitation; illustrates government's role in US industrialization through land grants and subsidies.)

11. **Immigration and industrialization (USA)** — Millions of European immigrants (Irish, Germans, Italians, Eastern Europeans, Scandinavians) provided cheap labor for American factories, mines, and railroad construction in the late 19th century. (By 1900, the US had surpassed Britain as the world's largest industrial economy; immigration was its primary labor supply mechanism.)

12. **Russian industrialization** — Russia began industrializing in the 1880s–1890s under Finance Minister Sergei Witte, building railroads (Trans-Siberian Railroad, 1891–1904) and attracting French investment; industry was concentrated in a few cities with a small but militant proletariat. (Russia's late, state-directed industrialization created exactly the social tensions Marx had predicted; it set the stage for the 1905 and 1917 revolutions.)

13. **Chinese failure to industrialize** — China did not industrialize in the 19th century despite being aware of Western technology; Qing imperial conservatism, Confucian disdain for commerce, the Self-Strengthening Movement's failure, and the disruption of the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864) all contributed. (Contrasting China's failure with Japan's success is a high-yield AP comparison.)

14. **Diffusion of technology** — The process by which industrial techniques spread from Britain to other regions through the movement of machines, engineers, capital, and published knowledge. (Britain initially tried to prevent diffusion through laws banning skilled worker emigration, but could not stop it; Samuel Slater smuggled textile machinery knowledge to the USA in 1793.)

> [!exam-tip]
> For the AP exam, be ready to explain **Japan vs. China** as responses to Western pressure: Japan's state-led industrialization succeeded; China's Self-Strengthening Movement failed. The key difference: Japan's new Meiji government was willing to transform political and social institutions; China's Qing government was not.
