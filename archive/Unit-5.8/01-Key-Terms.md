# Key Terms — Unit 5.8: Reactions to the Industrial Economy

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Labor union** — An organization of workers that collectively negotiates with employers over wages, hours, and working conditions; illegal in most countries until the mid-19th century. (The collective bargaining model proved more immediately effective than revolutionary movements for most workers; unions drove the major improvements in working conditions from the 1850s onward.)

2. **Marxism** — The political and economic theory developed by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels; argues that material conditions (especially economic relations of production) drive history, that capitalism is inherently exploitative, that class struggle between bourgeoisie and proletariat is history's engine, and that capitalism will inevitably collapse and be replaced by socialism then communism. (The most globally influential political ideology of the 20th century; directly inspired Russian, Chinese, Cuban, and Vietnamese revolutions.)

3. **Historical materialism** — Marx's theory that the economic base of society (the mode of production, relations between classes) determines its political and cultural "superstructure" (government, religion, ideology). (Fundamental to understanding Marxism; contrasts with idealist theories that argue ideas drive history; tested on AP when explaining why Marx would say that Enlightenment natural rights ideology serves bourgeois interests.)

4. **Class struggle** — Marx's concept that history is driven by conflict between economic classes; in industrial capitalism, between the **bourgeoisie** (owners of the means of production) and the **proletariat** (wage laborers who own nothing but their labor). (The concept makes industrialization's social conflict legible as a structural feature, not individual bad behavior.)

5. **Surplus value** — Marx's concept that workers produce more value than they are paid; the difference (surplus value) is appropriated by the capitalist as profit — the mechanism of exploitation. (The intellectual core of Marxist economics; contested by liberal economists who argue that profit represents the capitalist's contribution of risk and organization.)

6. **Socialism** — A broad family of political ideologies advocating collective or state ownership of the means of production and cooperative management of the economy; ranges from reform socialism to Marxist communism. (In the 19th century, socialism was the general term for opposition to capitalist private ownership; it encompassed both moderate reformers and revolutionary communists.)

7. **Utopian socialism** — Early socialist movements (Robert Owen, Charles Fourier, Henri de Saint-Simon) that advocated creating ideal cooperative communities as alternatives to capitalism, rather than seizing state power. (Called "utopian" by Marx, who criticized them for being impractical and ignoring class conflict; but Owen's cooperative factories and communities influenced later cooperative and labor movements.)

8. **Robert Owen (1771–1858)** — Welsh manufacturer and social reformer who created a model factory community at New Lanark, Scotland, demonstrating that workers could be treated humanely and still be profitable; later advocated cooperative communities. (Represents the reform-minded, non-revolutionary wing of early socialism; his experiments influenced British labor and cooperative movements.)

9. ***Communist Manifesto* (1848)** — Pamphlet by Marx and Engels arguing that capitalism creates class struggle, that the proletariat must overthrow the bourgeoisie and establish collective ownership, and ending with the famous call: "Workers of the world, unite!" (Published in 1848, the year of Europe-wide revolutions; became the founding document of revolutionary socialism.)

10. **Anarchism** — A political philosophy rejecting all forms of government authority and hierarchy, advocating immediate revolution to create a society of free individuals and voluntary cooperation. (Associated with Mikhail Bakunin (Russia) and Peter Kropotkin; competed with Marxism within the International Workingmen's Association [First International]; responsible for several political assassinations of heads of state in the 1880s–1900s.)

11. **First International (International Workingmen's Association, 1864–1876)** — An international organization of socialist, anarchist, and labor groups founded by Marx; dissolved due to conflict between Marxists and anarchists. (Illustrates the internal divisions within the 19th-century left; also illustrates the first attempt to organize labor internationally across national borders.)

12. **Second International (1889–1916)** — A successor organization of socialist parties founded after the First International's collapse; included major socialist parties from Germany (SPD), France, Britain, and Russia. (Demonstrated the growing strength of socialist parties; collapsed in 1914 when most socialist parties supported their national governments' entry into World War I rather than international solidarity.)

13. **Reformism / Revisionism** — The strategy of working within existing political systems to achieve gradual improvements in workers' conditions, rather than pursuing revolutionary overthrow of capitalism. (Eduard Bernstein's "revisionism" argued that Marxist revolution was unnecessary — workers could achieve socialism gradually through democratic politics; opposed by revolutionary Marxists.)

14. **Trade union / Craft union** — A union of skilled workers in a particular trade (e.g., printers, carpenters, miners); distinguished from industrial unions (which organize all workers in an industry regardless of skill). (Craft unions were historically the stronger form because skilled workers had leverage; industrial unions were more radical but harder to organize in an era of abundant unskilled immigrant labor.)

15. **Social Gospel** — A Protestant Christian reform movement in the USA and Britain that argued Christianity required addressing poverty, inequality, and social injustice; provided moral foundation for reform legislation. (Reflects the religious dimension of reactions to industrialization; distinct from but often aligned with socialist politics.)

> [!exam-tip]
> On AP stimulus questions involving Marx or socialist texts, always identify: (1) What specific industrial condition is being responded to? (2) What is the proposed solution — reform or revolution? (3) What class is the author addressing? Understanding the *audience* and *context* of socialist texts is essential for sourcing points on DBQs.
