# Unit 4.5: Maritime Empires Develop

#apwh #unit-4 #period-2

## AP Exam Period
**Period 2: 1450–1750**

## Summary

After the initial establishment of maritime empires in the late 15th and early 16th centuries, European powers faced the challenge of sustaining and expanding their control over vast territories and trade networks. The Spanish empire deepened its reliance on silver extraction and the plantation complex; the Portuguese faced competition from the Dutch and English in the Indian Ocean and Atlantic. The 17th century saw the rise of new imperial powers — the Dutch Republic and England — deploying joint-stock companies ([[VOC]], British East India Company) as instruments of commercial empire. The Atlantic slave trade reached its height in this period, transporting millions of enslaved Africans to fuel sugar and tobacco plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil. Silver from the Americas flowed through Spain to China, linking global economies. The period also saw intensifying competition between European powers, with the Dutch displacing Portugal in Southeast Asia and England challenging Spain in the Caribbean.

## Belief Systems: Christianity, Syncretism, and Missionary Activity

AMSCO 4.5 explicitly includes the **effects of maritime empire expansion on belief systems** — a subsection that is easy to overlook because economic topics dominate. As maritime empires developed, Christianity spread along the same routes as trade and conquest. Catholic missionary orders — **Jesuits**, Franciscans, Dominicans, and Augustinians — followed Spanish and Portuguese fleets, establishing churches and schools across the Americas, Africa, and Asia. Conversion was rarely voluntary: in the Americas, the destruction of indigenous temples, forced baptisms, and the replacement of indigenous festivals with Catholic ones were systematic. In some regions (especially the Philippines and Latin America), the result was mass nominal conversion; in others (Japan after 1597, China after the Rites Controversy), Christianity was actively suppressed.

The most significant religious outcome of maritime empire expansion was **syncretism** — the blending of Catholic Christianity with indigenous or African spiritual traditions. In Mexico, the **Virgin of Guadalupe** fused Catholic Marian devotion with the Aztec goddess Tonantzin; in the Caribbean and Brazil, **Candomblé** and **Vodou** blended West African orishas with Catholic saints under conditions of enslavement; in the Philippines, Catholic festivals incorporated indigenous ritual elements. These syncretic traditions were not simply confusion or deception — they represented active negotiation by colonized peoples to preserve spiritual meaning within imposed religious frameworks. The Catholic Church tolerated varying degrees of syncretism, often pragmatically, because outright suppression could trigger resistance.

The **Protestant Reformation** also had imperial consequences: Protestant powers (England, the Netherlands) competed with Catholic Spain and Portugal partly on religious grounds, and Protestant missionaries (later than Catholic ones) eventually produced their own conversion movements in North America and elsewhere.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain how joint-stock companies enabled the maintenance and expansion of maritime empires.
- Analyze the development and consequences of the Atlantic slave trade.
- Describe the global silver trade and its economic consequences.
- Explain how maritime empire expansion spread Christianity and produced religious syncretism across the Americas, Africa, and Asia.
- Evaluate the role of missionary activity and forced/voluntary conversion in shaping colonial societies.
