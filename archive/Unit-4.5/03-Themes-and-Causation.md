# Themes and Causation — Unit 4.5: (4.5): Maritime Empires Develop

#apwh #unit-4 #period-2

---

## Economic Systems

The global silver trade was the circulatory system of the 17th-century world economy. Silver mined at Potosí by coerced Indigenous labor traveled to Seville, funded Spain's European wars, and then flowed onward to China — where the Ming Dynasty required silver for tax payments. This made the Americas, Spain, and China nodes in a single global economic circuit. Simultaneously, the plantation complex created an Atlantic economy based on enslaved labor producing sugar, tobacco, and later cotton for European markets. The joint-stock company model (VOC, EIC) represented a financial innovation that spread risk and enabled large-scale, sustained commercial ventures. Together, these systems created the first genuinely global economy, though one built on profound inequality and coercion.

---

## Governance

Maintaining empires over long distances required constant administrative innovation. The Spanish empire struggled with the growing power of creole elites who resented metropolitan control, and with the corruption of distant governors beyond effective oversight. The VOC and EIC operated as quasi-states — they had armies, navies, and diplomatic services — blurring the line between commercial enterprise and governmental authority. This model raised questions about accountability and sovereignty that would not be fully resolved until the 19th century. The rising power of private commercial entities relative to state authority is a defining governance trend of this period.

---

## Social Structures

The Atlantic slave trade created the African diaspora and transformed the demographic character of the Americas. By 1700, enslaved Africans and their descendants outnumbered Europeans in much of the Caribbean and parts of South America. African cultural traditions — religious practices, musical forms, languages — survived and adapted in the New World, creating hybrid Afro-American cultures. The racial hierarchy embedded in Atlantic slavery was legally codified in slave codes and the casta system, creating a social order in which race determined legal status, occupational opportunities, and life chances. This racialization of social hierarchy was a distinctive product of Atlantic slave economies.

---

## Human Migration

The Atlantic slave trade was the largest forced migration in human history. Between 1500 and 1900, approximately 12.5 million Africans were shipped to the Americas; about 2 million died during the Middle Passage alone. The demographic impact on West and Central Africa was profound: while American crops partially offset population losses through increased food supply, the removal of millions of young adults (primarily men) disrupted family structures, agricultural systems, and political stability across the continent. Simultaneously, European voluntary migration to the Americas — though smaller in absolute numbers — established settler populations that would eventually demand political independence.

---

## Technology & Environment

The plantation complex produced severe environmental consequences: sugar cultivation exhausted soils and required constant expansion into new territory (a major driver of deforestation in Brazil and the Caribbean). The monoculture model of plantation agriculture created ecological fragility, making plantation economies vulnerable to crop diseases and price shocks. The global silver trade, meanwhile, had long-term environmental consequences at Potosí: mercury (used in silver refining) contaminated soil and water, and the mountain itself was gradually hollowed out.

> [!exam-tip]
> When analyzing the **Atlantic slave trade**, always address **African agency** — some African rulers actively participated in the trade. This doesn't justify the trade, but it adds the complexity AP graders reward: avoid portraying Africa as purely passive victim.
