# Unit 2.1: Key People and Groups — The Silk Roads

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves to use travelers' accounts as primary source stimuli. Know Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta well — their observations are evidence of what the Silk Roads made possible.

---

## Marco Polo (1254–1324)
**Time Period:** Late 13th–early 14th century
**Region:** Venice → Central Asia → China (Yuan Dynasty)

- Venetian merchant who traveled the overland Silk Roads to the court of Kublai Khan and spent nearly two decades in China, returning to write *The Travels of Marco Polo*
- His account described the wealth and sophistication of China, fueling European desire for direct access to Asian trade goods — a long-term cause of the Age of Exploration
- His journey was only possible because of the *Pax Mongolica* — he traveled under Mongol protection across Central Asia; without Mongol unification, such a journey would have been impossible

## Ibn Battuta (1304–c. 1368/69)
**Time Period:** 14th century
**Region:** Morocco → Middle East → East Africa → South Asia → Southeast Asia → China

- Moroccan Muslim scholar and traveler who journeyed approximately 75,000 miles over 29 years, visiting nearly every Muslim-ruled territory and many beyond
- His account (*Rihla*) is a primary source for the spread of Islamic civilization along trade routes, the condition of the Silk Roads under Mongol successors, and the nature of diasporic Muslim merchant communities
- His travels demonstrate the Islamic world's role as the connective tissue of the Silk Roads — he was welcomed in Muslim communities from Mali to the Malabar Coast because Islam had spread along those same routes

## Mongol Empire (1206–1368)
**Time Period:** 13th–14th centuries
**Region:** Eurasia

- The Mongol conquests initially disrupted Silk Road trade by destroying key cities (Samarkand, Baghdad), but the establishment of the Pax Mongolica (c. 1250s–1340s) created unprecedented safety for overland travel
- Mongol rulers actively promoted trade by protecting merchants, standardizing weights and measures, and building caravanserai; they profited from taxing commerce
- The collapse of Mongol rule (Yuan collapse in China 1368; Ilkhanate collapse mid-14th century) destabilized the overland Silk Road and accelerated European desire for maritime alternatives

## Sogdian Merchants
**Time Period:** Antiquity through c. 10th century (declining after Islamic conquest, but lasting into the early medieval period)
**Region:** Central Asia (Samarkand, Bukhara)

- Sogdian merchants were the dominant middlemen on the overland Silk Roads for centuries, operating from the city-states of modern Uzbekistan
- They established diasporic trading colonies from China to the Byzantine Empire, using family networks and shared language to build trust-based commercial relationships across enormous distances
- Their letters (the "Sogdian Ancient Letters," found in China) are primary sources for how Silk Road trade actually functioned — evidence of merchant networks, prices, and routes

## Muslim Merchants
**Time Period:** 7th century–1450 and beyond
**Region:** Middle East, Indian Ocean, Silk Roads broadly

- After the rise of Islam in the 7th century, Muslim merchants gradually displaced Sogdians and others to dominate both overland and maritime trade networks
- Muslim merchants spread Islam wherever they traded — their commercial diasporas in East Africa (Swahili coast), Southeast Asia (Malacca), and the Indian subcontinent were the primary mechanism of Islam's geographic expansion in this period
- The Islamic world's embrace of commerce (unlike Christian Europe's suspicion of profit) made Muslim merchants effective agents of both trade and cultural diffusion

## Chinese Rulers (Song and Yuan Dynasties)
**Time Period:** 960–1368
**Region:** East Asia (China)

- Song Dynasty rulers promoted maritime trade, developing advanced ships (junks), compasses, and the world's first paper money — all of which enhanced Silk Road commerce
- Yuan Dynasty (Mongol-ruled China) integrated China into the broader Mongol trade network, making Chinese porcelain, silk, and other luxury goods more accessible to western markets than ever before
- The Ming Dynasty's initial continuation of maritime trade (Zheng He's voyages) before abrupt withdrawal illustrates how state policy could open or close China's participation in global networks

## Yersinia pestis (Plague Bacterium)
**Time Period:** 1330s–1350s most destructively
**Region:** Central Asia → Middle East → Europe → North Africa

- Not a person or group, but the disease organism that exploited Silk Road networks to devastate Eurasian populations; listed here because the AP exam treats the Black Death as a transformative historical agent
- Traveled from its origins in Central Asian rodent populations westward along the Silk Roads, killing an estimated 25–50% of the populations it reached
- Its demographic consequences — labor shortages in Europe, political instability in the Yuan and Ilkhanate — reshaped every region it touched, making it one of the most consequential results of Silk Road connectivity
