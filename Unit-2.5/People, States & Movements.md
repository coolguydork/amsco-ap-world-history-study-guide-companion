# People, States & Movements — Topic 2.5

> [!exam-tip]
> The three travelers — Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta, and Margery Kempe — are the AP exam's favorite primary sources for this topic. Know each one's **point of view**, **purpose**, **historical situation**, and **audience**. The exam will give you an excerpt and ask you to evaluate its usefulness as historical evidence (Sourcing & Situation skill).

## Key Travelers

**Marco Polo (c. 1245–1324)**
A Venetian merchant who traveled to the court of Kublai Khan in the late 13th century (c. 1271–1295). After returning to Italy, he dictated *The Travels of Marco Polo*, describing the wealth, urbanization, and customs of China — including paper money, silk clothing, and Hangzhou's size. Many Europeans initially disbelieved his accounts. His **point of view** was that of a merchant outsider; he remained culturally foreign throughout his journey, which sharpened his observational eye but also skewed him toward trade-related details. Only when other travelers confirmed his route did Europe accept China's prosperity. (AMSCO pp. 114, 116)

> [!tip]
> **Sourcing Polo:** Strength — detailed firsthand descriptions of Chinese urbanization and commerce. Limitation — as a Christian European merchant, he filtered observations through his own cultural lens and commercial interests; he may have exaggerated to impress European audiences.

**Ibn Battuta (1304–1353)**
A Muslim scholar and jurist from Morocco who spent roughly 30 years traveling across the Dar al-Islam — Central Asia, Southeast Asia, South Asia, China, Spain, North Africa, and Mali. His account *A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Traveling* is one of the most detailed primary sources on the medieval Islamic world. Unlike Polo, Battuta traveled as an **insider** — welcomed as a respected Muslim jurist (*qadi*) and Sufi student wherever he went. His **point of view** was devoutly religious; he evaluated societies by how faithfully they practiced Islam. See [[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]]. (AMSCO p. 116)

> [!tip]
> **Sourcing Ibn Battuta:** Strength — insider access to Muslim courts, madrasas, and merchants; evaluates Islamic law in practice across a vast geography. Limitation — as a Muslim jurist, he paid little attention to non-Muslim populations and judged societies by Islamic standards, potentially distorting his view of religious diversity.

**Margery Kempe (c. 1373–c. 1440)**
An English Christian mystic who dictated *The Book of Margery Kempe* — one of the earliest autobiographies in English. Kempe could neither read nor write; scribes recorded her accounts of pilgrimages to Jerusalem, Rome, Germany, and Spain. Her **point of view** was that of a middle-class medieval woman with intense spiritual visions and 14 children; her work is significant both as a travel account and as a rare firsthand record of a medieval woman's inner life. Less focused on political or commercial observations than Polo or Battuta. (AMSCO p. 116)

> [!tip]
> **Sourcing Kempe:** Strength — rare female perspective on medieval travel and spirituality; provides social and devotional detail. Limitation — her focus on personal mystical experience means she provides less systematic geographic or cultural observation than Polo or Battuta.

**Rashid al-Din (c. 1247–1318)**
A Persian historian and physician at the Mongol court who compiled the *Jami' al-Tawarikh* ("Compendium of Chronicles") — a comprehensive world history commissioned by the Mongol Ilkhanate. His work documented Mongol history and cross-cultural connections across Afro-Eurasia and is a key secondary source on Mongol-era exchange. (AMSCO Summary Card)

**Xuanzang (7th century CE)**
A Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled to India and back along the Silk Roads, bringing back Buddhist scriptures. His journey helped popularize Buddhism in China and facilitated the doctrinal synthesis that produced Chan Buddhism. (AMSCO p. 112)

## Key States & Kingdoms

**Song Dynasty China (960–1279)**
Under the Song, [[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism\|Neo-Confucianism]] developed and spread to neighboring states. The printing press made Buddhist and Confucian texts widely available. Hangzhou was the cultural and commercial capital of southern China. (AMSCO p. 112)

**Srivijaya Empire (Sumatra, c. 7th–13th c.)**
A sea-based Hindu kingdom on Sumatra that facilitated Indian Ocean trade and spread Hindu culture through maritime Southeast Asia. (AMSCO p. 113)

**Majapahit Kingdom (Java, 1293–1527)**
A Buddhist kingdom on Java that eventually absorbed Hindu, Islamic, and Chinese-Buddhist influences, exemplifying Southeast Asia's role as a cultural crossroads. (AMSCO p. 113)

**Khmer Empire / Angkor Kingdom (present-day Cambodia)**
The most powerful kingdom in mainland Southeast Asia. The royal monuments at Angkor Thom display both Hindu artwork (adorning earlier structures) and later Buddhist additions — physical evidence of sequential religious diffusion. (AMSCO p. 113)

**Sinhala Dynasties (Sri Lanka)**
Buddhist kingdoms where monasteries became major centers of Buddhist study. Buddhist priests wielded significant political influence, advising monarchs on governance. (AMSCO p. 113)

## Key Movements

**Spread of Buddhism**
Moved from India to China via the Silk Roads (Xuanzang, 7th c.), then adapted into Chan/Zen Buddhism. Spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam through Chinese cultural influence. Spread to Southeast Asia via Indian Ocean trade. See [[Concept Notes/Buddhism|Buddhism]].

**Spread of Islam**
Carried by merchants, missionaries, and conquerors into West Africa (Timbuktu), South Asia (Urdu language, Bhakti response), and Southeast Asia (Java, Malay Peninsula). Islam unified its recipients into the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]]–connected Dar al-Islam. See [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Overview|Indian Ocean Overview]] and [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview|Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview]].

**The Crusades (as cultural exchange)**
Western European Crusaders encountered Byzantine and Islamic cultures, stimulating demand for Eastern goods and opening Europe to new ideas — and to disease. The resulting economic activity (credit, land markets) contributed to the rise of a European middle class. See [[Concept Notes/Crusades|Crusades]]. (AMSCO p. 115)
