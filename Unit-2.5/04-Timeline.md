# Unit 2.5: Timeline — Cultural Consequences of Connectivity

#apwh #unit-2 #period-1

## Big Picture

This timeline documents how each major trade network served as a corridor of cultural transmission, making the period c. 1200–1450 one of the most consequential eras of religious and intellectual exchange in world history. From the transmission of Chinese technology westward, to the spread of Islam into West Africa and Southeast Asia, to the diffusion of Persian literary culture across the Islamic world, these events demonstrate that trade routes were highways of culture, not just commerce.

## Timeline Table

| Date | Event | Route/Region | Cultural Consequence |
|------|-------|-------------|----------------------|
| c. 700–900 | Paper-making technology spreads from China to the Islamic world | Silk Roads → Central Asia → Baghdad | Cheaper books and manuscripts accelerate Islamic scholarship; Baghdad's House of Wisdom thrives on paper |
| c. 1000–1200 | Buddhism declines in India as Islam arrives; Buddhist traditions consolidate in China, SE Asia, Japan | Silk Roads + Indian Ocean | Buddhist art, architecture (stupas, pagodas), and scripture become defining features of East/Southeast Asian culture |
| c. 1100s | Paper-making reaches Europe via Islamic Spain and Sicily | Mediterranean/Trans-Saharan connections | European literacy expands; universities and scriptoria benefit from cheaper writing material |
| c. 1200 | Sufi orders (tariqas) establish networks across Persia, Central Asia, and into South Asia | Silk Roads + Indian Ocean coast | Sufism becomes the dominant form of popular Islam; lodges serve as both religious and commercial nodes |
| c. 1200–1300 | Gunpowder weapons technology spreads from China westward through Mongol conquests | Silk Roads / Mongol military routes | Gunpowder empires (Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal) become possible; European military technology is transformed |
| 1206 | Delhi Sultanate founded in India; Muslim rulers begin patronizing Sufi orders | Indian subcontinent | Islam becomes established religion of northern India; Hindu-Muslim cultural synthesis begins |
| c. 1250–1350 | Persian becomes fully established as the literary and administrative language of the Islamic world from Egypt to India | Silk Roads + Indian Ocean | Persian poetry (Rumi, Hafiz), architecture, and administrative practice become the prestige culture of Islamic civilization |
| c. 1270–1295 | Marco Polo travels Silk Roads to China and back | Silk Roads | European awareness of Asian wealth and culture grows; motivates later desire for direct sea routes to Asia |
| c. 1300 | Islam firmly established on East African coast (Swahili city-states: Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi) | Indian Ocean trade routes | Arabic script, Islamic architecture, and Swahili (Bantu + Arabic fusion) language develop |
| c. 1300 | Islam begins spreading into maritime Southeast Asia via Indian Ocean merchants | Indian Ocean | Local rulers convert to Islam for commercial and political advantage; Malacca will become major Islamic center by 1400s |
| 1324 | Mansa Musa's hajj to Mecca | Trans-Saharan + overland to Mecca | West African Islam gains prestige; Islamic scholars and architects travel to Mali; Timbuktu grows as intellectual center |
| c. 1325–1354 | Ibn Battuta's travels across Islamic world and beyond | All three major networks | His *Rihla* documents Islamic cultural unity and local variation; provides key primary source on cultural connectivity |
| c. 1330–1400 | Timbuktu grows as center of Islamic learning, hosting major madrasas | Trans-Saharan route | West Africa produces Islamic scholars who participate in global Islamic intellectual culture |
| 1405–1433 | Zheng He's seven Indian Ocean voyages | Indian Ocean | Chinese goods (porcelain, silk) diffuse further; Chinese knowledge of Indian Ocean world expands; Islamic connections reinforced |
| c. 1400–1450 | Malacca sultanate (founded c. 1400) converts to Islam and becomes major entrepôt | Indian Ocean / maritime SE Asia | Islam becomes dominant religion of maritime Southeast Asia; Malay language becomes the trade lingua franca of the region |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam sometimes presents a timeline or map and asks you to identify what it demonstrates about a historical pattern. For this topic, the key argument is: cultural consequences followed trade routes, and were often more durable than the commercial connections themselves.
