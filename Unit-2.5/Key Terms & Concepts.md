# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 2.5

> [!exam-tip]
> AP Theme codes for this topic: **CDI** (Cultural Developments & Interactions), **TEC** (Technology & Innovation), **SIO** (Social Interactions & Organization). Terms below map to these themes.

## Religion & Syncretism

**Chan Buddhism (Zen Buddhism)**
A syncretic faith that fused Indian Buddhism with Chinese Daoist principles, popularized in part by the 7th-century monk Xuanzang. Under the Song Dynasty (960–1279), it spread widely among the scholar gentry. Its influence extended to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Chan Buddhist painting — exemplified by Mu-ch'i's c. 1250 "sketch style" — became influential across East Asia. (AMSCO p. 112)

**Neo-Confucianism**
A syncretic intellectual tradition that fused Confucian rationalism with Daoist and Buddhist abstract ideas. Originated in the Tang Dynasty but matured in the Song. Spread to Japan, Vietnam, and became Korea's official state ideology. See [[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]]. (AMSCO p. 112)

**[[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]]**
The blending of two or more religious or cultural traditions into a new, hybrid form. Topic 2.5 is full of syncretic examples: Chan Buddhism (Buddhist + Daoist), Urdu language (Sanskrit-Hindi + Arabic + Persian), Swahili (Bantu + Arabic), Javanese Islam (Mughal + local + Chinese-Buddhist traits). A key concept for comparison essays.

**Dar al-Islam**
"House of Islam" — the network of Muslim-ruled lands stretching from West Africa to Southeast Asia. Ibn Battuta traveled primarily within the Dar al-Islam. Its shared legal, scholarly, and commercial culture was itself a product of cultural diffusion. (AMSCO p. 117)

**Bhakti Movement**
Hindu devotional movement in South Asia that sought spiritual links between Hinduism and Islam, smoothing the cultural encounter between the two traditions. See [[Concept Notes/Bhakti Movement|Bhakti Movement]]. (AMSCO p. 113)

## Technology Transfer

> [!tip]
> AP essays on technology diffusion often ask *where something originated* (usually China or the Islamic world) and *how* it reached Europe (almost always via Islamic intermediaries). Know the chain.

**Papermaking**
Originated in China; spread to Europe via the Islamic world in the 13th century. Combined with printing technology to drive a rise in literacy and eventually contribute to the European Renaissance. (AMSCO p. 114)

**Gunpowder**
Originated in China; spread westward along trade routes. Transformed warfare across Afro-Eurasia. (AMSCO p. 114)

**Magnetic compass**
Chinese invention; diffused via trade to enable more reliable open-ocean navigation. See also [[Unit-2.1/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts (2.1)]]. (AMSCO pp. 78, 114)

**Lateen sail**
A triangular sail that allows ships to sail closer to the wind, enabling more flexible ocean navigation. Spread through the Indian Ocean and Mediterranean trade networks. See [[Unit-2.3/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts (2.3)]]. (AMSCO pp. 97, 114)

**Stern rudder**
A steering device mounted at the ship's rear that greatly improved maneuverability. Diffused from Chinese shipbuilding into broader maritime use. (AMSCO pp. 97, 114)

**Astrolabe**
An astronomical instrument used to determine latitude by measuring the position of stars. Improved seafaring precision. Associated with Islamic scholarly advances in astronomy. (AMSCO pp. 97, 114)

**Champa rice**
A fast-ripening, drought-resistant rice strain from Vietnam (Champa Kingdom); spread to China and India, supporting population growth, urbanization, and increased agricultural output. (AMSCO p. 114)

## Language & Cultural Blending

**Swahili**
A Bantu-based language with significant Arabic vocabulary, spoken along the East African coast. Product of centuries of Indian Ocean trade interaction between Bantu Africans and Arab merchants. (AMSCO p. 113)

**Urdu**
A language of South Asia combining Sanskrit-based Hindi with Arabic and Persian (Farsi) influences — a linguistic record of the Islamic encounter with the Indian subcontinent. (AMSCO pp. 27, 113)

## Disease

**Black Death (bubonic plague)**
Caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*; spread westward along trade routes from Central Asia, reaching Europe via the Mediterranean in 1347. A major epidemic 1347–1351 killed an estimated 25 million in Europe alone. Devastated Constantinople in 1346 and 1349. Weakened the European feudal system by reducing agricultural labor. A prime example of the *unintended consequences* of trade connectivity. (AMSCO p. 115)

## Urban Centers

**Hangzhou**
A Chinese city of approximately one million people under the Song Dynasty; center of culture and trade at the southern end of the Grand Canal. Described by Marco Polo. Home to poets and artists as well as a thriving Arab merchant community. (AMSCO p. 114)

**Timbuktu**
A West African city on the Niger River that became a major center of Islamic scholarship and trans-Saharan trade. Evidence of Islam's intellectual diffusion into sub-Saharan Africa. See [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview|Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 113)

**Samarkand**
A Central Asian city on the Silk Roads; known for Islamic scholarship, bustling markets, and as a waystation for merchants. Raided and ultimately weakened by Tamerlane. See [[Unit-2.1/Silk Roads Overview|Silk Roads Overview]]. (AMSCO pp. 79, 114)

**Constantinople**
Byzantine capital at the crossroads of Europe and Asia. Weakened by the Fourth Crusade (1204) and devastated by the plague (1346, 1349). Fell to the Ottoman Turks in 1453 — an event often used to mark the end of the Middle Ages. See [[Concept Notes/Crusades|Crusades]]. (AMSCO p. 114)

## Key Distinctions for the Exam

> [!warning]
> Students often confuse the *mechanism* of diffusion with the *result*. Always specify both in an essay: e.g., "Buddhism spread via Silk Roads merchants and monks (*mechanism*), resulting in the syncretic Chan Buddhism in China (*result*)." Generic claims like "trade spread ideas" will not earn AP evidence points.

> [!tip]
> **Continuity vs. Change:** Many religions that spread still retained their core identity while adapting locally. Islam in Southeast Asia blended with local traditions rather than replacing them outright — this is *change with continuity*, a classic CCOT argument.
