## Historical Reasoning Skills

### Causation
**Cause → Effect chains for this topic:**

Trade route stability under the [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]] → safer long-distance travel → more merchants, missionaries, and scholars crossing Afro-Eurasia → accelerated diffusion of religions, technologies, and ideas.

Islamic trade networks in the Indian Ocean → Muslim merchants settling in Southeast Asian port cities → intermarriage and royal conversion → Islam spreads to Java and the Malay Peninsula by c. 1200–1450.

Crusader contact with Byzantine and Islamic worlds → European demand for Eastern goods → expanded trade → Black Death spreads to Europe (1347) → 25 million deaths → labor shortage → weakening of [[Concept Notes/Feudalism|Feudalism]].

> [!exam-tip]
> The Black Death is the exam's go-to example of **unintended consequences** of exchange networks. If a prompt asks you to explain a consequence of Afro-Eurasian trade, disease diffusion is a strong, specific argument.

### Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

**What changed c. 1200–1450:**
- Islam spread into new regions (West Africa, Southeast Asia), creating Swahili and Urdu as blended languages
- Buddhism was absorbed into syncretic traditions (Chan Buddhism in China; absorbed into local practice in Southeast Asia)
- Printing and papermaking drove rising literacy across Afro-Eurasia
- Technology (compass, lateen sail) improved maritime capacity
- The Black Death reshaped European demographics and weakened feudalism

**What stayed the same (continuity):**
- Core religious identities (Buddhism, Islam, Hinduism, Christianity) persisted even as they adapted locally
- Trade routes themselves were a structural continuity — the Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, and Trans-Saharan networks had functioned for centuries; 2.5 shows their *cultural* effects, not their creation
- Scholar-merchant-pilgrim networks within Dar al-Islam remained intact

> [!tip]
> Good CCOT thesis structure: "While [continuity], the period 1200–1450 saw significant change in [specific outcome] due to [specific cause]."

### Comparison

**Marco Polo vs. Ibn Battuta — insider/outsider perspectives:**

| | Marco Polo | Ibn Battuta |
|--|-----------|-------------|
| Origin | Venice (Christian, merchant) | Morocco (Muslim scholar/jurist) |
| Travel sphere | East Asia — culturally foreign to him | Dar al-Islam — culturally familiar |
| Point of view | Outsider; observational curiosity enhanced by cultural distance | Insider; evaluated societies by Islamic standards |
| Focus | Commerce, urbanization, material wealth | Islamic law, piety, scholarly networks |
| Limitation | Commercial bias; possible exaggeration for European audience | Underrepresented non-Muslim societies |

> [!exam-tip]
> A comparison of Polo and Battuta is one of the most commonly tested SAQ and DBQ prompts for this topic. Know the insider/outsider distinction cold.

**Buddhism vs. Islam — diffusion mechanisms compared:**

| | Buddhism | Islam |
|--|---------|-------|
| Primary mechanism | Monks and merchants along Silk Roads | Merchants, missionaries, and rulers via Indian Ocean and Sahara |
| Adaptation | Fused with Daoism (Chan), Confucianism (Neo-Confucianism) | Blended with local traditions in Southeast Asia and Africa |
| Syncretic result | Chan/Zen Buddhism; Neo-Confucianism | Swahili culture; Javanese Islam |
| Key regions | East Asia, Southeast Asia | West Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia |

## Historiographical Perspective

**Cultural Diffusion vs. Imposition:** Historians debate whether the spread of Islam in Southeast Asia and Africa was primarily peaceful (commercial diffusion, intermarriage, voluntary adoption) or coercive (conquest, political pressure). For this period (1200–1450), the weight of evidence favors *peaceful commercial diffusion* — AMSCO notes that Muslim rulers on Java blended their faith with local traditions rather than replacing them outright. (AMSCO p. 113)

**Sourcing and Situation:** The Think As a Historian section for this topic (AMSCO p. 120) focuses on evaluating primary sources by asking: Who created it? What is their point of view? For what purpose? For what audience? In what historical situation? These are not just academic questions — they are the AP exam's Document Analysis rubric criteria for the DBQ.
