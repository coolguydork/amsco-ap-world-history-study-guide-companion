# Timeline & Chronology — Technological Innovations

## Curated Key Dates

| Date | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 12th century | Magnetic compass reaches European and Chinese mariners | Enabled direction-finding at night and in cloudy weather; adopted from China |
| 12th century | Astrolabe improved by Muslim navigators | Allowed latitude measurement; diffused to Europe via Al-Andalus and trade |
| 1221 | Gunpowder weapons reach Europe (via Mongols) | Military technology from China → Mongols → Europe; reshaped warfare and conquest |
| 15th century | Portuguese develop the caravel | Fast, maneuverable ocean-going ship; lateen sails allow sailing into wind |
| 1433 | Ming China withdraws from Indian Ocean exploration | Zheng He's voyages end; power vacuum in Indian Ocean that Europeans later fill |
| 1488 | Bartolomeu Dias rounds Cape of Good Hope | First proof of a sea route around Africa to the Indian Ocean |
| 1492 | Columbus reaches the Americas | Technologies (caravel, compass, astrolabe, lateen sail) enable transatlantic crossing |
| 1498 | Vasco da Gama reaches India by sea | First direct European sea link to Indian Ocean; bypasses Ottoman overland routes |
| 1519–1522 | Magellan's fleet circumnavigates the globe | Confirmed Earth's actual size; validated navigational technology and wind-pattern knowledge |
| 1600 | English East India Company (EIC) founded | Joint-stock model applied to long-distance Asian trade |
| 1602 | Dutch East India Company (VOC) founded | Most powerful joint-stock trading company; displaced Portugal in Indian Ocean |
| 1697 | Tsar Peter the Great visits Western Europe | Studies military/naval technology; illustrates continued technological diffusion |

## Narrative Chronology

**Pre-1450 background.** The technologies that powered European exploration mostly existed before 1450 — but in dispersed locations. The compass was Chinese, the astrolabe Islamic, the lateen sail Arab. Al-Andalus (Islamic Iberia) was a critical transmission point: as Islamic scholars and their texts entered Christian Europe through Spain, navigational and astronomical knowledge diffused westward. The classical Greek tradition of astronomical chart-making also fed into European practice.

**1450s–1490s: Portugal leads.** Under Prince Henry's patronage (d. 1460), Portugal systematically explored Africa's Atlantic coast. Dias's rounding of the Cape of Good Hope in 1488 was the crucial technological proof-of-concept — a sea route to Asia existed. Within four years, Columbus had crossed the Atlantic (1492), and within a decade da Gama had reached India (1498). The caravel made all of this possible; without it, open-ocean voyages in the Atlantic remained too dangerous.

**1490s–1520s: Spain enters.** Columbus's landfall inaugurated Spain's westward program. The joint Spanish-Portuguese competition — formalized in the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) — structured the early phase of European maritime expansion. Magellan's circumnavigation (1519–1522) capped this phase by confirming the full geography of the globe.

**1600–1750: Northern Europeans displace Iberians.** The English and Dutch challenged Portuguese dominance in the Indian Ocean in the early 1600s. The VOC (1602) and EIC (1600) applied the joint-stock financial model to long-distance trade, creating durable institutions that outlasted individual voyages. The Dutch fluyt ship — optimized for cargo efficiency — replaced the Portuguese caravel and carrack as the dominant commercial vessel.

## Exam Tip: Periodization

> [!exam-tip]
> AP Period 2 (c. 1450–1750) begins precisely with the developments in Topic 4.1. Examiners frequently ask about **what changed** at this period boundary. The answer centers on technology enabling transoceanic connection: caravel + lateen sail + compass + astrolabe = the Western Hemisphere entering global trade networks for the first time. Before 1450, Afro-Eurasian networks were extensive but the Americas were completely separate. After 1492, they were permanently linked.
