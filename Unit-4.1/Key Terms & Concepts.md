# Key Terms & Concepts — Technological Innovations

## Navigation & Ships

**Caravel**
Small, fast, maneuverable sailing ship developed by the Portuguese in the 15th century. Approximately 75 feet long with 2–3 masts and lateen sails. Its design allowed it to survive open-ocean storms better than earlier ships and to sail into the wind, making it the primary vessel for early European exploration. (AMSCO p. 191, 194)

**Carrack**
Larger trading vessel (~150 feet) using both square and lateen sails on 3–4 masts. Used primarily for bulk trade cargo by Portugal from the 14th–17th centuries. Less maneuverable than the caravel but with greater cargo capacity. (AMSCO p. 194)

**Fluyt**
Dutch trading ship (~80 feet, square sails, 2–3 masts) optimized for commercial efficiency in the 16th–17th centuries. The Dutch VOC relied on it for high-volume trade in the Indian Ocean. (AMSCO p. 194)

**Lateen sail**
Triangular sail used by Arab sailors in the Indian Ocean. Unlike ancient square sails (which required the wind to blow from behind), the lateen could catch wind on *either* side of the ship, allowing sailors to travel in multiple directions — including into the wind. Combined with square sails, it enabled ocean crossings for the first time. (AMSCO p. 193)

**Magnetic compass**
Direction-finding device originally invented in China (initially for fortune-telling, later navigational use). Works because Earth acts as a giant bar magnet — freely moving magnetized needles align with the north-south axis. Reached European and Chinese mariners in the 12th century. Essential for navigation in cloudy weather or at night. (AMSCO pp. 191, 193)

**Astrolabe**
Instrument that allowed sailors to determine their latitude — how far north or south they were from the equator — by measuring the angle of the sun or stars. Improved by Muslim navigators in the 12th century and diffused to European sailors through cross-cultural contact, including via Al-Andalus (Islamic Spain). (AMSCO pp. 191, 193)

**Cartography**
The science and art of mapmaking. Improvements in cartography during this period gave sailors more accurate maps of coastlines, currents, and star positions. Combined with astronomical charts and compass technology, better maps dramatically reduced navigational uncertainty. (AMSCO p. 191)

**Astronomical chart**
Map of stars and constellations used to determine a ship's direction and location. Ancient traditions of star-chart making existed in Babylonia/Mesopotamia (2nd millennium BCE), China (5th century BCE), and classical Greece. Telescopes expanded their precision from 1609 onward. (AMSCO pp. 192–193)

> [!tip]
> Remember: the compass tells you *direction* (N/S/E/W); the astrolabe tells you *latitude* (how far north or south). Neither alone was sufficient — sailors needed both, plus knowledge of wind patterns and cartography, to navigate the open ocean reliably.

## Economic & Political Concepts

**Maritime empires**
Empires built on control of sea routes and trading ports rather than contiguous land territory. Portugal and Spain were the first; Britain, France, and the Netherlands followed. Their power rested on naval technology, trading post networks, and the ability to project force along ocean routes. (AMSCO p. 192)

**Primogeniture laws**
Legal inheritance rules giving an entire estate to the eldest son. In Europe, this left younger sons without land or income, creating a class of ambitious men willing to seek fortune abroad — one social push factor behind European exploration and colonization. (AMSCO p. 191)

**Omani-European rivalry**
Competition between the Omani sultanate (based in the Arabian Peninsula / East Africa) and European powers (especially Portugal) for control of Indian Ocean trade routes and coastal ports. Portugal established forts in Oman but faced repeated local resistance. This rivalry illustrates that European expansion was actively contested, not unopposed. (AMSCO p. 192)

**Gunpowder weapons**
Originally invented in China, improved by the [[Concept Notes/Mongols\|Mongols]] into offensive weapons, and reaching Europe by 1221. European powers — along with Turks, Mughals, and Chinese — used gunpowder weapons to build and defend empires. European metallurgical advantages led to particularly deadly refinements. Sea pirates (including the Dutch "Sea Beggars") also adopted gunpowder weapons. (AMSCO p. 194)

> [!warning]
> The AP exam often tests whether students can identify the *origins* of technologies. Compass = China. Astrolabe = improved by Islamic scholars. Lateen sail = Arab/Indian Ocean. Gunpowder = China. Only the caravel is genuinely Portuguese — and even it synthesized multiple traditions. Never attribute these inventions to Europe in an FRQ.

## People & Institutions

**Prince Henry the Navigator (Portugal, 1394–1460)**
Portuguese prince who bankrolled expeditions down Africa's Atlantic coast toward the Cape of Good Hope. Never sailed far out to sea himself, but his court at Sagres synthesized knowledge from Greek, Islamic, and Asian navigational traditions. His patronage established Portugal's early lead in maritime exploration. (AMSCO p. 192)

**[[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|Joint-stock companies]]**
Business organizations in which investors pool capital and share profits and risks proportionally. Critical for funding expensive, high-risk long-distance trade voyages. The English [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC, founded 1600) and the [[Concept Notes/Dutch East India Company\|Dutch East India Company]] (VOC, founded 1602) were the most powerful, effectively acting as arms of state power in the Indian Ocean. (Clean Key Dates)

**Al-Andalus**
Islamic-governed region of the Iberian Peninsula (modern Spain/Portugal), c. 711–1492. A crucial zone of cultural diffusion where Islamic science, mathematics, and navigational knowledge entered European intellectual life — including improvements to the astrolabe and other instruments that later powered European exploration. (AMSCO p. 192)

> [!exam-tip]
> **Key AP framing:** The technologies enabling European ocean exploration were products of **cross-cultural diffusion** through existing trade networks — exactly the Learning Objective for Topic 4.1. When writing FRQ evidence, always connect the technology to its origin culture and the mechanism of transfer (trade, scholarly contact, Al-Andalus, etc.).
