## Economy: Strategies

**Capital**
Material wealth available to produce more wealth. As European entrepreneurs entered long-distance markets, capital accumulation grew — passing from entrepreneurs to laborers, enabling investment. Silver from the Americas dramatically increased actual capital in circulation. (p. 232)

**[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|Mercantilism]]**
The economic doctrine that national wealth equals gold and silver reserves. Mercantilist states maximized exports, minimized imports, and used colonies as sources of raw materials and captive markets for finished goods. The dominant European economic framework c. 1450–1750.

**Commercial Revolution**
The transformation from barter-based local trade to a gold-and-silver international economy, driven by four factors: European overseas colonies, new ocean trade routes, population growth, and inflation. Affected all world regions. (p. 233)

**[[Concept Notes/Price Revolution|Price Revolution]]**
The high rate of inflation during the 16th and early 17th centuries, caused partly by population growth and partly by the massive influx of American silver and gold into European markets. A direct consequence of Potosí silver mining. (p. 233)

**[[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|Joint-stock companies]]**
Corporations in which investors bought shares (stock) and split both profits and risks of trade and colonization ventures. Offered **limited liability** — investors could not lose more than their initial investment. Made large-scale empire-building commercially viable for the European middle class. (p. 233)

**Limited liability**
The legal principle that an investor is not responsible for a company's debts beyond the amount of their own investment. Key innovation that made joint-stock companies attractive and fueled the Commercial Revolution. (p. 233)

**[[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (British)**
Founded 1600 by royal charter. A joint-stock company that drove British commercial expansion into Asia, eventually becoming the administrative arm of British India. One of the most powerful corporations in history. (p. 233)

**Dutch East India Company (VOC)**
*Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie* — founded 1602. The first major joint-stock company; pioneered the shareholder investment model for empire. Dominated the spice trade in Southeast Asia and established Batavia (Jakarta) c. 1619–1620 as its Asian hub. Made enormous profits in the Spice Islands. (p. 233)

> [!exam-tip]
> The VOC is the AP exam's go-to example of joint-stock company innovation. Know: founded 1602, Netherlands, spice trade, Batavia. It outcompeted the Portuguese in Southeast Asia through speed, finance, and organization — not just military force.

**Triangular trade**
The three-legged Atlantic trading system: (1) European manufactured goods (firearms, cloth) to West Africa; (2) enslaved Africans to the Americas; (3) American commodities (sugar, tobacco, rum) to Europe. Sugar was the most profitable American export. By the 1700s, Caribbean sugar and rum financed enormous fortunes in Britain. (p. 234)

**Monopoly**
An exclusive right to trade in a commodity or region, chartered by a European ruler to a joint-stock company or government entity. Spain's tobacco monopoly alone generated ~⅓ of total government revenue. Monopolies sustained mercantilist control over colonial trade. (p. 235)

## Government: Latin America

**Viceroys**
Spanish-appointed administrators who governed colonial territories as representatives of the Spanish crown. There were two main viceroyalties: New Spain (Mexico/Central America) and Peru (South America). Slow transatlantic communication gave viceroys significant de facto autonomy despite formal royal oversight. (p. 236)

**Audiencias**
Royal courts established in Spanish America to hear appeals against viceroy decisions and policies. Designed to keep viceroys accountable to the Spanish crown. An institutional check within colonial administration. (p. 236)

## Culture: Blending

**Syncretism**
The combining of different religious beliefs and practices into a new hybrid system. Emerged across the Atlantic world as enslaved Africans and indigenous peoples blended their traditions with Christianity. See also the [[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]] concept note. (p. 237)

**Santería**
"The way of the saints." Syncretic religion blending West African spiritual traditions with Roman Catholicism. Originated in Cuba; spread across Latin America and North America. (p. 237)

**Vodun**
"Spirit" or "deity." Syncretic belief system originating with Dahomey, Kongo, and Yoruba peoples enslaved in Saint-Domingue (present-day Haiti). Ancestor of Haitian Vodou. (p. 237)

**Candomblé**
"Dance to honor the gods." Syncretic religion combining Yoruba, Fon, and Bantu traditions from different parts of Africa. Developed in Brazil among enslaved Africans. (p. 237)

**Virgin of Guadalupe**
A syncretic Catholic cult that emerged in Mexico around a dark-complexioned Virgin Mary who appeared to an indigenous man in 1531. Catholic saints' days coinciding with indigenous celebrations became focal points for religious blending. Patron saint of New Spain. (p. 238)

**Creoles**
People born in the Americas of Spanish (or European) descent. By 1750, creoles dominated political life in New Spain. Their exclusion from top colonial offices held by peninsulares (Spanish-born) fueled the independence movements of the early 19th century [[Unit-5.2/[[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] & Revolutions Overview|Nationalism & Revolutions Overview]]. (p. 237)

**Polygyny**
The practice of a man having more than one wife. Became more common in West African societies affected by the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]], which removed more than two-thirds of the enslaved population from male-dominated age groups, creating gender imbalance. (p. 236)

## Society: Slave Trade

**Dahomey**
West African kingdom that became economically powerful through participation in the slave trade, raiding neighboring peoples and exchanging enslaved captives for European firearms. Economic dependence on this trade made Dahomey (and similar societies) economically dependent on Europe. (p. 236)

**Oyo**
West African Yoruba kingdom that similarly engaged in slave-raiding. Like Dahomey, Oyo's power grew from firearms acquired through the slave trade, intensifying regional warfare and the overall slave trade cycle. (p. 236)

## Additional Concepts

**Financial bubbles**
Speculative investment schemes based on overinflated share prices. Early 18th-century France (Mississippi Company) and England (South Sea Company) both suffered major bubble collapses, sending many investors into bankruptcy. Contrasts with the more stable Dutch financial model. (p. 234)

**Florentine Codex**
Compiled from 1555 by Spanish Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún; one of the most widely cited primary sources on Aztec life before the Spanish conquest. Despite a Spanish author, it is considered relatively reliable as Sahagún interviewed indigenous informants. (p. 237)
