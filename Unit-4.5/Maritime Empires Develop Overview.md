# Maritime Empires Develop

**Essential Question:** What economic strategies did maritime empires use to increase their power, and how did the developing empires affect political, economic, religious, and cultural dynamics?

**AP Themes:** `GOV` · `ECN` · `SIO`
**Period:** c. 1450–c. 1750 (AP Period 2)
**Printed Pages:** 232–242 (AMSCO)

> [!exam-tip]
> Three words capture this topic on the AP exam: **silver, sugar, and slavery**. These were the engines of maritime empire — know how each powered [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|mercantilism]] and what each cost indigenous and African peoples.

---

## The Big Picture

Maritime (sea-based) empires transformed commerce from small-scale, barter-based local trade into large-scale international exchange using gold and silver. New ocean routes opened a truly global economy. The Atlantic trading system moved labor — including millions of enslaved Africans — and produced a cultural synthesis of African, American, and European peoples. Three commodities drove it all: silver (extracted from the Americas), sugar (grown by enslaved labor), and slavery (the coercive system that made the plantation economy work).

---

## Economic Strategies

### Mercantilism

European powers in the 15th–17th centuries measured national wealth by gold and silver reserves. This produced **mercantilism**: maximize exports, minimize imports, accumulate precious metals. Colonies existed to serve the mother country's balance of trade — supplying raw materials and buying finished goods. See [[Unit-4.5/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts]] for definitions of capital, mercantilism, and related terms.

### The Commercial Revolution

The shift to a gold-and-silver trade economy is called the **Commercial Revolution**. AMSCO identifies four driving factors (p. 233):

1. Development of European overseas colonies
2. Opening of new ocean trade routes
3. Population growth
4. Inflation — partly from population pressure, partly from increased gold and silver in circulation

The high inflation of the 16th–early 17th century is the **[[Concept Notes/Price Revolution|Price Revolution]]**, caused significantly by American silver flooding European markets.

### [[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|Joint-Stock Companies]]

The Dutch, English, and French created **joint-stock companies** — corporations in which investors bought shares and split both profits and risks. **Limited liability** meant an investor's loss was capped at their initial investment. This made empire-building commercially viable for the middle class.

Key companies: the **British East India Company** (EIC, founded 1600) and the **Dutch East India Company** (VOC, founded 1602). Spain and Portugal instead used direct royal grants to explorers. See [[Unit-4.5/People, States & Movements|People, States & Movements]] for the VOC in detail.

The Dutch led European finance: a stock exchange by 1602, the Bank of Amsterdam by 1609, the highest standard of living in Europe. England and France meanwhile suffered from speculative **financial bubbles** — overinflated share schemes that collapsed, causing bankruptcies and economic damage.

### Triangular Trade

The **triangular trade** was a three-legged Atlantic trading system: European manufactured goods (firearms, cloth) → West Africa; enslaved Africans → Americas; sugar, tobacco, rum → Europe. Sugar was the most profitable American commodity. By the 1700s, Caribbean sugar and rum were generating enormous wealth in Britain.

> [!tip]
> The triangular trade is sometimes tested as a Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT) prompt. Note what was *new* (Atlantic route, scale of enslaved-person transport) versus what *continued* (coercive labor, elite extraction of profit).

## Rivalries and Trade Networks

### Indian Ocean Competition

The Portuguese dominated Indian Ocean trade in the 1500s after defeating a combined Muslim–Venetian fleet in 1509 [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview|Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview]]. Morocco then captured and broke the Songhai Empire in 1591 using firearms, disrupting the trans-Saharan network. The Spanish and Portuguese moved to fill that vacuum.

### New Monopolies vs. Continuing Regional Markets

European rulers chartered **monopolies** — exclusive trading rights granted to joint-stock companies or government-backed merchants — to maintain control over trade profits. Spain's tobacco monopoly alone generated roughly one-third of its government revenue.

Meanwhile, traditional regional markets in Afro-Eurasia continued. Improved shipping increased volume: wool and linen from Western Europe, cotton from India, silk from China all circulated through globally connected port cities.

### Effects of the [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Slave Trade|Atlantic Slave Trade]] on Africa

The slave trade devastated West African societies (AMSCO p. 235–236):

- Kingdoms such as **Kongo** were weakened — population loss slowed growth, trade competition drove violence
- Raiding kingdoms (**Dahomey**, **Oyo**) became economically dependent on European goods, especially firearms, creating a cycle of escalating raids
- Gender imbalance: more than two-thirds of those enslaved were male, causing a rise in **polygyny** and forcing women into traditionally male roles
- One silver lining: the **Columbian Exchange** brought new crops (maize, peanuts, manioc) to Africa, eventually spurring population growth

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate the slave trade's *short-term* and *long-term* effects on Africa. Short-term: demographic disruption and violence. Long-term: Columbian Exchange crops partially offset population decline, but economic dependence set the stage for 19th-century imperialism.

## Political and Cultural Changes for Indigenous Peoples

### Colonial Administration

Spanish royalty appointed **viceroys** to govern colonies in their name. To check viceroy independence, **audiencias** (royal courts) heard appeals against viceroy decisions. However, slow communication between Spain and the Americas limited direct crown control. This created de facto autonomy — a tension that would eventually fuel independence movements (**Columbian-born creoles** began pushing for independence from Spain by 1750).

> [!exam-tip]
> The viceroy / audiencia system is a classic AP comparison point: how does Spanish colonial administration compare to the Ottoman timar system or the Mongol administration model? See [[Unit-3.2/Empires: Administration Overview|Empires: Administration Overview]].

### Cultural Destruction and Synthesis

Conquistadors systematically destroyed indigenous records — Cortés ordered the burning of Aztec books, leaving historians dependent on Spanish accounts. The **Florentine Codex** (compiled 1555 by Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún) is among the most reliable surviving sources on Aztec life, but it still reflects Spanish biases.

Spanish and Portuguese transplanted their languages and religion. Spanish predominates across Latin America; Portuguese in Brazil — legacies that persist today. Yet indigenous languages survive in pockets (Guatemala, highland Mexico).

## Global Interactions and Religious Change

### Syncretism in the Americas

The forced mixing of peoples produced **syncretic belief systems** — combinations of different religious traditions. Three key examples (AMSCO p. 237):

- **Santería** — African faith blended with Roman Catholicism; emerged in Cuba, spread across Latin America and North America
- **Vodun** — "spirit/deity"; originated with Dahomey, Kongo, and Yoruba peoples enslaved in Saint-Domingue (Haiti)
- **Candomblé** — "dance to honor the gods"; Yoruba + Fon + Bantu traditions, developed in Brazil

The **African American church** in North America similarly blended Christianity with African spiritual traditions. About 1 in 10 enslaved Africans practiced Islam, making them the first significant Muslim presence in the Americas.

The **Virgin of Guadalupe** cult in Mexico exemplifies Catholic syncretism with indigenous traditions: a dark-complexioned Virgin whose feast day overlapped with an indigenous celebration became the patron saint of New Spain.

### Religious Conflicts from Global Connections

Increased global connections also sharpened religious conflicts:
- **[[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]]** spread Islam widely and may have influenced the development of **Sikhism** (blending Muslim and Hindu traditions)
- The Mughal emperor Akbar attempted to mediate Muslim–Hindu tensions [[Unit-3.2/Empires: Administration Overview|Empires: Administration Overview]]
- The Sunni–Shi'a split worsened Ottoman–Safavid conflict [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview]]
- Catholic–Protestant divisions, and Anglican vs. other Protestant sects, drove European settlement of North America as groups sought religious freedom

> [!tip]
> **[[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]]** is one of the highest-yield concepts across Units 1–5. It shows up in Silk Roads Buddhism, Indian Ocean Islam, and now Atlantic Christianity. When you see a DBQ with religious evidence, ask: is this syncretism or conflict — or both?
