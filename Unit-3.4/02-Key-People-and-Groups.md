# Unit 3.4: Key People and Groups

#apwh #unit-3 #period-2

## One Ruler Per Empire — Administration and Legitimation

Each entry focuses on what the AP exam cares about most: **how** each ruler organized administration and **how** each ruler legitimated their authority. Use these as your evidence anchors for SAQ and LEQ responses.

---

### Suleiman the Magnificent (r. 1520–1566) — Ottoman Empire

**Period:** c. 1450–1750 | **Region:** Anatolia, Middle East, North Africa, SE Europe
**AP Focus:** Administration, Legal Reform, Legitimation through Religion and Law

- **Administration:** Suleiman presided over the most sophisticated administrative system among the six empires. The devshirme system filled the military (janissaries) and civil service with converted Christian-born officials whose loyalty was entirely to the sultan. Grand Viziers — the empire's chief ministers — were typically devshirme graduates, meaning the most powerful administrators in the world were technically the sultan's slaves.
- **Legitimation:** Suleiman was called "the Lawgiver" (Kanuni) by his own subjects — a title that emphasized his role as the source of legal order, synthesizing Islamic law (sharia) with Ottoman customary law (kanun). He also claimed the title of caliph after the Ottoman conquest of Egypt (1517), positioning the sultan as the protector of all Sunni Muslims.
- **Significance for AP:** The millet system — in which religious minorities (Greek Orthodox, Armenians, Jews) governed their own communities under Ottoman supervision — is Suleiman's era's most AP-tested administrative feature. It shows how the Ottomans managed religious diversity **without requiring conversion**.
- **Causation:** Suleiman's military campaigns into Europe (siege of Vienna, 1529) and the Mediterranean demonstrated that Ottoman expansion was both land- and sea-based, distinguishing it from the purely continental expansion of Russia and the Qing.

### Shah Ismail I (r. 1501–1524) — Safavid Empire

**Period:** c. 1450–1750 | **Region:** Persia (modern Iran and surrounding areas)
**AP Focus:** Religious Legitimation, Shia Imposition, Sectarian Conflict

- **Administration:** Shah Ismail founded the Safavid Dynasty by combining military power (his Qizilbash tribal cavalry) with a revolutionary religious claim: he declared himself a divine representative of Shia Islam, blurring the line between political and religious authority in a way that was unique among the six empires.
- **Legitimation:** Ismail imposed **Twelver Shia Islam** as the Safavid state religion, forcibly converting a predominantly Sunni Persian population. This was the most aggressive religious legitimation strategy of any of the six empires — and it deliberately defined Safavid identity in opposition to the Sunni Ottoman Empire. The Safavid-Ottoman conflict was as much a sectarian war (Shia vs. Sunni) as a geopolitical one.
- **Significance for AP:** The Safavids are the primary example of using **religion as a tool of exclusion rather than inclusion**. Compare with Akbar's religious tolerance: both were strategies for managing diverse populations, but the Safavids used religious uniformity to forge a distinct Persian identity, while the Mughal strategy sought to incorporate diverse subjects.
- **Causation:** Safavid Shia imposition generated a permanent Sunni-Shia divide in the Islamic world that shaped the geopolitics of the entire region — the Ottoman-Safavid rivalry was one of the most prolonged conflicts of the period, fought through both direct warfare and proxy conflicts.

### Akbar (r. 1556–1605) — Mughal Empire

**Period:** c. 1450–1750 | **Region:** Indian Subcontinent
**AP Focus:** Religious Tolerance, Administrative Integration, Syncretism

- **Administration:** Akbar's mansabdar system ranked all imperial officials on a scale of 1 to 10,000, assigning each a salary through a temporary land grant (jagir) rather than hereditary landholding. Crucially, Akbar incorporated Hindu Rajput chieftains into the mansabdar hierarchy — meaning the Mughal administrative system was genuinely multi-religious.
- **Legitimation:** Akbar abolished the jizya (the tax on non-Muslims), banned the destruction of Hindu temples, married Hindu Rajput princesses (and allowed them to practice Hinduism), and promoted a syncretic court culture that blended Persian, Hindu, and Islamic artistic traditions. Late in his reign, he promoted Din-i-Ilahi ("Divine Faith"), a personal syncretic religion that drew on Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, and Jainism — though it attracted few followers.
- **Significance for AP:** Akbar is the AP curriculum's premier example of **religious tolerance as a political strategy**. His policies are most commonly compared (and contrasted) with Aurangzeb (who reversed them) and with Ottoman millet system (a different model of tolerating rather than integrating minorities).
- **Causation:** Akbar's tolerance was not purely ideological — it was a practical response to the reality that Muslims were a small ruling minority (roughly 20%) over a Hindu majority. Integration was more sustainable than permanent confrontation.

### Kangxi Emperor (r. 1661–1722) — Qing Dynasty

**Period:** c. 1450–1750 | **Region:** China, Manchuria, Mongolia, Central Asia
**AP Focus:** Dyarchy, Confucian Legitimation, Management of Diversity

- **Administration:** The Kangxi Emperor governed through the **dyarchy system**: every significant government position was held by both a Manchu and a Chinese official simultaneously, ensuring Manchu political control while retaining Chinese administrative expertise. He also retained and expanded the civil service examination system inherited from the Ming Dynasty, signaling continuity with Chinese tradition.
- **Legitimation:** Kangxi performed Confucian rituals, patronized Chinese scholarship (sponsoring massive encyclopedia and dictionary projects), and presented himself as the rightful heir to the Mandate of Heaven — despite being ethnically Manchu rather than Han Chinese. At the same time, he used Tibetan Buddhist imagery and sponsorship to legitimate Qing rule over Mongol and Tibetan subjects, demonstrating a **multi-audience legitimation strategy**.
- **Significance for AP:** The Kangxi Emperor demonstrates that a **foreign dynasty** could govern effectively by adopting the cultural symbols of its subjects. Compare with the earlier Yuan (Mongol) Dynasty, which was less culturally adaptive and shorter-lived.
- **Causation:** The Qing's success in ruling China for nearly three centuries (1644–1912) owes much to this dual strategy: enforcing Manchu identity (requiring Han men to wear the queue hairstyle) while simultaneously performing Chinese cultural legitimacy.

### Ivan the Terrible (r. 1547–1584) — Russian Empire

**Period:** c. 1450–1750 | **Region:** Eastern Europe, Siberia
**AP Focus:** Autocratic Governance, Expansion, Orthodox Christian Legitimation

- **Administration:** Ivan IV (Ivan the Terrible) was the first Russian ruler to formally take the title of **Tsar** (derived from "Caesar"), signaling his claim to imperial status equivalent to the Byzantine emperors. He created the oprichnina — a separate territorial administration run by his personal agents (oprichniki) — to crush the power of the traditional boyar (noble) class and concentrate authority in the tsar.
- **Legitimation:** Ivan used **Russian Orthodox Christianity** as a legitimating tool, positioning Moscow as the "Third Rome" — the rightful heir to the Christian imperial tradition after the fall of Constantinople (1453) to the Ottomans. He commissioned the Cathedral of St. Basil in Moscow as a monument to military victory and divine favor.
- **Significance for AP:** Ivan represents the most **authoritarian** governance model among the six empires — his reign featured mass executions of nobles, the destruction of whole cities, and the creation of a secret police. Compare with Akbar's inclusive approach: both were expanding empires, but through very different political cultures.
- **Causation:** Russia's expansion eastward into Siberia (accelerating in the late 16th century) was driven by fur trade wealth and enabled by firearms technology — paralleling how other gunpowder empires expanded, but in a direction (northward and eastward) distinct from all other empires in the group.

### Askia Muhammad (r. 1493–1528) — Songhai Empire

**Period:** c. 1450–1750 | **Region:** West Africa (Niger River bend)
**AP Focus:** Islamic Legitimation, Administrative Reform, Trans-Saharan Trade

- **Administration:** Askia Muhammad (born Muhammad Ture) seized power from the founder of the Songhai Empire, Sunni Ali, after the latter's death, and immediately embarked on administrative reform. He replaced Sunni Ali's system of personal loyalty appointments with a more systematic structure of appointed provincial governors and Islamic scholars (ulama) serving as judges and administrators — making Songhai's government significantly more institutionalized.
- **Legitimation:** Askia performed the hajj to Mecca in 1497 — explicitly modeling himself on Mansa Musa's famous hajj of 1324–25 — and received the title of Caliph of the Western Sudan from the Sharif of Mecca. He patronized Islamic scholarship at Timbuktu (the Sankore Mosque/University), attracting scholars from across the Islamic world.
- **Significance for AP:** Askia Muhammad shows how West African rulers used **Islamic credentials** to legitimate authority both domestically and in the wider Muslim world — a continuation of the pattern established by Mansa Musa in the Mali Empire (see [[Unit-1.7]]). Compare with the Ottoman sultan's claim to the caliphate: both rulers used Islamic religious prestige as a legitimation tool, but from very different geographic and political contexts.
- **Causation:** Askia's administrative reforms extended Songhai's reach across the Niger River bend, consolidating control over the trans-Saharan trade routes that were the empire's economic lifeline. His reign marked the height of Songhai power before the Moroccan invasion (1591) destroyed the empire.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about **methods of legitimation** — how rulers justified their right to rule. For each of the six rulers above, be able to state: (1) what religious tradition they drew on, (2) one specific action that demonstrated that legitimation, and (3) how it compared to at least one other ruler in the group.

> [!warning]
> Do not confuse **Sunni Ali** (the military conqueror who founded Songhai's dominance, r. 1464–1492) with **Askia Muhammad** (the administrator-ruler who followed him). On the AP exam, Askia Muhammad is the more important figure for questions about Islamic legitimation and administrative organization.
