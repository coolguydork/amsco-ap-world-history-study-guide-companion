# Topic 3.4 — Comparison in Land-Based Empires

> [!summary] Essential Question
> By what methods did empires **increase their societal and cultural influence** from c. 1450–c. 1750?

## Why Comparison Matters Here

Topic 3.4 is explicitly a *comparison* topic — it asks you to analyze **what land-based empires had in common** and **what distinguished them**. This is exactly the kind of thinking the AP exam rewards in LEQ and DBQ responses. By the end of this note, you should be able to compare any two of these empires on: military, bureaucracy, taxation, legitimacy, and decline.

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 3.4 content almost always appears on the AP exam as a **LEQ prompt** asking you to "evaluate the extent to which" land-based empires were similar or different in their methods of consolidation or expansion. Have at least two empires ready to compare on each dimension.

---

## What All Land-Based Empires Had in Common

Despite their differences, every major land-based empire in the period c. 1450–c. 1750 shared these structural features:

1. **Gunpowder weapons** — All relied on cannons, muskets, and artillery to conquer and hold territory. This was the technology that made large, multiethnic empires possible in the first place.

2. **Centralized bureaucracy** — All empires built administrative systems to govern diverse populations: civil servants, tax collectors, provincial officials, governors.

3. **Legitimizing ideology** — All rulers used religion, art, or architecture to justify their authority to subjects who might otherwise resist.

4. **Taxation systems** — All empires extracted resources from peasant populations to fund armies and administration.

5. **Elite military forces** — All empires developed specialized soldier corps: Janissaries (Ottoman), Ghulams (Safavid), samurai (Japan), Cossacks (Russia), Aztec warrior castes.

6. **Decline through similar mechanisms** — Weak leadership, overtaxed peasants, military costs exceeding revenues, religious/ethnic internal conflict.

> [!tip]
> The **conquered often influenced the conquerors**. The Mongols became Buddhist and adopted Chinese administrative practices. The Mughals incorporated Hindu art into Islamic architectural forms. Cultural blending was the norm, not the exception.

## Military Comparison

### Soldiers: Elite Forces

| Empire | Elite Force | Recruitment Method | Loyalty To |
|--------|------------|-------------------|-----------|
| Ottoman | **[[Concept Notes/Janissaries\|Janissaries]]** | Devshirme — Christian boys forcibly recruited | Sultan |
| Safavid | **Ghulams** | Georgian, Armenian, Circassian populations | Shah |
| Aztec | **Warrior castes** | Tribute from conquered states (including human sacrifice) | Emperor |
| Japan | **Samurai** | Hereditary warrior class, salaried by daimyo | Daimyo / Shogun |

Both the Janissaries and Ghulams were recruited from **minority religious or ethnic groups** — outsiders whose upward mobility depended entirely on the empire, ensuring loyalty. The Aztecs similarly used tribute (including people) to sustain their military and religious systems.

> [!warning]
> The **Janissaries and Ghulams** are commonly confused. Remember: **Janissaries = Ottoman Empire** (devshirme, Christian boys from the Balkans). **Ghulams = Safavid Empire** (Georgian, Armenian, Circassian peoples). Both are slave soldiers; both created fierce loyalty by cutting recruits off from their origins.

### Warfare Between Empires

Strong militaries did not prevent conflict *between* empires:
- **Ottoman-Safavid War** — driven by both Sunni-Shi'a religious divide and competition over border territories and trade routes
- **Safavid-Mughal conflict** — primarily about control of resources and trade routes in present-day Afghanistan (religion less central here)
- **Morocco vs. Songhai (1591)** — Morocco invaded and sacked the Songhai capital of Gao, ending the empire; Morocco could not hold all Songhai territory afterward

## Bureaucracy Comparison

Different empires recruited bureaucrats in fundamentally different ways:

| Empire | Bureaucratic Recruitment | Key Feature |
|--------|-------------------------|-------------|
| Ming/Qing China | **Civil service exam** | Merit-based; scholar-gentry class |
| Ottoman Empire | **Devshirme** | Slave-soldiers trained as administrators |
| Safavid Empire | **"Men of the pen"** — Persian scholarly class | Native Persian bureaucrats loyal to shah |
| Songhai | **Scholars from Timbuktu madrasas** | Islamic scholarly class |
| Inca | **Federal system of provinces** | Nobles loyal to emperor; decimal organization |
| Aztec | **Tributary control** — minimal centralization | Ruled by fear and tribute, not direct bureaucracy |

The **Aztec Empire** was notably *less* centralized and bureaucratic than other land empires. It kept control through **force, fear, and intimidation** rather than a governed bureaucracy. This made it vulnerable: when Spanish conquistadors arrived in 1519, Aztec subject peoples joined the Spanish against their oppressors (see [[Unit-4.3/Spanish Empire in the Americas]]).

## Taxation Comparison

| Method | Empire | How It Worked | Long-term Problem |
|--------|--------|--------------|------------------|
| **Zamindar tax collection** | Mughal | Officials collected from peasants based on land/production; kept a portion | Zamindars increasingly skimmed revenue; built private armies |
| **Ottoman tax farming** | Ottoman | "Tax farmers" paid a fixed sum to government; kept excess | Corruption; farmers extracted as much as possible |
| **Aztec tribute lists** | Aztec | Conquered peoples provided yearly tributes (goods, people) | Created deep resentment among subject peoples |
| **Ming "hard currency"** | Ming China | Paper money → hyperinflation → switch to rice then silver | Silver dependency created vulnerability |
| **Inca mita** | Inca | Labor tribute — subjects worked on state projects | Efficient but collapsed under Spanish disruption |

All these systems worked tolerably under strong central leadership — and all eroded under weak or corrupt leadership, as tax collectors kept more and more for themselves.

## Legitimacy Comparison

Rulers used **religion**, **art**, and **architecture** to justify their authority:

| Source of Legitimacy | Examples |
|---------------------|---------|
| **Religion — Islam** | Ottoman caliph as successor to the Prophet; Safavid shah as Shi'a imam; Songhai ruler's conversion and Mecca pilgrimage |
| **Religion — Christianity** | Divine right of kings (James I, Louis XIV); Church as legitimizer (Parliament established in 1265) |
| **Religion — syncretic** | Akbar's Din-i Ilahi; Aztec human sacrifice rituals |
| **Art** | Ottoman miniature paintings; Qing emperor portraits; European royal portraiture |
| **Architecture** | Taj Mahal (Mughal); Suleymaniye Mosque (Ottoman); Palace of Versailles (France); El Escorial (Spain); St. Petersburg Winter Palace (Russia) |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about **monumental architecture as legitimacy**. Know at least three examples across different empires. The Taj Mahal, Suleymaniye Mosque, and Palace of Versailles are the most commonly tested.

## Decline: Common Patterns

All three Islamic Gunpowder Empires declined for overlapping reasons:

1. **Weak or corrupt leadership** after strong founding rulers
2. **Harem politics** in the Ottoman Empire — rival factions promoting different heirs
3. **Military costs** exceeding revenues; peasants overtaxed
4. **Failure to modernize** militaries and navies as European technology advanced
5. **Religious conflict** — Ottoman-Safavid sectarian warfare; Aurangzeb's intolerance triggering Hindu and Sikh revolts
6. **European economic penetration** — British and French trading companies inserted themselves into Mughal India's economy before taking political control

The empires that **did** survive — Russia and China — were those that could modernize (Russia under Peter the Great) or had sufficient geographic size and population to absorb shocks (China).

## The Americas: Aztec and Inca

These empires are often included in Unit 3 comparisons because they operated on the same structural principles — though without gunpowder.

**Aztec (Mexica) Empire:**
- Tributary empire — controlled surrounding peoples through force and fear, not direct governance
- **Tribute lists** demanded local products, labor, and people (for human sacrifice)
- Less bureaucratic than Eurasian empires
- Warrior society — military success was the basis of social mobility
- Religion deeply intertwined with politics: human sacrifice legitimized the ruling class's cosmic role

**Inca Empire:**
- More bureaucratic than Aztec — federal system of provinces with nobles loyal to the emperor
- **Mita** — labor tribute system; subjects owed the state labor on state projects (roads, temples)
- Decimal organization of empire
- **Quipu** — knotted string record-keeping system (no writing)
- Roads and messenger system enabled tight administrative control

> [!tip]
> The AP exam may ask you to **compare Aztec or Inca administration** with one of the Eurasian empires. Key contrast: Aztec = minimal direct control, ruled by fear; Inca = organized bureaucratic control with labor tribute. Both are tribute-based, but the mechanisms differ sharply.

## Full Comparison Table: Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal (1450–1750)

| Dimension | Ottoman | Safavid | Mughal |
|-----------|---------|---------|--------|
| **Religion** | Mostly Sunni; some tolerance under Suleiman | Shi'a; no tolerance; conversion mandatory for Sunnis | Tolerance under Akbar; less tolerance under later rulers |
| **Taxes** | Taxes on non-Muslims; tax farming; taxes on peasants | Taxes to enforce Shi'a adherence; taxes on peasants | Taxes on unbelievers abolished by Akbar; reinstated later |
| **Military** | Janissaries (devshirme) — granted villages for upkeep; strong navy | Qizilbash (Turcoman militants); military independent of central government; no significant navy | Warriors granted villages; zamindars made military independent of central government; small navy |
| **Bureaucracy** | Devshirme-trained officials; dual authority system | "Men of the pen" — Persian scholarly class | Zamindars; civil service under Akbar |
| **Key strength** | Military power, strategic location of Istanbul | Shi'a ideological unity, cultural patronage | Wealth, diverse population, religious tolerance (Akbar) |
| **Key weakness** | Harem politics; naval decline after Lepanto | No navy; no geographic defenses; sectarian extremism | Zamindars' corruption; Aurangzeb's intolerance |

## Continuity and Change

**Continuity**: The structural logic of empire — extract resources through taxation, use them to fund an army, use the army to protect and expand territory, use religion and art to legitimate the whole arrangement — was remarkably consistent across all land-based empires from the Aztec to the Qing.

**Change**: By the end of Period 2, a new kind of empire was emerging — the **maritime empire**, based on naval power and transoceanic trade rather than land control. The Gunpowder Empires, for all their greatness, were not equipped to compete with this new model. Their decline accelerated in Period 3 (Revolutions, Topics 5–6).
