# Key Terms & Concepts — Comparison in Land-Based Empires

These are the core vocabulary items for Topic 3.4. Most appear in comparison or analysis questions on the AP exam — know not just the definition but which empire used each system and **why**. (AMSCO pp. 177–181)

---

**[[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|Gunpowder Empires]]**
A term coined by historian Marshall G. S. Hodgson in the 1950s referring to the large land-based empires of Southwest and South Asia — chiefly the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal — that flourished c. 1450–1750 and whose military power rested on gunpowder weapons, especially large cannons. The term emphasizes their shared technological foundation and is widely used in AP World History. (AMSCO p. 182)

**Devshirme**
The Ottoman "blood tax" or tribute system in which the sultan's agents periodically took boys — usually from Christian communities in the Balkans — converted them to Islam, and trained them for elite military or administrative service. Because devshirme recruits owed everything to the sultan and had no independent tribal or family power base, they became the most loyal servants of the state. See also [[Unit-3.2/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms 3.2]] for administrative context.

> [!exam-tip]
> Devshirme is both a military and bureaucratic tool. Boys could become Janissaries (military) or civil servants (administrative). The key idea: it created loyalty to the sultan rather than to tribal or local leaders.

**Janissaries**
Elite Ottoman infantry soldiers recruited through the devshirme system. Originally enslaved Christian boys from the Balkans, they were converted to Islam and trained from childhood. Over time the Janissaries became a powerful military and political force. Many were compensated through tax farming rather than direct salaries. (AMSCO p. 178)

**Ghulams**
The Safavid equivalent of the Janissaries — enslaved soldiers drawn from the Georgian, Armenian, and Circassian populations within the Safavid Empire. Like the Janissaries, they existed to offset the power of tribal warriors (the Qizilbash) who had their own loyalties separate from the shah. (AMSCO p. 178)

**Qizilbash**
Turcoman (Turkic nomadic) militants who helped the Safavid dynasty establish power in Iran at the start of the sixteenth century. They were fierce fighters but their tribal loyalties made them an unstable power base for the shah — hence the creation of the Ghulam slave-soldiers as a counterbalance. (AMSCO p. 179, comparison table)

**Centralized bureaucracy**
An administrative system in which power flows from a central authority (emperor, sultan, shah) through appointed officials rather than through decentralized nobles, warlords, or tribal leaders. Centralized bureaucracies were essential for governing large, diverse empires. Methods of recruiting bureaucrats varied significantly across empires (civil service exams, devshirme, "men of the pen," provincial noble appointments). (AMSCO p. 179)

**Civil service examination system**
The Chinese merit-based system for selecting government officials, emphasizing mastery of Confucian texts. Used by both the Ming and [[Concept Notes/Manchu\|Manchu]] (Qing) dynasties as the primary method of recruiting the scholar-gentry into government service. Contrasts sharply with the devshirme system in that it was based on intellectual merit rather than enslaved service. See [[Concept Notes/Civil Service Examination|Civil Service Examination]].

**"Men of the pen" (Persian bureaucrats)**
The class of Persian-speaking bureaucrats that the Safavid shahs recruited for civil administration. While the Qizilbash provided military muscle and the Ghulams provided a slave-soldier counterweight, the Persian administrative class — educated and literate — ran the day-to-day machinery of government. (AMSCO p. 179)

**Mansa**
A Mandinka word meaning "sultan" or "king," used in the Songhai Empire to refer to the ruler. The mansa employed educated scholars — trained at the madrasas of Timbuktu — as bureaucrats to administer the empire. (AMSCO p. 179)

**Madrasas**
Islamic schools or colleges that provided religious and scholarly education. In the Songhai Empire, the madrasas of Timbuktu trained the administrative class that staffed the imperial bureaucracy. (AMSCO p. 179)

**Tax farming**
An Ottoman revenue system in which the sultan sold the right to collect taxes in a given region to a private individual (the "tax farmer"). The tax farmer paid a fixed annual sum to the central government upfront, then collected more than that amount from local residents to make a profit. This eliminated the need for a large state tax bureaucracy but created incentives for exploitation. (AMSCO p. 180)

**Zamindars**
Tax officers in the Mughal Empire appointed to collect taxes from the peasant class based on land holdings and agricultural production. Over time the zamindars became a powerful local elite, sometimes making the Mughal military effectively independent of central government control. (AMSCO p. 180)

**Tribute system / tribute lists**
The revenue model of the Aztec (Mexica) Empire. Conquered states submitted annual tribute — goods, products, and sometimes people — to the Aztec ruling class. The tribute lists specified what each region owed. Unlike the Ottoman or Ming taxation systems, the Aztec system involved minimal direct administrative oversight of conquered territories; it was enforced through fear and military intimidation rather than bureaucratic infrastructure. (AMSCO p. 180)

**Hard currency**
Commodity money — typically silver or gold coin — as opposed to paper currency. The Ming Empire switched from paper money (which led to counterfeiting and hyperinflation) to demanding taxes in silver coins. This decision accelerated the global silver trade, as Chinese demand for silver pulled enormous quantities of American silver from the Spanish Empire into China. (AMSCO p. 180)

> [!exam-tip]
> The Ming switch to silver taxation is a crucial connection to Unit 4's coverage of the global silver trade. It is a great example of how domestic policy in one empire had global economic consequences.

**Divine right**
The doctrine, asserted by European monarchs, that their authority to rule came directly from God and was therefore absolute and unchallengeable. It was one of several legitimacy strategies used by rulers to justify authority over diverse populations. Comparable to Islamic rulers' claims to the caliphate. (AMSCO p. 181)

**Caliph / caliphate**
The title "caliph" (Arabic: successor to the Prophet Muhammad) was claimed by Ottoman sultans to legitimize their authority over the world's Muslims. Invoking the caliphate gave the sultan both political and religious authority — a powerful tool when ruling a multiethnic empire. (AMSCO p. 181)

**Monumental architecture**
Large-scale public building projects — palaces, mausoleums, mosques, temples — used by rulers to project power, wealth, and divine favor. Examples from 3.4: the **Taj Mahal** (Mughal mausoleum), the **Palace of Versailles** (French royal palace), and **El Escorial** (Spanish palace-monastery). (AMSCO p. 181)

**Syncretic religion / Din-i-Ilahi**
[[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]] is the blending of different religious traditions. The Mughal Emperor Akbar attempted a syncretic religion called Din-i-Ilahi, drawing on Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, and other traditions, to unite his religiously diverse empire. The attempt had little popular success. See [[Unit-3.3/Empires: Belief Systems Overview|3.3]] for more on religion as a tool of empire.
