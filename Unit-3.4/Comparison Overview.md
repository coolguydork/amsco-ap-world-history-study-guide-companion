# Comparison in Land-Based Empires

> "Foreigners appreciate only military power. . . . Thus, they submit to us wholeheartedly and do not dare to despise China once we display our hunting techniques to them."
> — Qianlong, Emperor of China, 1735

**Essential Question:** By what methods did empires increase their societal and cultural influence from c. 1450–c. 1750?

Topic 3.4 is a pure **comparison topic** — it synthesizes what you learned in [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|3.1]], [[Unit-3.2/Empires: Administration Overview|3.2]], and [[Unit-3.3/Empires: Belief Systems Overview|3.3]] into a single analytical frame. The AP exam will ask you to compare how empires built and maintained power using three main methods: **military might**, **centralized bureaucracy**, and **claims to legitimacy** through religion, art, and architecture. (AMSCO pp. 177–181)

---

## The Big Picture

From c. 1450 to c. 1750, large land-based empires dominated Eurasia and the Americas. These empires grew by incorporating conquered lands, but ruling over diverse populations — with different ethnicities, religions, and tribal loyalties — required more than raw force. Rulers developed sophisticated methods to consolidate control and justify their authority. At the same time, the conquered often shaped the conquerors: blended cultures emerged across all the major empires.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP Learning Objective is narrow and precise: "Compare the **methods** by which various empires **increased their influence** from 1450 to 1750." Focus on the HOW, not just the what. For each method, know 2–3 specific examples from different empires.

## Method 1: Military Might

All major land empires relied on well-trained, well-equipped armies. In Eurasia, **gunpowder weapons** — especially large cannons — supplemented traditional cavalry and infantry. In the Americas, the ferocity of Aztec and Incan warriors allowed them to intimidate and conquer neighbors.

### Elite Soldiers

Several rulers created **loyal, professional military forces** independent of local tribal or noble power:

- **Janissaries** (Ottoman Empire): Elite soldiers recruited through the [[Unit-3.2/Key Terms & Concepts|devshirme]] system — mostly enslaved Christian boys taken as a "blood tax" from the Balkans, converted to Islam, and trained as soldiers loyal to the sultan rather than to local leaders.
- **Ghulams** (Safavid Empire): Enslaved soldiers drawn from Georgian, Armenian, and Circassian populations; served the same function as the Janissaries — offsetting the power of the Qizilbash tribal warriors.
- **Qizilbash**: Turcoman militants who initially helped the Safavids seize power but whose tribal loyalties made the shah wary; the Ghulams were partly created to balance them.

### Warfare Between Empires

Strong militaries did not prevent wars among land empires. Key conflicts:

- **Ottoman–Safavid Wars**: Driven by both territorial disputes along their shared border AND the Sunni–Shi'a religious divide. The Ottomans were predominantly Sunni; the Safavids had made Shi'a Islam the state religion. Each regarded the other as heretics.
- **Safavid–Mughal Conflicts**: Less about religion (both Muslim), more about control over trade routes and territory in present-day Afghanistan.
- **Morocco vs. Songhai (1591)**: Morocco invaded, sacked the capital at Gao, and ended the Songhai Empire — though Morocco could not hold all the territory it conquered.

> [!tip]
> The Ottoman–Safavid conflict is a classic AP comparison: both were Muslim [[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|gunpowder empires]], but the Sunni–Shi'a split made them deadly rivals. This is a great example of **religion deepening political conflict** rather than uniting empires.

## Method 2: Centralized Bureaucracy

Controlling vast, diverse territories required organized administrative systems. How empires recruited bureaucrats differed significantly:

| Empire | Bureaucratic Recruitment Method |
|--------|----------------------------------|
| Ming/Qing China | **Civil service examinations** — tested Confucian knowledge; open (in theory) to male subjects of the scholar-gentry class |
| Ottoman | **Devshirme system** — recruited enslaved Christian boys who became civil servants strictly loyal to the sultan |
| Safavid | **"Men of the pen"** — a bureaucratic class drawn from the Persian-speaking population |
| Songhai | Educated scholars from the **madrasas** of Timbuktu; the ruler was called the **mansa** (Mandinka for "sultan") |
| Inca | **Federal provincial system** — provinces headed by nobles loyal to the emperor; organized on a decimal system |
| Aztec | **Less centralized** — ruled through tribute and intimidation rather than a formal bureaucracy |

See also: [[Concept Notes/Civil Service Examination|Civil Service Examination]]

### Taxation

Every empire needed revenue. Methods varied widely:

- **Ottoman tax farming**: The sultan appointed "tax farmers" who paid a fixed annual sum to the central government, then collected more from residents to turn a profit. Many Janissaries were paid this way.
- **Mughal zamindars**: Tax officers appointed to collect from peasants based on land and agricultural production.
- **Aztec tribute lists**: Conquered states submitted yearly tribute — goods, products, and sometimes people (for sacrifice). There was minimal direct government oversight.
- **Ming hard currency**: After disastrous inflation from paper money, the Ming required taxes paid in **silver coins** — accelerating the global silver trade that would reshape the early modern economy.

## Method 3: Legitimacy — Religion, Art & Architecture

Ruling over diverse peoples meant rulers needed to justify their authority beyond military power. Three main tools:

### Religion
- **Ottoman and Mughal sultans** invoked the title of **caliph** — successor to the Prophet Muhammad — to claim religious as well as political authority.
- **European monarchs** asserted **divine right** — the Christian God had granted them the mandate to rule.
- **Songhai rulers** converted to Islam, giving their empire a religious and legal structure aligned with the broader Islamic world.
- **Aztec rulers** used human sacrifice in religious rituals to reinforce their role as intermediaries between the gods and the people.
- **Akbar (Mughal)** attempted a syncretic new religious movement called Din-i-Ilahi that blended Islam, Hinduism, and other traditions — largely unsuccessful as a state religion. See [[Unit-3.3/Empires: Belief Systems Overview|3.3]] and [[Concept Notes/Syncretism|Syncretism]].

### Art
- Portrait paintings of Qing emperors and officials projected imperial power and dignity.
- Miniature paintings in the Ottoman Empire illustrated manuscripts and glorified the court.
- European rulers financially supported artists (patronage) whose work glorified the monarch.

### Monumental Architecture
- **Taj Mahal** (Mughal): A mausoleum built by Shah Jahan — a statement of imperial wealth, artistic achievement, and Islamic piety all at once.
- **Mosques** throughout the Mughal Empire projected Islamic identity and the ruler's piety.
- **Palace of Versailles** (France): Louis XIV's palace as the ultimate symbol of absolute monarchical power.
- **El Escorial** (Spain): Philip II's palace-monastery — merging royal and religious authority in stone.

> [!exam-tip]
> Monumental architecture is one of the most exam-tested forms of legitimacy. Know at least two examples from different regions, and be able to explain **why** a ruler built a structure, not just **what** it was.

## Why Empires Declined

The same factors that built empires could also destroy them. Common decline factors across land-based empires include:

- **Military stagnation**: Failure to keep up with European innovations in military and naval technology (William McNeill's thesis).
- **Harem politics / succession crises**: Competing princes and court factions destabilized succession.
- **Religious conflict**: Sunni–Shi'a split weakened Ottoman–Safavid relations; Hindu–Muslim tensions in Mughal India; Safavid theocratic ideology eroding.
- **Financial overextension**: Expensive armies, tax burdens on peasants, and declining agricultural revenues.
- **Intolerance**: Amy Chua's argument — empires that became less tolerant of minority religions and ethnicities lost their competitive edge and the loyalty of diverse subjects.

## Russia as a Case Study: Peter the Great

Russia under **Peter the Great (r. 1682–1725)** illustrates how a land empire could adapt to survive. Peter modernized the Russian military, introduced Western dress codes, mandated mathematics education, and invited foreign craftsmen to develop Russian manufacturing. His reforms were top-down, state-directed, and often coercive — but they kept Russia competitive as European military technology raced ahead. (AMSCO pp. 185–188, primary source documents)
