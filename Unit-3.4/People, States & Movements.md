# People, States & Movements — Comparison in Land-Based Empires

Topic 3.4 is comparative by design — the AP exam expects you to know key rulers and empires across multiple regions and to explain their methods side by side. (AMSCO pp. 177–188)

---

## Key Rulers

**Suleiman I ("the Magnificent"), r. 1520–1566 — Ottoman Empire**
The Ottoman Empire reached its greatest territorial and military height under Suleiman. He led campaigns into Europe, including the siege of Vienna (1529), and maintained the most technologically advanced army in the world at his time. AMSCO references his siege of Esztergom in present-day Hungary (with French artillery), illustrating how even rival powers cooperated with the Ottomans against common enemies. See [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|3.1]] and [[Unit-3.2/People, States & Movements|3.2 People]].

**Akbar ("the Great"), r. 1556–1605 — Mughal Empire**
Akbar is the most-tested Mughal emperor on the AP exam. He is notable for his policy of religious tolerance — he abolished the jizya (tax on non-Muslims), welcomed Hindu officials into his administration, and attempted the syncretic Din-i-Ilahi religious movement. However, his successors reversed many of these policies, and the reimposition of the jizya by Aurangzeb later deepened Hindu–Muslim tensions. See [[Unit-3.3/People, States & Movements|3.3 People]].

> [!exam-tip]
> Akbar's tolerance vs. Aurangzeb's intolerance is a classic AP comparison within the Mughal Empire. Historian Amy Chua argues that tolerance was a key factor in imperial longevity — Akbar's reign supports this thesis.

**Peter the Great (Peter I), r. 1682–1725 — Russian Empire**
Peter modernized Russia through top-down, state-directed reforms: he reorganized and re-equipped the military along European lines, mandated Western dress at court (Decree on Western Dress, 1701), invited foreign craftsmen and experts, founded the College of Manufactures to develop Russian industry, and mandated mathematics education in the provinces. His reforms show how a land empire could adapt to survive in an era of rapid European military advancement. Primary source documents on pp. 186–188 of AMSCO.

**Qianlong Emperor, r. 1735–1796 — Qing (Manchu) Dynasty**
Quoted at the opening of the chapter: "Foreigners appreciate only military power." His statement captures the Qing approach to empire — projecting strength and demanding submission. The Qing expanded Qing territory significantly and used portrait paintings of the emperor as tools of legitimacy and cultural projection.

**Suleiman I vs. Akbar — A Side-by-Side**

| Dimension | Suleiman I (Ottoman) | Akbar (Mughal) |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------|
| Religious policy | Sunni Islam as state religion; non-Muslim taxes (jizya) | Initially tolerant; abolished jizya; tried Din-i-Ilahi |
| Bureaucracy | Devshirme + tax farming | Zamindars; Hindu officials included |
| Military | Janissaries; strong navy | Zamindars made military semi-independent; small navy |
| Legitimacy | Caliph title; mosques; miniature painting | Syncretic religion; Taj Mahal (his grandson's project) |
| Decline factor | Harem politics; military stagnation after c. 1600 | Aurangzeb reversed tolerant policies → religious conflict |

## Key States & Empires

**Ottoman Empire (c. 1299–1922)**
The longest-lived of the Gunpowder Empires. Centered in Anatolia (present-day Turkey), it controlled the eastern Mediterranean, North Africa, and southeastern Europe at its height. Key features for 3.4: Janissaries, devshirme, tax farming, the caliphate title, miniature paintings, and the Sunni–Shi'a conflict with the Safavids. See [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|3.1]] and [[Unit-3.2/Empires: Administration Overview|3.2]].

**Safavid Empire (1501–1722)**
Founded by Shah Ismail I; made Shi'a Islam the state religion — a defining choice that set the Safavids apart from (and at war with) the Sunni Ottoman Empire. Key features for 3.4: Ghulams, Qizilbash, "men of the pen" bureaucrats. Declined due to military weakness, theocratic ideological decay, and inability to modernize. See [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|3.1]].

**Mughal Empire (1526–1857)**
A Muslim-ruled empire over a majority-Hindu population in South Asia. Key features for 3.4: zamindars, tribute from conquered states, tensions between tolerance (Akbar) and intolerance (Aurangzeb), the Taj Mahal as legitimacy architecture. See [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|3.1]], [[Unit-3.3/Empires: Belief Systems Overview|3.3]].

**Ming/Qing China**
Both dynasties used the civil service examination system, though the Manchu Qing imposed ethnic restrictions. Key feature for 3.4: civil service exams as bureaucratic recruitment; the Ming shift to silver taxation as an economic turning point. See [[Concept Notes/Civil Service Examination|Civil Service Examination]], [[Unit-3.2/Empires: Administration Overview|3.2]].

**Russian Empire (under Peter the Great)**
Russia was a land-based empire that modernized dramatically in the early 1700s by adopting European military and administrative models. Key for 3.4: Peter's reforms as a case study in how empires adapt to external pressure. (AMSCO pp. 185–188)

**Songhai Empire (c. 1464–1591)**
West African empire centered on the Niger River bend; ruled by a mansa; administered by scholars educated at Timbuktu's madrasas. Fell when Morocco invaded in 1591 and sacked the capital of Gao. The Moroccan invasion demonstrates that even a powerful land empire could be destroyed by external military force.

**Aztec (Mexica) Empire (c. 1345–1521)**
A less-centralized tributary empire in Mesoamerica. Controlled neighboring states through fear and tribute demands rather than direct bureaucratic administration. Human sacrifice served as a religious legitimacy tool. Fell to Spanish conquest in 1521. Key AP comparison: the Aztec tribute model vs. the Ottoman bureaucratic-devshirme model — both extracted resources from conquered peoples but through very different mechanisms.

**Inca Empire (c. 1438–1533)**
South American empire organized into a federal system of provinces headed by nobles loyal to the emperor, with administration based on a decimal system. Had no writing system; used quipus (knotted strings) for record-keeping. Fell to Spanish conquest in 1533.

## Movements

**Devshirme recruits as a social movement**
The devshirme was not just an administrative policy — it created a new social class. Boys taken from Christian communities could rise to become grand viziers (the sultan's chief ministers) or military commanders. This upward mobility was real, even though it was rooted in enslaved service, and it gave the system social legitimacy.

**Religious schism as a political force**
The Sunni–Shi'a split was not just theological — it was a geopolitical fault line that structured warfare and diplomacy across Southwest Asia for centuries. The Ottoman–Safavid Wars were fought along this divide. Understanding the schism is essential for explaining Ottoman and Safavid foreign policy. See [[Unit-3.3/Empires: Belief Systems Overview|3.3]].
