# Unit 3.4: Key Terms for Land-Based Empire Comparison

#apwh #unit-3 #period-2

## Vocabulary for Comparative Analysis

These terms are the analytical vocabulary for comparing the six major land-based empires. Knowing these terms — and being able to apply them to specific empires — is essential for AP MCQ, SAQ, and LEQ responses.

---

**Devshirme** — The Ottoman practice of recruiting young boys from Christian families in conquered territories, converting them to Islam, educating them, and training them as soldiers (*janissaries*) or administrators. *(Significant because it created a loyal administrative and military class with no independent power base — their status depended entirely on the sultan — and it allowed talented individuals from non-Muslim backgrounds to reach the highest levels of Ottoman government. It is frequently compared to the Chinese civil service exam as a meritocratic selection system.)*

**Mansabdar** — An official in the Mughal Empire who held a ranked position (*mansab*) in the imperial hierarchy, combining civil and military responsibilities, and received a salary through a land grant (*jagir*) rather than a fixed wage. *(Significant because the mansabdar system was the Mughal's primary tool for integrating diverse regional elites — including Hindu Rajput chiefs — into the imperial administration, making the empire function as a coalition rather than a conquest state.)*

**Dyarchy** — The Qing Dynasty's system of pairing Manchu and Chinese officials in government positions to ensure Manchu control while retaining Chinese administrative expertise. *(Significant because it shows how the Qing — a foreign (Manchu) ruling dynasty over a Chinese majority — solved the problem of governing a population that vastly outnumbered them. It is a key comparison point with the Mughal strategy of incorporating Hindu elites.)*

**Millet System** — The Ottoman practice of organizing non-Muslim minority communities (Greek Orthodox Christians, Armenian Christians, Jews) as self-governing units (*millets*) under their own religious leaders, who were responsible for collecting taxes and maintaining internal order. *(Significant because it allowed the Ottoman Empire to govern enormous religious diversity without requiring conversion, and it provided a degree of cultural autonomy that was unusual in the period. Compare to Akbar's policy of religious tolerance in the Mughal Empire.)*

**Jizya** — A tax levied on non-Muslims (dhimmis) living under Islamic rule, historically justified as payment for military protection and the right to practice their faith. *(Significant because the imposition or removal of the jizya is one of the clearest indicators of an empire's policy toward religious minorities: Akbar abolished it, signaling tolerance; Aurangzeb reimposed it, signaling stricter Islamic enforcement. The AP exam frequently uses jizya as evidence in questions about religious policy.)*

**Tax Farming** — A revenue collection system in which the government sells the right to collect taxes in a given area to a private individual (tax farmer), who pays the government upfront and then collects taxes from the population, keeping any surplus as profit. *(Significant because tax farming was used by the Ottoman, Safavid, and other empires as a way to generate immediate revenue without building a full bureaucracy — but it led to abuse and uneven collection, often burdening the peasantry.)*

**Bureaucracy** — A system of government administration carried out by appointed officials organized in a hierarchical structure with defined roles and responsibilities. *(Significant in this context because the degree of bureaucratic development varied significantly across the six empires — the Qing retained the sophisticated Chinese civil service bureaucracy, while the Songhai relied more on personal loyalty networks and Islamic scholars as administrators.)*

**Gunpowder Empire** — A term used by historians to describe the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires (and sometimes extended to include the Qing and Russian), emphasizing how access to gunpowder weapons — especially cannons — gave these states a decisive military advantage that enabled their rapid territorial expansion. *(Significant because it links military technology to state formation, showing that the wave of imperial expansion in this period had a common technological cause — though it can oversimplify by underemphasizing administrative and economic factors.)*

**Legitimation** — The process by which a ruler or government establishes its right to rule as accepted and valid by its subjects, typically through appeals to religion, tradition, personal charisma, military success, or legal authority. *(Significant because all six empires faced legitimation challenges — the Manchu Qing ruling over Chinese, the Mughal Muslims ruling over Hindus, the Ottoman sultans claiming both secular and religious authority — and their different solutions reveal much about each empire's political culture.)*

**Syncretic** — Describing a belief system, culture, or political practice that blends elements from two or more distinct traditions into a new, hybrid form. *(Significant because syncretism was a political tool as much as a cultural phenomenon: Akbar's Din-i-Ilahi religion, the Songhai court's blend of Islam and indigenous African traditions, and the Qing use of Confucian and Tibetan Buddhist symbols all represent strategic syncretism to appeal to multiple subject populations.)*

**Tributary** — Describing a state or people that pays regular tribute (goods, money, or labor) to a more powerful polity as a condition of maintaining autonomy or receiving protection. *(Significant because the Qing tributary system — inherited from the Ming — organized China's relations with surrounding states and defined the East Asian international order. Compare with the Ottoman millet system as different solutions to the problem of managing subordinate communities.)*

**Patrimonial State** — A form of government in which the ruler treats the state as personal property, distributing power and resources to loyal followers (the ruler's "household") rather than through impersonal bureaucratic institutions. *(Significant because many of the empires in this period combined patrimonial elements — personal loyalty to the ruler — with bureaucratic elements. The Safavid and Songhai empires were more patrimonial; the Qing and Ottoman were more bureaucratic.)*

**Absolutism** — A form of government in which the ruler claims unlimited, unchecked authority over all aspects of political, economic, and social life, typically justified by divine right or religious mandate. *(Significant because while European monarchs like Louis XIV are the textbook examples of absolutism, the concept applies equally to the Ottoman sultan, the Mughal emperor, and the Russian tsar — and the AP exam may ask you to compare these "absolutist" rulers across civilizations.)*

**Tribute** — Goods, money, labor, or other valuables paid by a subordinate group to a dominant power as a condition of political subordination, protection, or trade access. *(Significant because tribute was a universal feature of empire in this period — virtually all six empires collected tribute from conquered peoples, though the form varied: the Qing collected symbolic tribute from East Asian neighbors, the Mughal collected revenue tribute through the mansabdar system, and the Songhai collected tribute in gold and agricultural products.)*

**Cosmopolitan Court** — A royal court that attracts artists, scholars, merchants, and officials from diverse ethnic, linguistic, and religious backgrounds, serving as a center of cultural production and political legitimation. *(Significant because most of the six empires maintained cosmopolitan courts as a deliberate tool of soft power: the Mughal court at Agra attracted Hindu Rajput nobles, Persian poets, and European Jesuit missionaries; the Ottoman court at Constantinople drew Greek scholars, Venetian artists, and Jewish physicians; the Qing court employed Jesuit astronomers. Cosmopolitanism signaled imperial confidence and breadth.)*

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently pairs **devshirme** (Ottoman) with the **civil service exam** (Qing/Chinese) as two different meritocratic selection systems. Know the key difference: devshirme selected from conquered non-Muslim populations and required conversion; the civil service exam was open to male subjects and tested mastery of Confucian classics.

> [!warning]
> Do not use "gunpowder empire" as if it fully explains these empires. The AP exam rewards students who go beyond the label to explain **specific** administrative, economic, and cultural factors. Gunpowder is a cause of expansion; devshirme, the millet system, and the mansabdar system explain how empires **maintained** control after conquest.
