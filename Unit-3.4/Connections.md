## Compare With — Cross-Topic Analysis

**3.1 vs. 4.4 — Land empire vs. maritime empire methods**
The Gunpowder Empires used armies and territorial control; European maritime empires used navies, trading companies, and colonial extraction. Both built bureaucracies, but their geographic and technological basis differed fundamentally.

**Janissaries vs. Ghulams — Internal comparison within 3.4**
Both were enslaved soldiers used to offset tribal power and create loyal military forces directly answerable to the ruler. The key difference: Janissaries were drawn from Christian communities in the Ottoman Balkans via devshirme; Ghulams were drawn from Christian and other minority communities within the Safavid Empire itself. Both systems reflect the same structural logic applied to different demographic realities.

**Aztec tribute model vs. Ottoman devshirme model**
Both extracted resources (goods, labor, people) from conquered territories. But the Ottoman system created loyal state servants who strengthened central authority; the Aztec tribute model extracted resources without integrating conquered peoples into a unified administrative structure, leaving the empire vulnerable to internal revolt (which Cortés exploited in 1519–1521).

## AP Threads Running Through 3.4

**Comparative empire-building**
The explicit skill of Topic 3.4 — comparing how empires used military might, bureaucracy, and legitimacy across different cultural and geographic settings.

**Centralization patterns**
All land empires sought to reduce the power of local nobles, tribal leaders, and regional governors relative to the central authority. Methods differed (devshirme, civil service exams, zamindars) but the goal was the same.

**Religion and empire**
Religion was simultaneously a tool of legitimacy (caliphate, divine right, Aztec sacrifice) and a source of conflict (Sunni–Shi'a wars, Hindu–Muslim tensions in Mughal India). See [[Unit-3.3/Connections|3.3 Connections]].

**Military technology and imperial power**
Gunpowder technology was the equalizer that allowed the Gunpowder Empires to rise; falling behind in military technology was a key factor in their decline (McNeill thesis). Peter the Great's reforms demonstrate the strategic imperative to modernize.
