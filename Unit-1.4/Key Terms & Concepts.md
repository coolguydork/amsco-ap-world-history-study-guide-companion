# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.4

*AMSCO pp. 33–42 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## North American Terms

**Mississippian culture**
First large-scale civilization in North America; emerged in the Mississippi River Valley, c. 700s–800s CE. Known for building enormous earthen mounds. Peak urban center: Cahokia (southern Illinois). Abandoned Cahokia by ~1450; declined fully by 1600. (AMSCO p. 33)

**Cahokia**
Largest Mississippian mound site, in present-day southern Illinois. At its peak (c. 1100 CE), likely housed 10,000–20,000 people — comparable to many contemporary European cities. The central mound covered the area of 12 football fields and rose 100 feet. (AMSCO p. 33)

**Matrilineal society**
A society in which social status and inheritance pass through the mother's line. The Mississippian Great Sun's title passed to a sister's son, not to his own son. Contrasts with patrilineal societies (common in Eurasia). (AMSCO p. 33)

**Chaco and Mesa Verde**
Two Southwest cultures known for innovative architecture in arid terrain. The Chaco built large multi-room stone-and-clay structures; Mesa Verde people carved multi-story cliff dwellings into sandstone cliffs. Both declined in the late 13th century as the climate dried. (AMSCO p. 34)

## Maya Terms

**Maya city-states**
Political units of Mayan civilization — each an independent city ruled by a divine king, with no overarching central government. Wars were fought for tribute and sacrifice captives, not territorial control. Approximately 40 cities existed at peak, ranging 5,000–50,000 in population. (AMSCO pp. 34–35)

**Theocracy**
Government ruled by religious leaders or in which the ruler is considered divine. Both the Maya and the Aztec were theocracies — kings claimed divine descent and justified their authority through religious roles. The Aztec emperor was the "Great Speaker," divine representative of the gods. (AMSCO p. 36)

**Human sacrifice**
Practice in multiple Mesoamerican civilizations. In Maya: war captives were sacrificed during religious ceremonies. In Aztec: driven by the belief that the gods had sacrificed themselves to create the world — human sacrifice was a cosmic debt repayment. Scale is debated; Spanish accounts may exaggerate the extent. (AMSCO pp. 35, 37)

## Aztec Terms

**Mexica**
The name the Aztecs used for themselves. "Aztec" is a later scholarly term derived from their legendary homeland of Aztlan. (AMSCO p. 35)

**Tenochtitlan**
Aztec capital city, founded 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco (modern Mexico City). Population reached ~200,000 — one of the largest cities in the world. Featured aqueducts, a 150-foot pyramid, stone temples, and chinampas. (AMSCO p. 35)

**Chinampas**
"Floating gardens" — artificial agricultural plots created by the Aztec in Lake Texcoco by layering mud and vegetation. Dramatically expanded the amount of arable land available in a lake environment. An innovative response to geographic constraint. (AMSCO p. 35)

**Tribute system (Aztec)**
The mechanism by which the Aztec Empire maintained dominance. Conquered peoples paid tribute in food, cloth, firewood, feathers, and luxury items. Local rulers remained in place as tribute collectors; Aztec warriors and officials were stationed in provincial capitals. Bred deep resentment that contributed to the empire's vulnerability to Spanish conquest. (AMSCO p. 36)

**Pochteca**
The Aztec merchant class that specialized in long-distance trade of luxury goods. Occupied a distinct social position between craftspeople and the nobility. Demonstrates the complexity of Aztec social organization beyond a simple two-tier elite/peasant model. (AMSCO p. 36)

## Inca Terms

**Pachacuti** (r. c. 1438–1471)
The 9th Sapa Inca (emperor) who dramatically expanded the Inca Empire. His name means "Transformer" or "Shaker of the Earth." Military victories under him and his son created the empire that stretched from Ecuador to Chile. Key person to know for the AP exam. (AMSCO p. 37)

**Tawantinsuyu**
The Inca name for their empire, meaning "The Four Regions" — reflecting the empire's division into four administrative provinces. Emphasizes the Inca's sophisticated approach to governing vast, diverse territory. (AMSCO p. 37)

**Mit'a system**
The Inca labor tax — mandatory public service rather than payment of goods. Men aged 15–50 owed the state agricultural labor, construction work (roads, bridges, buildings), and military service. Distinct from the Aztec tribute system — labor instead of goods. Continued under Spanish colonial rule (an important continuity for [[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview|Topic 4.4]]). (AMSCO p. 37)

**Quipu**
System of knotted strings used by the Inca for recording numerical data and transmitting messages across the empire. The only Andean record-keeping technology — the Inca had no written script. Demonstrates that complex civilizations can develop sophisticated administration without alphabetic writing. (AMSCO p. 38)

**Carpa Nan**
The Inca road system — approximately 25,000 miles of roads traversing mountains, deserts, and rainforests. Used primarily by government officials and military forces (not commercial trade). Made communication and troop movement possible across the largest empire in the Americas. (AMSCO p. 38)

**Waru waru**
Inca terrace-farming technique — raised agricultural beds with drainage channels that captured rainwater, reduced erosion during floods, and stored water for dry periods. Allowed cultivation of crops like potatoes and maize in steep Andean terrain. (AMSCO p. 38)

**Animism**
Belief that elements of the physical world (rivers, mountains, stones, built objects) can have supernatural powers. Practiced by the Inca, who called these sacred objects *huaca*. A pre-existing religious framework that existed alongside and intertwined with state religion (Inti worship). (AMSCO p. 38)

**Royal ancestor veneration**
Inca practice of mummifying dead rulers, who continued to "rule" and retained their property and servants in death. Because new rulers inherited nothing, they were structurally pressured to conquer new territory — a built-in driver of imperial expansion. (AMSCO p. 38)

> [!exam-tip]
> **Mit'a vs. Tribute** is a high-frequency AP comparison. Mit'a = labor owed to the state (Inca). Tribute = goods owed to the conqueror (Aztec). Both are coercive labor/extraction systems, but they produce different relationships between rulers and ruled. The Aztec tribute system bred more resentment; the Inca mit'a was more integrated into the state's welfare system (roads, storehouses, food redistribution).

> [!warning]
> Don't say the Inca had "no writing." They had no **alphabetic script** — but quipu served recording functions. The distinction matters for AP arguments about communication, record-keeping, and state complexity.
