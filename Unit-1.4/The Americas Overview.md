# Topic 1.4 — Developments in the Americas

*AMSCO pp. 33–42 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

> [!exam-tip]
> The Americas were entirely isolated from Afro-Eurasia during this period — no iron tools, no horses, no wheeled vehicles, no contact with other continents until 1492. The AP exam loves asking you to **compare** American civilizations with those elsewhere: how did they achieve complexity without these technologies? Use the Inca and Aztec as your primary examples, with the Maya as supporting detail.

## Big Picture

When European explorers arrived in the late 15th century, they encountered civilizations as sophisticated as any in the world. The Americas hosted a remarkable diversity of societies — from the mound-builders of North America to the towering city-states of Mesoamerica to the mountain empire of the Andes. What unites them is that they achieved complexity **without iron, the wheel, or contact with Afro-Eurasia**, making them a critical test case for how and why states develop.

The key civilizations for the AP exam are the **Maya** (city-states, Mesoamerica), the **Aztec/Triple Alliance** (empire, central Mexico), and the **Inca/Tawantinsuyu** (empire, Andes). North American cultures — the Mississippian, Chaco, and Mesa Verde — appear occasionally on the exam but in less depth.

## North America: Mississippian Culture

The first large-scale civilization in North America emerged in the 700s–800s CE in the Mississippi River Valley. Mississippians built enormous **earthen mounds** — some as tall as 100 feet and covering the area of 12 football fields. The largest was **Cahokia**, located in southern Illinois, which was a major urban center by c. 1100 CE.

**Society:** A rigid class structure — a ruler called the **Great Sun**, beneath him priests and nobles, then farmers and artisans, and enslaved people (usually prisoners of war) at the bottom. The Mississippian system was **matrilineal**: social standing and succession passed through the woman's line (the Great Sun's title passed to a sister's son, not his own son).

**Decline:** Cahokia was abandoned by about **1450** (within AP Period 1) and other Mississippian cities by 1600. Probable causes include flooding, crop failures, and later European-introduced diseases — historians still debate the primary driver.

**Chaco and Mesa Verde:** Two Southwest cultures known for architectural innovation in an arid climate. The Chaco built large stone-and-clay housing complexes; Mesa Verde people carved multi-story cliff dwellings out of sandstone. Both declined in the late 13th century as the climate became drier.

> [!warning]
> Students often skip North American cultures entirely. While they're tested less than Maya/Aztec/Inca, Cahokia's scale (comparable to contemporary European cities) and the matrilineal succession pattern are favorite AP points.

## Mesoamerica: The Maya

Mayan civilization peaked between 250–900 CE (the Classic Period), but **Maya city-states continued to exist through and beyond our AP period (1200–1450)**. At peak, the Maya occupied southern Mexico, Belize, Honduras, and Guatemala, with roughly 40 cities of 5,000–50,000 people.

**Government:** The core political unit was the **city-state** — each ruled by a king (considered divine), comprising a city and its surrounding territory. No central government unified all Maya. Wars between city-states were fought primarily for **tribute** and **captives for human sacrifice**, not territorial control. This sets the Maya apart from both the Aztec and Inca empires.

**Religion, Science & Technology:**
- Incorporated the concept of **zero** into their number system
- Developed a complex **hieroglyphic writing system** (one of the few in the Americas)
- Built **astronomical observatories** (e.g., at Chichen Itza) to create a calendar more accurate than those used in Europe at the time
- **Step pyramids** served as religious temples
- Both men and women could serve as priests; priests conducted astronomical observations, designed the calendar, and oversaw religious ceremonies

**Decline:** Drought, deforestation, and eventual European diseases contributed. The Maya never fully "collapsed" — millions of Maya-descended people live in the region today.

> [!tip]
> Compare Maya city-states with Greek poleis: both are decentralized political systems where culturally unified peoples fight each other for dominance while sharing religion, language, and cultural practices. Neither built a lasting empire over all their people.

## Mesoamerica: The Aztec (Triple Alliance)

The **Aztecs** (also called the Mexica) were originally hunter-gatherers from northern Mexico. They migrated south in the 1200s and founded their capital **Tenochtitlan** in 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco — where Mexico City stands today. Over the following century they conquered surrounding peoples and built an empire stretching from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific.

**Capital and Technology:** Tenochtitlan grew to nearly **200,000 people** — one of the largest cities in the world. Key engineering feats: a network of **aqueducts** for fresh water; a massive stone pyramid (150 feet tall) at the city center; **chinampas** (floating gardens on the lake) that dramatically expanded agricultural land.

**Government:** A **theocracy** — the emperor (called the Great Speaker) was simultaneously political ruler and divine representative of the gods. Social hierarchy: emperor → land-owning nobles (also military leaders) → scribes and healers → craftspeople → **pochteca** (specialized merchant class trading in luxury goods) → peasants and soldiers → enslaved people.

**Empire maintenance:** The Aztec used a **tribute system** — conquered peoples paid tribute in food, cloth, firewood, feathers, and luxury goods. Local rulers were left in place as tribute collectors. Aztec warriors and officials were stationed in provincial capitals. This system was effective but bred deep resentment among subject peoples.

**Religion:** Polytheistic, with hundreds of deities. The Aztecs believed the gods had sacrificed themselves to create the world, so **human sacrifice** was a form of cosmic debt repayment. The scale of sacrifice is debated — Spanish accounts may be exaggerated to justify conquest. Human sacrifice also served a political function, demonstrating Aztec power to subject peoples.

**Decline:** By the late 15th century, the empire was overextended, technologically limited (no wheeled vehicles, no iron, no pack animals beyond dogs), and alienating subject peoples through tribute extraction. When Spanish forces arrived in 1519, many subject peoples allied with them to overthrow Aztec rule.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Aztec tribute system is the defining exam concept for this empire. Know: (1) what tribute was, (2) how it was collected, (3) why it bred resentment, and (4) how that resentment contributed to the empire's fall when the Spanish arrived.

## South America: The Inca (Tawantinsuyu)

In 1438, a leader named **Pachacuti** ("Transformer of the Earth") began conquering neighboring tribes near Cuzco, Peru. Within a generation, his military victories and those of his son expanded the **Incan Empire (Tawantinsuyu)** from Ecuador to Chile — the largest empire in the Americas and, by land area, one of the largest in the world.

**Government:** Four provinces, each with its own governor and bureaucracy. The Inca used a **mit'a system** — mandatory public service (labor tax), not tribute. Men aged 15–50 provided agricultural labor, construction, and military service. Conquered local leaders who showed loyalty were rewarded and kept in their positions.

**Religion:** "Inca" means "people of the sun"; the **sun god Inti** was paramount. Rulers were considered Inti's representative on earth. Royal **ancestor veneration** was central — dead rulers were mummified and continued to "rule," retaining their property and servants. This meant new rulers had no inheritance and were pushed to conquer new territory to build their own wealth — a structural driver of imperial expansion.

**Achievements:**
- **Quipu**: knotted string system for recording numerical data and messages — the only Andean record-keeping technology (no writing system)
- **Terrace farming** (*waru waru*): sophisticated terraced agriculture with drainage channels and water storage; enabled cultivation of potatoes, maize, and cotton in mountainous terrain
- **Carpa Nan**: 25,000-mile road network enabling rapid movement of armies, officials, and goods across diverse terrain
- Expert **masonry** and **bridge-building** across Andean valleys

**Decline:** Spanish conquistador Francisco Pizarro arrived in 1532 during a devastating civil war of succession. The Inca fell to the Spanish in 1533, though outposts held out until 1572.

> [!tip]
> **Aztec vs. Inca:** The Aztec collected tribute (goods) and left local rulers in place; the Inca collected labor through mit'a and built a centralized bureaucracy with governors. The Aztec bred more resentment; the Inca built more integration. Both fell to the Spanish — but for different structural reasons.

## Civilizational Continuities

AMSCO raises an important historiographical debate: were Mesoamerican civilizations connected through the earlier **Olmec** civilization, or did they develop more independently? Evidence for connection: the feathered serpent god appears in Olmec, Maya, and Aztec religion; Olmec ritual practices (pyramids, ball courts, human sacrifice) appear across later cultures. Evidence against: some cultures may have developed these features independently. This debate matters for AP because it's about **causation and continuity in historical analysis**.
