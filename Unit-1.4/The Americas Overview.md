# Topic 1.4 — Developments in the Americas

> [!summary] Essential Question
> What **states developed in the Americas**, and how did they **change over time**?

## Context: Parallel Development, Separate World

The Americas developed complex civilizations **entirely independently** of Afro-Eurasia. No contact existed between these worlds until European arrival in 1492. The civilizations of Mesoamerica and the Andes show that state formation, urbanization, religious complexity, and social hierarchy can emerge through many different paths.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Americas are a favorite AP comparison topic precisely because they show **parallel development**: similar patterns of state-building (tribute, hierarchy, religious legitimacy, monumental architecture) appearing across vastly separated regions without direct contact.

---

## North America: Mississippian Culture

The **Mississippian Culture** emerged in the 700s–800s in what is now the eastern United States (Mississippi River Valley). It represents the **first large-scale civilization in North America**.

### Government and Society

- A chief called the **Great Sun** ruled each large town
- Rigid class structure: Great Sun → priests and nobles → farmers, hunters, merchants, artisans → slaves (usually prisoners of war)
- **Matrilineal society**: social standing determined by the mother's side — when the Great Sun died, the title passed to *a sister's son*, not his own son
- Women farmed; men hunted

### Architecture

Mississippians built **enormous earthen mounds**, some reaching 100 feet tall and covering 12 football fields. The largest is **Cahokia** (southern Illinois). Comparable in function to pyramids: centers of ritual and power.

### Decline

People abandoned Cahokia around **1450** and other large cities by 1600. Theories: flooding/climate change caused crop failure; European-introduced diseases decimated the population.

## Southwest Cultures: Chaco and Mesa Verde

Two cultures in what is now the southwestern United States developed innovative building techniques to survive in an arid region:

- **Chaco**: built large multi-room stone and clay housing structures
- **Mesa Verde**: built multi-story homes carved into cliff sides using sandstone bricks

Both declined in the **late 13th century** as climate became drier — an early example of **environmental factors shaping civilization**.

## Mesoamerica: The Maya

**Mayan civilization** reached its height between **250 and 900 CE**, then declined before experiencing a partial revival. Mayans spread across southern Mexico, Belize, Honduras, and Guatemala.

### Government: City-States

Mayan government was organized as **city-states** — each ruled by a king (usually male, but women ruled when no male heir was available or mature enough). Key features:

- Kings claimed descent from gods
- Wars between city-states were common, but fought primarily for **tribute** and war **captives** (for sacrifice) — not for territory
- No central government ruled all Maya; the strongest city-state would dominate neighbors
- Common people paid taxes (in crops) and provided military service

### Religion, Science, and Technology

- **Astronomy** linked to religion: priests used astronomical observations to design the religious calendar (more accurate than any European calendar at the time) and decide when to go to war
- Priests could be male or female; they conducted ceremonies honoring deities of sun, rain, and corn
- Human sacrifice was practiced (war captives offered to gods)
- Developed **zero** in their number system
- Complex **writing system**
- Learned to make **rubber**

### Decline

Scholars cite drought, deforestation, and Spanish attacks (1500s) as causes of collapse.

## Mesoamerica: The Aztecs (Mexica)

The **Aztecs** were originally hunter-gatherers who migrated to central Mexico from the north in the **1200s**. In **1325**, they founded their capital **Tenochtitlan** on an island in a swampy lake (site of modern Mexico City). Over the next 100 years, they built an empire stretching from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific Ocean.

### Capital City: Tenochtitlan

- Population of nearly **200,000** — one of the largest cities in the world
- Aqueducts provided fresh water
- A **Great Pyramid** rose 150 feet at the city's center
- **Chinampas** — floating gardens on Lake Texcoco — expanded agricultural land
- Ditches irrigated fields and drained parts of the lake

### Government, Economy, and Society

**Tribute system**: Conquered peoples were forced to pay tribute (food, cloth, firewood, luxury goods). Local rulers kept their positions as tribute collectors. This allowed **political dominance without direct administrative control**.

**Social hierarchy**: Emperor (Great Speaker, divine representative of gods) → land-owning nobles (also military leaders) → scribes and healers → craftspeople and traders → **pochteca** (special merchant class trading in luxury goods) → peasants and soldiers → enslaved people (those who didn't pay debts or were punished for crimes; also sacrifice victims).

**Government structure**: Aztec government was a **theocracy** — rule by religious leaders. The emperor was both political ruler and divine representative.

### Religion

- Polytheistic pantheon of hundreds of deities, many with both male and female aspects
- **Human sacrifice** was central: the gods had sacrificed themselves to create the world, so humans owed repayment through sacrifice and bloodletting
- Human sacrifice also served a political function — demonstrating Aztec imperial power

> [!warning]
> AP sources about Aztec sacrifice often come from Spanish invaders, who had an obvious motive to portray the Aztecs as barbaric and deserving of conquest. Be suspicious of sources that rely heavily on Spanish accounts for the *extent* of human sacrifice.

### Role of Women

Women wove the cloth used as tribute — a vital economic role. Some became priestesses, midwives, healers, or merchants. A few noblewomen were scribes. Women who produced more cloth tribute could cause husbands to take additional wives.

### Decline

By the late 15th century, the Aztec Empire weakened because: lack of wheeled vehicles and pack animals made agriculture inefficient; constant military expansion to secure sacrificial victims overextended the empire; extracted tribute bred deep resentment among subject peoples. When Spaniards arrived in **1519**, many conquered tribes were ready to rebel and joined the Spanish.

## South America: The Inca

In **1438**, a tribal leader called **Pachacuti** ("transformer/shaker of the earth") began conquering tribes near modern Cuzco, Peru. Within decades, the **Incan Empire** extended from Ecuador to Chile — the largest empire in pre-Columbian American history.

### Government, Economy, and Society

- **Four provinces**, each with a governor and bureaucracy
- Conquered leaders who demonstrated loyalty were rewarded
- **Mit'a system**: mandatory public service (labor) instead of tribute — men aged 15–50 provided agricultural labor, construction (including the famous road system), and other work
- **Carpa Nan** (road system): 25,000 miles of roads used mainly by government and military
- Bridges were critical in the mountainous Andes

> [!tip]
> **Aztec vs. Inca administration comparison**: The Aztecs used a tribute system (conquered people paid goods); the Inca used mit'a (conquered people provided labor). The Aztecs allowed local rulers to govern; the Inca established their own provincial governors. The Inca created more administrative integration; the Aztecs created more resentment.

### Religion

- **Inti** (sun god) was most important; Inca rulers were his earthly representatives
- **Temple of the Sun** in Cuzco was the religious center
- **Royal ancestor veneration**: dead rulers were mummified and continued to "rule" — owning their property, servants, and possessions. New rulers couldn't inherit this wealth, motivating constant conquest
- **Animism (huaca)**: belief that geographical features and objects (mountains, rivers, stones) could have supernatural power
- Human sacrifice was practiced but apparently less frequently than among the Aztecs
- **Quipu**: knotted string system for recording numerical information (trade, engineering, messages) — the Inca had no written language

### Achievements

- **Terrace agriculture** using **waru waru** (raised beds with water channels) to grow potatoes and maize in mountains
- Sophisticated road and bridge construction (the Carpa Nan)
- Masonry: buildings without mortar that still stand today (Machu Picchu)

### Decline

In **1532**, Spanish conquistador Francisco Pizarro arrived during an Incan civil war of succession. The weakened military made conquest easier. European **diseases** also devastated the population. The Spanish conquered the core of the empire in **1533**.

## Comparing Three American Civilizations

| Feature | Maya | Aztec | Inca |
|---------|------|-------|------|
| Region | Mexico/Central America | Central Mexico | Andes, South America |
| Period | 400–1517 | 1200–1521 | 1200–1533 |
| Government | City-states with kings | Emperor (theocracy) + tribute | Emperor + provincial governors + mit'a |
| Economy | Moderate trade | Extensive tribute | Mit'a (labor) |
| Religion | Polytheistic; some human sacrifice | Polytheistic; extensive human sacrifice | Polytheistic; some human sacrifice; ancestor veneration |
| Technology | Writing, zero, calendar, rubber | Chinampas, step pyramids, calendar | Quipu, roads, terrace agriculture, masonry |
| Decline | Drought, deforestation, Spanish | Disease, resentment, Spanish | Disease, civil war, Spanish |
