# Unit 1.4: State Building in Africa (1200–1450)

**AMSCO Unit:** Unit 1 — The Global Tapestry
**AP Exam Period:** Period 1 (1200–1450)
**Key Regions:** West Africa (Mali Empire), East Africa (Swahili Coast), Southern Africa (Great Zimbabwe), Northeast Africa (Ethiopia/Axum)

## Summary

Africa in 1200–1450 was home to some of the world's most powerful and sophisticated states, yet it is often underrepresented in world history courses. The Mali Empire in West Africa controlled vast gold resources and hosted one of the most wealthy and culturally vibrant courts in the world, as demonstrated by Mansa Musa's spectacular 1324 hajj. The Swahili city-states along the East African coast were thriving Islamic commercial centers fully integrated into the Indian Ocean trading network. In southern Africa, the Zimbabwe Plateau supported the Great Zimbabwe state, whose stone enclosures remain among Africa's most impressive architectural monuments. Ethiopian Christianity maintained a distinct African Christian tradition. These states demonstrate that Africa was not isolated from global networks — it was deeply embedded in them.

## AMSCO Learning Objectives

- Explain the diversity of African states and their connections to global trade networks
- Analyze how Islam shaped governance and culture in West and East African states
- Explain the role of the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade networks in African state formation
- Evaluate continuity and change in African political structures between 1200 and 1450

## AP World History Key Concepts

- **KC-1.2.I** — Islam's spread to sub-Saharan Africa shaped political authority and cultural identity
- **KC-1.3.II** — Trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trade networks integrated African states into global commerce
- **KC-1.2.II** — African rulers used religious ideology (Islam, indigenous religions, Christianity) to legitimize political authority
- **KC-2.3.III** — The spread of disease and goods along trade networks had demographic and political consequences
