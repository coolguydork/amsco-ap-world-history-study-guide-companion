# People, States & Movements — Topic 1.4

*AMSCO pp. 33–42 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Key People

### Pachacuti (r. c. 1438–1471) — Inca Empire
The transformative ruler who built the Inca Empire. Beginning from a small tribal base near Cuzco, Peru, his conquests (followed by his son's) created **Tawantinsuyu** — the largest empire in the Americas, stretching from Ecuador to Chile. His name means "Transformer of the Earth" (or "Shaker"). He established the administrative framework: four provinces, each with its own governor; the mit'a labor system; and the road network (Carpa Nan) that made the empire governable. **The single most important individual for Topic 1.4 on the AP exam.**

### Moctezuma I (r. 1440–1469) — Aztec Empire
Fifth Aztec emperor (tlatoani); expanded the Triple Alliance empire significantly. Under him, the Aztec tribute system matured and the empire's reach extended from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific. He and Tlacaelel (his adviser) restructured Aztec ideology, placing the sun god Huitzilopochtli at the center of religion and institutionalizing human sacrifice as a state practice. Represents the Aztec at the height of their power.

### Nezahualcoyotl (1402–1472) — Aztec poet-king
Ruler of Texcoco, one of the three cities in the Aztec Triple Alliance. A poet, philosopher, and architect who composed some of the most celebrated Aztec poetry — including the opening verse of the AMSCO chapter. He oversaw architectural projects in Texcoco and is remembered as a philosopher-king. His existence complicates caricatures of Aztec civilization as solely militaristic. Good example for contextualizing Aztec cultural sophistication.

### Lady Xoc — Maya noblewoman
Elite Maya woman depicted in carved stone lintels performing a bloodletting ritual — piercing her tongue and pulling a thorned rope through it as an offering to the gods. She demonstrates both the role of elite women in Maya religious life and the broader Mesoamerican practice of **bloodletting** (as distinct from human sacrifice) as a form of divine communication. A primary source example for AP questions about Maya religion and gender roles.

## Key Comparison: Aztec vs. Inca Empire-Maintenance

| Feature | Aztec | Inca |
|---|---|---|
| **What was extracted** | Tribute — goods (food, cloth, luxury items) | Labor — mit'a (mandatory public service) |
| **Local rulers** | Left in place as tribute collectors | Retained if loyal; Inca officials added above them |
| **Administrative reach** | Provincial organization; Aztec officials stationed in capitals | 4 provinces, each with full governor + bureaucracy |
| **Subject loyalty** | Low — tribute resentment drove rebellion | Higher — labor was reciprocal (state provided food, goods) |
| **Communication** | Runners; no road network comparable to Inca | Carpa Nan (25,000-mile road system) + quipu |
| **Vulnerability** | Many subjects willing to ally with Spanish | Civil war of succession weakened empire at Spanish arrival |

> [!exam-tip]
> AP exam comparison questions love pairing Aztec and Inca. The mit'a vs. tribute distinction is the most testable difference. A secondary distinction: the Inca had **a far more integrated administrative infrastructure** (roads, quipu, four-province system) than the Aztec, who relied heavily on existing local power structures.

## The Olmec Legacy

AMSCO addresses the historiographical debate about whether later Mesoamerican civilizations descended from or independently developed alongside the **Olmec** (c. 1500–400 BCE). Evidence for connection includes: the feathered serpent deity appearing in Olmec, Maya, and Aztec religion; ritual practices (step pyramids, ball courts, human sacrifice) shared across cultures. Evidence against: independent development may explain some similarities. For AP purposes: treat the Olmec as important **context** for Mesoamerican civilizations, not a proven direct ancestor.
