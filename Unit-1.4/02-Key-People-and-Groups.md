# Unit 1.4: Key People and Groups — Developments in the Americas (1200–1450)

## Individuals and Groups

### The Mexica (Aztec) People
**Period:** c. 1200–1521 | **Region:** Mesoamerica (Mexico)

- According to their own tradition, the Mexica were nomadic migrants from the north ("Aztlan") who arrived in the Valley of Mexico around 1200 and were initially subordinate to established city-states
- Founded Tenochtitlan in 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco (fulfilling a divine prophecy — an eagle eating a serpent on a cactus, still depicted on Mexico's flag)
- Within a century of founding Tenochtitlan, the Mexica had built the largest empire in Mesoamerican history through military conquest and strategic alliances

---

### Itzcoatl
**Period:** r. 1427–1440 | **Region:** Aztec Empire

- The Aztec ruler who formed the Triple Alliance (with Texcoco and Tlacopan) that became the foundation of the Aztec Empire
- Conquered the dominant regional power (the Tepanec) and established Aztec military supremacy in the Valley of Mexico
- According to Aztec tradition, Itzcoatl ordered the destruction of historical records from conquered peoples and the rewriting of history to center the Mexica — a deliberate act of historical revision

---

### Moctezuma I (Ilhuicamina)
**Period:** r. 1440–1469 | **Region:** Aztec Empire

- Expanded the Aztec Empire southward toward the Gulf Coast and Oaxaca, dramatically increasing the number of tribute-paying states
- Instituted the Flower Wars as a systematic practice for obtaining sacrificial victims while keeping neighboring states as permanent enemies rather than conquering them
- Formalized the social hierarchy of the Aztec Empire, distinguishing the noble class (pipiltin) from commoners (macehualtin) through dress codes, legal privileges, and educational differences

---

### Pachacuti
**Period:** r. 1438–1471 | **Region:** Inca Empire (Peru)

- The greatest Inca conqueror and state-builder; transformed a small regional kingdom in the Cusco valley into the vast Tawantinsuyu empire in a single generation
- Created the architectural and administrative systems that defined the Inca Empire: the road network, the mit'a labor system, state storehouses (qollqa), and the system of indirect rule through local elites (curacas)
- Commissioned the construction of Machu Picchu as a royal estate and religious center; rebuilt Cusco as a grand imperial capital shaped like a puma

> [!exam-tip]
> Pachacuti is the Inca equivalent of figures like Sundiata (Mali) or Zhu Yuanzhang (Ming China) — an empire-founder whose personal decisions defined the shape of an entire civilization. The AP exam may ask you to explain HOW Pachacuti built the empire (road networks, mit'a, indirect rule) or to compare his methods with other empire-builders.

---

### Inca Nobility and Curacas
**Period:** 15th century | **Region:** Inca Empire

- The Inca nobility (ayllus of the ruling Inca ethnic group) held top administrative positions; Pachacuti created a new "nobility by privilege" to reward loyal non-Inca leaders
- Curacas were local leaders of conquered communities who were allowed to maintain their positions in exchange for collecting mit'a labor and tribute from their people
- This system of indirect rule — leaving local elites in place — is comparable to Mongol governance methods and is a key reason the Inca Empire could be so vast while its core population was relatively small

---

### Aztec Women
**Period:** 1200–1521 | **Region:** Aztec Empire

- Aztec women were expected to manage the household and weave cloth (a highly valued commodity paid as tribute), but some women achieved recognition as market traders, midwives, or religious practitioners
- The calmecac (elite school for nobles) admitted some girls, suggesting that literacy and religious education were not exclusively male domains
- Elite women could own property and participate in legal proceedings; queens and noble women played important roles in dynastic alliances through strategic marriages

---

### Inca Women and the Acllaconas
**Period:** 15th century | **Region:** Inca Empire

- Selected women (acllaconas, or "chosen women") were taken from their communities to serve the Inca state as weavers of fine cloth (cumbi), brewers of chicha (ritual beer), religious specialists, or concubines/wives of the Sapa Inca
- This system simultaneously removed attractive women from local communities (reducing local leaders' resources), concentrated high-status production in the capital, and created a class of women directly tied to the imperial apparatus
- Ordinary Inca women worked within the ayllu (kinship group) system, contributing labor alongside men under the mit'a system and managing agricultural production

---

### Maya Successor States
**Period:** 1200–1450 | **Region:** Yucatán Peninsula and highland Guatemala

- After the Classic Maya collapse (c. 800–900 CE), Maya peoples continued in smaller city-states; the Postclassic period saw the rise of Chichén Itzá and then Mayapán as dominant Yucatán powers
- Mayapán dominated the northern Yucatán Maya until its own collapse around 1441–1461 from internal rebellion, leaving a fragmented landscape of small competing city-states
- Highland Maya in Guatemala maintained distinct kingdoms (K'iche', Kaqchikel); their resistance to Spanish conquest was fierce and prolonged — demonstrating Maya resilience despite political fragmentation

---

### Mississippian Culture / Cahokia
**Period:** c. 800–1350 | **Region:** North America (present-day St. Louis, Missouri area)

- Cahokia was the largest pre-Columbian city north of Mexico, with a population estimated at 10,000–20,000 at its height (c. 1100 CE)
- Built a massive earthen platform mound (Monks Mound) larger at its base than the Great Pyramid of Egypt; organized a complex chiefdom society based on maize agriculture
- Had declined and largely been abandoned by 1350, possibly due to climate change, agricultural exhaustion, or political upheaval — before Europeans arrived

---

### Ancestral Puebloans (Anasazi)
**Period:** c. 900–1300 | **Region:** American Southwest (modern Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, Colorado)

- Built elaborate multi-story stone and adobe complexes at Chaco Canyon (a major regional ceremonial and trade hub) and Mesa Verde (famous cliff dwellings cut into canyon walls)
- Chaco Canyon served as a center of turquoise trade and ritual activity connecting communities across hundreds of miles; demonstrates long-distance exchange networks within North America
- Dispersed from their major centers by c. 1300, likely due to prolonged drought and resource depletion; their descendants are the modern Pueblo peoples of the Southwest

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam uses Mississippian culture and Ancestral Puebloans to demonstrate that **complex, organized societies existed across North America** — not just in Mesoamerica and the Andes. Be ready to contrast these decentralized chiefdom/community structures with the tributary empires of the Aztec and Inca.
