# Practice & Review — Topic 1.4

*AMSCO pp. 40–42 | Period 1: c. 1200–c. 1450*

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the excerpt below. (AMSCO p. 40)*

> "The Inka [Inca] ruled the greatest empire on earth. Bigger than Ming Dynasty China, bigger than Ivan the Great's expanding Russia, bigger than Songhai in the Sahel or powerful Great Zimbabwe in the East Africa tablelands, bigger than the cresting Ottoman Empire, bigger than the Triple Alliance [as the Aztec Empire is more precisely known], bigger by far than any European state, the Inka dominion extended over a staggering thirty-two degrees of latitude — as if a single power held sway from St. Petersburg to Cairo. The empire encompassed every imaginable type of terrain, from the rainforest of upper Amazonia to the deserts of the Peruvian coast and the twenty-thousand-foot peaks of the Andes in between. 'If imperial potential is judged in terms of environmental adaptability,' wrote the Oxford historian Felipe Fernandez-Armesto, 'the Inka [Inca] were the most impressive empire builders of their day.' "
>
> — Charles Mann, *1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus*, 2005

**1.** Why were the Inca able to achieve the accomplishment described in the passage?

(A) They divided their empire into four provinces so it would be easier to govern.
(B) They demanded heavy tribute from conquered groups living on the edges of their empire.
(C) They routinely sent armies through the empire to collect tribute.
(D) They forced conquered people to adopt the language of the Inca.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage emphasizes the Inca's ability to govern "every imaginable type of terrain" — environmental adaptability. The four-province administrative system (with governors, bureaucracies, and the road network that connected them) is what made governing this geographically diverse empire possible. Option B is wrong because the Inca used mit'a (labor), not tribute of goods. Option C describes the Aztec system, not the Inca. Option D is partially true (Quechua was promoted) but not the key mechanism of governance or the answer to the passage's main claim.

---

**2.** The Carpa Nan demonstrates the main point expressed by the writer in the excerpt because it

(A) made living in the rainforest possible
(B) provided a common system of written communication
(C) provided irrigation for terraced farmlands
(D) connected the parts of the empire

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The passage's main argument is that the Inca were remarkable for governing an empire spanning 32 degrees of latitude and every type of terrain. The Carpa Nan — the 25,000-mile road system — is precisely what made that geographic integration possible. Option A is wrong (the road didn't make rainforest habitation possible). Option B is wrong (the quipu served record-keeping functions; the road system moved people and goods, not writing). Option C is wrong (waru waru terracing provided irrigation, not the road network).

---

**3.** When the author of the passage describes the Incan civilization as the "greatest empire on earth," he means that it

(A) constructed great buildings and other structures
(B) conquered other empires, including the Aztecs
(C) adapted successfully to diverse environments
(D) were the wealthiest civilization in the world in 1491

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The passage explicitly quotes historian Fernandez-Armesto: "If imperial potential is judged in terms of environmental adaptability, the Inka were the most impressive empire builders of their day." The author's specific argument is about geographic and environmental scope — rainforest, desert, 20,000-foot peaks — not about buildings (A), conquering other empires (B, and the Inca never conquered the Aztec), or wealth (D, which the passage does not claim).

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Source-Based (Maya Evidence Problems)

**Source:** Lewis Spence, *The Myths of Mexico and Peru* (1913): "...accounts of Spanish authors concerning Maya mythology do not agree with the representations of the gods delineated in the codices. So few data have yet been collected regarding the Maya mythology that to dogmatize upon any subject connected with it would indeed be rash."

**(A)** Identify Spence's claim in the passage above.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identify Claim
> Spence's claim is that knowledge of Maya mythology is incomplete and uncertain because the available sources — Spanish accounts and indigenous codices — are inconsistent with each other. Therefore, scholars cannot yet make definitive conclusions about Maya religion.

**(B)** Explain ONE piece of evidence from an outside source that supports Spence's claim.

> [!tip] Model Response — Outside Evidence
> Archaeological evidence from Maya sites continues to reveal religious imagery that doesn't match either Spanish accounts or surviving codices. For example, the carved lintels at Yaxchilan (depicting Lady Xoc performing bloodletting rituals) provided information about elite Maya religious practice that Spanish colonial texts largely ignored, demonstrating that written accounts — both Spanish and indigenous — captured only part of the picture.

**(C)** Explain how Spence's argument affects understanding Mayan history.

> [!tip] Model Response — Effect on Understanding
> Spence's argument highlights the problem of **historical bias and incomplete evidence** in studying pre-Columbian civilizations. Because Spanish colonizers destroyed most Maya codices and wrote accounts filtered through their own cultural lens, our understanding of Maya religion is necessarily partial and potentially distorted. This means historians must use multiple types of evidence — archaeological, epigraphic, and written — and cannot rely on any single source type for accurate reconstruction of Maya beliefs. It also explains why interpretations of Maya civilization have changed dramatically as new archaeological evidence has emerged.

### SAQ 2 — Non-Source-Based (Aztec and Inca Comparison)

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which culture from the Incan civilization is **similar** to the culture of the Aztec civilization.

> [!tip] Model Response — Similarity
> Both the Inca and Aztec civilizations practiced **polytheistic religion that included human sacrifice**, though it was more frequent and institutionalized in Aztec practice. In both civilizations, rulers were considered to have a divine connection — the Aztec emperor was a divine representative of the gods, while the Sapa Inca was considered the representative of Inti (the sun god) on earth. This religious legitimation of political authority was a core cultural feature of both empires.

**(B)** Explain ONE difference in how the Incas and the Aztecs maintained their empires.

> [!tip] Model Response — Difference
> The Inca maintained their empire primarily through the **mit'a system** — mandatory labor service — while the Aztec relied on a **tribute system** demanding goods such as food, cloth, and luxury items from conquered peoples. Under the Inca, subjects owed the state labor (for roads, agriculture, and construction), and in return the state provided food and goods from its storehouses, creating a reciprocal relationship. Under the Aztec system, tribute was one-directional extraction, with conquered peoples gaining only protection in return. This difference helps explain why the Inca's subject peoples showed somewhat greater loyalty, while many Aztec subjects were willing to ally with Spanish forces against the empire.

**(C)** Explain ONE reason why the people living under Aztec rule wanted a change in rulers at the time the Europeans arrived.

> [!tip] Model Response — Resentment Under Aztec Rule
> The Aztec tribute system extracted food, cloth, firewood, and luxury goods from conquered peoples on a continuous basis, with no reciprocal benefit beyond protection. For many subject peoples, this extraction was economically burdensome and politically humiliating — local rulers were reduced to tribute collectors for the Aztec, and any failure to meet tribute demands was punished. Additionally, the Aztec practice of taking captives for human sacrifice from subject populations created a constant fear. When Spanish forces arrived in 1519, many subject peoples saw an opportunity to overthrow Aztec dominance and willingly allied with Hernán Cortés, providing the indigenous military support that ultimately enabled the conquest.

## Historical Reasoning Practice — Identify Evidence

*AMSCO p. 42 | AP Skill: Primary vs. Secondary Sources*

**Context:** The Codex Magliabecchi is a 16th-century manuscript depicting Aztec rituals, including human sacrifice. It is considered a primary source.

> [!exam-tip]
> **Primary source** = created during or closely after the historical period it describes, by a participant or observer. **Secondary source** = analyzes primary sources to draw conclusions. The Codex Magliabecchi is primary — but remember: it was compiled after Spanish arrival and likely reflects some Spanish influence in its framing of sacrifice.

**Practice Question:** If you were developing an argument about the Aztec population's attitude toward human sacrifice, what evidence might you use from the Codex Magliabecchi illustration?

> [!tip] Model Response — Evidence Analysis
> The Codex Magliabecchi illustration depicts an Aztec sacrifice ritual in which a human heart is offered to the war god Huitzilopochtli. As a primary source created close to the period of Aztec rule, it provides evidence that human sacrifice was a real, institutionalized practice — not a Spanish fabrication. The ritual imagery and the presence of priests and other participants suggest that sacrifice was a formalized religious ceremony, not random violence. However, the argument the evidence supports is limited: the image shows *that* sacrifice occurred and *how* it was performed, but it cannot tell us *how frequently* it happened, whether participants consented, or what the broader population thought about it. A historian using this image must acknowledge these limits and supplement it with other sources — such as indigenous oral accounts, Spanish texts, and archaeological evidence — to build a complete argument.

> [!warning]
> A common AP error: treating a primary source as automatically more reliable than a secondary source. The Codex Magliabecchi was compiled partly under Spanish oversight — it may reflect Spanish framing of Aztec sacrifice as barbaric to justify conquest. Always consider **who made the source, when, and for what purpose**.
