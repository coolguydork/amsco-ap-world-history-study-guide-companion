# Unit 1.4: Themes and Causation — Developments in the Americas (1200–1450)

## Governance

The Aztec and Inca empires represent two distinct models of imperial governance, both of which differ significantly from Eurasian imperial models. The Aztec Empire was a **tribute empire**: the Mexica extracted goods and labor from conquered peoples but left local governance largely intact, governing through terror (military force) and religion (the threat of divine punishment for non-compliance) rather than through a unified administrative system. This model was efficient for extraction but created no loyalty — conquered peoples had every incentive to rebel when an opportunity arose. The Inca Empire, by contrast, was a **redistributive state**: Pachacuti built a sophisticated administrative apparatus including the road network, state storehouses, the mit'a labor system, and provincial governors who lived in Cusco as both administrators and hostages. The Inca state actually *gave back* to its subjects through redistribution of goods from storehouses during famines — a social contract that, along with religious legitimacy, generated more genuine loyalty than the Aztec tribute model. Both empires used religion to legitimize imperial authority: the **Sapa Inca** was considered the son of the sun god Inti; **Aztec rulers (tlatoque)** were intermediaries between humanity and the gods who required human sacrifice to sustain the cosmic order.

## Economic Systems

Both the Aztec and Inca empires developed sophisticated economic systems without monetary currency (in the Inca case) or with only limited use of cacao beans and cloth as quasi-currency (in the Aztec case). The Aztec tribute system extracted enormous quantities of goods — cotton, cacao, feathers, jade, gold, food — from hundreds of subject communities, concentrating this wealth in Tenochtitlan where it supported a large non-agricultural class (priests, warriors, merchants, artisans, bureaucrats). The great Tlatelolco market in Tenochtitlan was one of the world's largest markets, with tens of thousands of buyers and sellers each day and a sophisticated system of quality control and dispute resolution. The Inca economy operated entirely without markets: the state owned all land and resources in theory, extracted labor through the mit'a, stored agricultural surplus in thousands of qollqa storehouses, and redistributed goods to support armies, workers, and communities in need. Both systems were highly effective at mobilizing labor for massive construction projects (pyramids, roads, temples), but both were also fundamentally extractive in ways that would generate resistance.

## Cultural Developments

Both the Aztec and Inca civilizations developed sophisticated cultural and intellectual traditions. The Aztec used a sophisticated writing system combining pictographs and ideographs (partially phonetic), maintained ritual calendars of 365 days (solar) and 260 days (ritual) that meshed in a 52-year cycle, and produced extraordinary art in featherwork, goldsmithing, and stone carving. Aztec poetry (cuicatl) was a highly developed literary form; the ruler Nezahualcoyotl of Texcoco was a celebrated philosopher-poet whose works are preserved. The Inca lacked a writing system but compensated with the quipu record-keeping system and an extraordinarily rich oral tradition that preserved dynastic histories and religious narratives. Inca visual culture expressed itself in textiles (the most valued art form), architecture (perfectly fitted stone, precise geometry), and the spectacular art of metalwork in gold and silver. Both civilizations were deeply engaged with cosmological questions — how to maintain the cosmic order, how to read celestial signals, and how to relate humanity to divine forces.

## Social Structures

Both societies were sharply hierarchical. In the Aztec Empire, the noble class (pipiltin) was distinguished from commoners (macehualtin) by birth, dress, education, and legal privileges. Merchants (pochteca) formed a separate hereditary class with their own courts, deity (Yacatecuhtli), and rights — occupying an ambiguous middle position between the noble and commoner worlds. Enslaved people existed but could own property and buy their freedom. The calpulli (clan-based community) provided social services, land tenure, and educational institutions at the local level, giving commoners a meaningful institutional framework. In the Inca Empire, the ayllu (kinship group) was the basic social unit, with collective responsibility for mit'a labor and agricultural production. The caste of "Inca by privilege" — non-ethnic Incas elevated to near-noble status for loyalty — shows that the Inca state creatively managed social hierarchy to build political support.

## Technology and Environment

The Americas' civilizations developed remarkable technologies within their unique geographic and ecological constraints. The Inca road system — 25,000 miles including rope bridges over Andean gorges, tunnels through mountains, and paved paths along coastal deserts — was arguably the most impressive pre-modern infrastructure achievement in the Americas. Aztec chinampas (artificial floating gardens constructed in Lake Texcoco's shallow waters) converted swampy lakebed into extraordinarily productive agricultural land, supporting Tenochtitlan's enormous population. Both civilizations demonstrated mastery of their environments through terrace agriculture (Inca), hydraulic engineering (Aztec), and careful management of altitude zones (the Inca "vertical ecology" that exploited different crops at different elevations). The absence of large domesticable draft animals (the wheel existed in the Americas as a toy but had no practical application without animals to pull wheeled vehicles) shaped both civilizations' approaches to transportation and construction.

## Human Migration

The Americas' civilizations developed in relative isolation from Eurasian developments, but this does not mean they were static. The Mexica themselves were migrants who arrived in the Valley of Mexico from the north around 1200 — the story of their migration (guided by Huitzilopochtli until they found the prophesied sign) is central to their imperial mythology. Inca expansion involved the deliberate relocation of populations: the Inca moved loyal ethnic groups (mitimaes) into newly conquered territories to dilute local resistance and spread Quechua language and Inca culture. Conversely, troublesome populations were relocated away from their homelands. This systematic use of forced migration as an administrative tool has parallels with Assyrian and later Roman practices in Eurasia — a remarkable example of convergent political evolution.
