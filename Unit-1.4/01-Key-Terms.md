# Unit 1.4: Key Terms — State Building in Africa (1200–1450)

## Vocabulary

**Mali Empire** — A powerful West African empire (c. 1235–1600) that controlled the gold-salt trade across the Sahara; successor to the Ghana Empire. (Significance: one of the wealthiest states in the world at its height — its gold resources made it extraordinarily prosperous by any regional standard; Mansa Musa's hajj put Mali on European maps; patron of Islamic scholarship in Timbuktu.)

**Mansa Musa** — Emperor (mansa) of Mali (r. 1312–1337) whose 1324–1325 hajj to Mecca became legendary for its scale and wealth; he distributed so much gold that he depressed Egyptian gold prices for a decade. (Significance: the most famous African ruler in world history; his pilgrimage demonstrated Mali's extraordinary wealth and connected West Africa to the Mediterranean Islamic world. See [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Mansa Musa]].)

**Timbuktu** — A major commercial and intellectual city in the Mali Empire, located at the intersection of trans-Saharan trade routes and the Niger River; became a center of Islamic scholarship with the famous Sankore mosque and university. (Significance: demonstrates that sub-Saharan Africa produced world-class scholarship and cultural achievement during this period.)

**Trans-Saharan Trade** — Long-distance commerce crossing the Sahara Desert, primarily exchanging West African gold and salt for North African and Mediterranean manufactured goods, horses, and Islamic scholarship. (Significance: the economic foundation of West African empire-building; Islam traveled with merchants along these routes, shaping West African governance and culture. See [[Unit-1.2/01-Key-Terms#Trans-Saharan Trade]].)

**Griots** — West African oral historians, musicians, and storytellers who preserved cultural memory and political history through performance rather than written texts. (Significance: demonstrates the vitality of non-written historical traditions; primary source for Mali's history including the Epic of Sundiata.)

**Sundiata Keita** — The legendary founder of the Mali Empire (r. c. 1235–1255) who unified Mande-speaking peoples and defeated the Sosso king Sumanguru Kante; subject of the "Epic of Sundiata" preserved by griots. (Significance: Mali's founding myth; illustrates how African states used a combination of military power and cultural legitimacy — both indigenous religious authority and later Islam — to build empire.)

**Swahili Coast** — The eastern coast of Africa (from Somalia to Mozambique) where Bantu-speaking African populations mixed with Arab, Persian, and Indian merchants to create a distinctive Afro-Islamic Swahili culture and language. (Significance: the East African end of the Indian Ocean trade network; gold, ivory, and enslaved people exported in exchange for Indian textiles and Chinese porcelain.)

**Kilwa** — The most powerful of the Swahili city-states (in present-day Tanzania), controlling the gold trade from the Zimbabwe Plateau to Indian Ocean merchants. (Significance: Ibn Battuta visited Kilwa and called it "one of the most beautiful cities in the world"; its ruins show the sophistication of East African Islamic urban culture.)

**Great Zimbabwe** — A stone-walled royal enclosure on the Zimbabwe Plateau in southern Africa (c. 1100–1450), capital of a powerful state that controlled gold production and exported it via the Swahili Coast to Indian Ocean trade networks. (Significance: demonstrates indigenous African state formation and architectural achievement — the massive dry-stone walls were built without mortar by local Shona-speaking peoples; Chinese porcelain and glass beads found at the site confirm active participation in long-distance Indian Ocean trade, making it incorrect to describe the state as isolated from external networks.)

**Swahili** — A Bantu language with significant Arabic loanwords, the *lingua franca* of East Africa's coastal trading world; also refers to the mixed Afro-Islamic culture of the East African coast. (Significance: demonstrates how trade creates cultural synthesis; Swahili identity blended African, Arab, Persian, and Indian elements.)

**Ethiopia (Zagwe/Solomonid Dynasty)** — A Christian kingdom in the highlands of East Africa that claimed continuity with the ancient Aksumite empire and the biblical lineage of King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. (Significance: one of the world's oldest continuous Christian traditions; maintained independence from Islamic pressure; produced the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela under the Zagwe Dynasty.)

**Lalibela** — A complex of eleven monolithic rock-hewn churches carved from solid rock in the 12th–13th centuries in Ethiopia, commissioned by King Lalibela of the Zagwe dynasty. (Significance: demonstrates the sophistication of Ethiopian Christian civilization; continues to be a major pilgrimage site; reflects African Christian architectural innovation distinct from European or Middle Eastern traditions.)

**Stateless Societies** — African communities organized around kinship, age grades, and village councils rather than centralized state authority; e.g., many Bantu-speaking communities in sub-Saharan Africa. (Significance: shows the diversity of African political organization — not all of Africa was organized into states; these communities coexisted with and traded with imperial neighbors.)

**Gold-Salt Trade** — The fundamental economic exchange of the trans-Saharan network: West Africa had abundant gold but scarce salt; the Sahara had vast salt deposits; North Africa had manufactured goods and access to Mediterranean markets. (Significance: the economic foundation of West African empire-building, including Ghana, Mali, and later Songhai.)

**Ibn Battuta in East Africa** — The Moroccan traveler visited the Swahili Coast cities (including Kilwa) in 1331 and described them as sophisticated Islamic urban centers comparable to cities in the Islamic heartland. (Significance: his account is the most detailed 14th-century source on Swahili city-states; demonstrates East Africa's full integration into the Islamic world.)

> [!exam-tip]
> African states are heavily tested in the AP exam, but often through unfamiliar primary sources. Know the THREE main African trading networks: **trans-Saharan** (West Africa), **Indian Ocean** (East Africa), and the internal networks connecting them. Also know the distinction between Islamic-influenced states (Mali, Swahili) and independent traditions (Great Zimbabwe, Ethiopia).
