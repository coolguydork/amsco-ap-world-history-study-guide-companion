# People, States & Movements

**Unit 9 — Globalization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 688–695**

---

## Key People

**Muhammad Yunus (b. 1940)**
Bangladeshi economist and founder of the Grameen Bank, which pioneered **microcredit** — small loans to impoverished rural women who lacked collateral for conventional bank loans. Won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006. After the 2013 Rana Plaza factory collapse in Dhaka, Yunus called it "a symbol of our failure as a nation" and advocated for an international minimum wage. On the AP exam he represents a *reform* response to globalization's inequality: using market tools to include the excluded rather than rejecting the global economy outright.

> [!tip]
> Microcredit is a **pro-poor reform** of globalization, not a rejection of it. Yunus believed globalization could be made equitable — contrast him with activists who demanded withdrawal from the WTO or the IMF.

**Theresa May (b. 1956)**
British Conservative politician who served as Prime Minister from 2016 to 2019. She inherited the politically explosive task of negotiating Britain's exit from the EU (Brexit) after the 2016 referendum. Her inability to secure a withdrawal deal acceptable to both Parliament and the EU's 27 remaining members forced her resignation in 2019. On the AP exam she represents the political difficulty of translating nationalist sentiment into workable policy — resistance to supranational integration had real governing consequences.

**Naomi Klein (b. 1970)**
Canadian author and activist whose book *No Logo* (1999) became the intellectual manifesto of the anti-globalization movement. Klein argued that transnational corporations had replaced genuine culture with branded imagery, and that global supply chains systematically exploited workers in the Global South. The AP exam uses her as an intellectual voice of anti-corporate globalization sentiment.

**Manal al-Sharif (b. 1979)**
Saudi women's rights activist who was arrested in 2011 for driving a car and posting the video on YouTube, making her a global symbol of digital activism and women's resistance in authoritarian states. She later became a prominent critic of social media platforms, warning that tools originally designed to empower activists were being weaponized by governments for surveillance and harassment. On the AP exam she represents both the power of technology for reform [[Unit-9.1/Advances in Technology & Exchange Overview|Advances in Technology & Exchange Overview]] and its limits when controlled by repressive states.

**Subcomandante Marcos (b. 1963)**
Spokesman and military leader of the **Zapatista Army of National Liberation (EZLN)** in Mexico, which launched its uprising on January 1, 1994 — the same day NAFTA took effect. Marcos framed the revolt as indigenous Mayan resistance to free trade policies that threatened small farmers. The Zapatistas used the internet to gain global attention, making them an early model of digital-era resistance movements.

## Key States

**China**
China simultaneously embraced *economic* globalization (joining the WTO in 2001) while aggressively resisting *cultural and informational* globalization. The government banned Twitter and Facebook after 2009 Uighur-Han riots in Urumqi, replacing them with state-monitored **Weibo**. China's suppression of the Uighur minority in Xinjiang represents state-enforced cultural homogenization in the face of ethnic and religious difference.

**United Kingdom**
The Brexit vote (2016) and its three-year political fallout represent the most dramatic case of a major developed-world state withdrawing from supranational integration. The UK formally left the EU in 2020. Brexit illustrated that resistance to globalization could come from *inside* wealthy, economically integrated democracies — not just from the Global South.

**North Korea**
The most extreme example of state resistance to globalization: near-total isolation from international trade, finance, and cultural exchange. AMSCO cites North Korea as a state that has resisted both economic and cultural globalization. The AP exam occasionally uses it as a contrast case — what total resistance looks like versus partial resistance.

**Bangladesh**
Site of the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse, Bangladesh's garment industry (the world's second-largest) exemplifies the labor and safety risks of globalization's supply chains. Bangladesh highlights how developing-world governments may tolerate poor factory conditions to attract foreign investment.

## Key Movements

**Anti-Globalization Movement**
A broad, decentralized coalition that emerged in the 1990s, uniting labor unions, environmental groups, NGOs, indigenous rights advocates, and anti-corporate activists. The 1999 Battle of Seattle was its most visible moment. Demanded human rights, fair trade, sustainable development, and debt relief. Notably global and internet-coordinated, yet internally diverse — some sought to reform globalization, others to reject it outright.

**Brexit Movement**
A British political movement combining economic nationalism (protect British jobs and industries) with cultural nationalism (limit immigration) and constitutional nationalism (restore Parliamentary sovereignty). Succeeded in 2016 referendum; formal exit in 2020. Inspired parallel nationalist movements across Europe (Le Pen in France, AfD in Germany).

**Zapatista Movement (EZLN)**
Indigenous Mayan uprising in Chiapas, Mexico (1994–present). Timed its launch to coincide with NAFTA's start. Resisted free trade as a threat to subsistence farming and indigenous land rights. Pioneer of internet-based global solidarity networking.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves to ask you to compare resistance movements across time: Zapatistas (1994, economic/indigenous) vs. Brexit (2016, nationalist/sovereignty) vs. anti-WTO protests (1999, labor/environment). What do they share? What distinguishes them? All three oppose external economic forces, but their constituencies, methods, and goals differ sharply.
