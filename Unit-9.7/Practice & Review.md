## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: 1999 WTO protests photograph, Seattle, Washington (AMSCO p. 693)*

**Question 1.** Which of the following 20th-century developments contributed most directly to the reaction shown in the photograph?

(A) Unprecedented population growth
(B) Rising economic and cultural globalization
(C) Increasing risk of nuclear war
(D) The emergence of fascism

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The protesters are reacting to the WTO, the institution governing economic globalization. Population growth, nuclear war risk, and fascism are not connected to WTO protests in 1999.

**Question 2.** The protesters shown in the picture focused on World Trade Organization policies they said led to

(A) greater decentralization of political power
(B) higher levels of immigration into developed countries
(C) weaker environmental protections
(D) lower agricultural production in developing countries

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Anti-WTO protesters specifically argued the organization's trade rules sacrificed environmental standards for corporate profit. Immigration (B) was a Brexit grievance, not a WTO-specific complaint. Decentralization (A) is the opposite of what the WTO was accused of.

**Question 3.** A historian would most likely use the photograph as evidence of which of the following trends in the late 20th century?

(A) Increasing public concern about human impact on the environment
(B) The declining influence of unions and the labor movement
(C) The global dominance of American media and popular culture
(D) Growing acceptance of free-market policies

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The Seattle protests drew environmental groups prominently (the "Sea Turtle" protesters referenced in AMSCO wore sea turtle costumes to highlight WTO trade rules that threatened endangered species). B is the opposite of the trend shown. D is also backwards — the photo shows resistance to free-market policies.

## Short-Answer Questions

**SAQ 1** — Use the passage from Jagdish Sheth and Naresh Malhotra, "Global Consumer Culture," *Encyclopedia of International Marketing* (AMSCO p. 694):

*"Critique of, and resistance to, the spread of consumer culture is as old as the emergence of consumer culture in the 18th century... Religious, environmentalist, nationalist, and anti-corporate critiques have emerged with considerable vigor..."*

(A) Identify ONE thinker from the 18th or 19th century who would have viewed consumer culture critically, as described in the passage.

(B) Explain ONE reason some consumers avoid transnational firms, according to the authors.

(C) Explain ONE way in which globalization increased despite growing resistance in the late 20th century.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A
> Karl Marx (19th century) would have viewed consumer culture as described in the passage. Marx argued that capitalism created alienation — workers were separated from the goods they produced and from their own labor. His critique of the dehumanizing effects of industrial capitalism directly anticipates the passage's reference to "alienating dehumanizing effects of materialism."

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B
> According to the authors, 13% of consumers avoid transnational firms because they distrust global companies to behave responsibly and dislike brands that impose American values. This reflects concern that multinational corporations prioritize profit over ethical production, local cultures, and workers' rights — a central argument of the anti-globalization movement.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C
> Despite resistance, globalization expanded in the late 20th century because China joined the WTO in 2001, dramatically increasing the organization's economic reach and integrating the world's most populous nation into global trade networks. The WTO survived the 1999 Seattle protests and continued to govern 90%+ of international trade.

**SAQ 2** — Answer all parts.

(A) Identify ONE example of a state government that resisted globalization after 1900.

(B) Explain ONE social reason for resistance to globalization after 1990.

(C) Explain ONE reason for resistance to global financial institutions after 1990.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A
> China resisted cultural globalization after 1900, particularly after 2009. Following Uighur-Han riots in Urumqi, China banned Twitter and Facebook, introduced the state-controlled Weibo platform, and implemented mass surveillance of the Uighur Muslim minority in Xinjiang to prevent the spread of ideas it deemed threatening.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B
> One social reason for resistance to globalization after 1990 was the perceived threat to cultural identity. As American consumer culture, media, and values spread globally, many communities feared the erosion of local traditions, languages, and religious practices. Religious fundamentalism — Islamic, Christian, and Hindu — grew partly as a defense of traditional identity against Western secular modernity promoted by cultural globalization.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C
> Critics resisted the IMF after 1990 because the institution attached austerity conditions to its loans — requiring debtor governments to cut social spending, privatize state enterprises, and open markets to foreign competition. Critics argued these conditions harmed the poor in developing nations while benefiting wealthy creditor states and multinational corporations. Anti-IMF protests in 23 countries in 2001–2002 reflected this widespread anger.

## LEQ Prompts

**LEQ 1 (Causation):** Explain the causes of resistance to economic globalization in the period 1990–2020.

*Thesis approach:* Argue that resistance emerged from three reinforcing causes — economic exploitation (labor, debt), cultural anxiety (identity, sovereignty), and political exclusion (undemocratic international institutions). Use Rana Plaza, Brexit, and the Battle of Seattle as evidence from distinct categories of resistance.

**LEQ 2 (Comparison):** Compare resistance to globalization in the late 20th century with resistance to earlier forms of economic integration in the period 1750–1900.

*Thesis approach:* Both periods featured economic losers organizing politically: 19th-century Luddites resisting industrial capitalism; late-20th-century anti-globalists resisting free trade. Similarity: both targeted external economic forces disrupting local livelihoods. Difference: anti-globalization movements were internet-coordinated and global in scope; earlier resistance was local/regional.

**LEQ 3 (Continuity and Change Over Time):** Evaluate the extent to which resistance to globalization changed from 1900 to the present.

*Thesis approach:* Continuity — resistance has consistently appealed to economic fairness and cultural/national identity. Change — the forms shifted from isolated state autarky (North Korea, early 20th century) to mass organized movements (anti-WTO 1999) to democratic political backlash (Brexit 2016), reflecting growing public capacity to mobilize and vote against globalization.

## Key Exam Contrasts to Know

| Contrast | Side A | Side B |
|---|---|---|
| Reform vs. rejection | Muhammad Yunus (microcredit) | North Korea (total isolation) |
| Economic vs. cultural resistance | Anti-WTO protesters | Brexit voters |
| Bottom-up vs. top-down | Fair trade consumers, NGOs | China's internet censorship |
| Debt relief vs. debt restructuring | Cancellation of debt | Renegotiation of terms |
| Technology for liberation vs. control | Zapatistas, al-Sharif | Weibo, Saudi surveillance |
