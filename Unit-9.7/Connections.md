## Builds On

**[[Unit-9.4/Economics in the Global Age Overview\|Economics in the Global Age Overview]]**
The economic globalization that 9.7 resists — WTO, IMF, World Bank, free trade agreements, multinational corporations, structural adjustment — is established in 9.4. You cannot explain anti-globalization without first understanding what was being globalized and who benefited.

Cultural resistance (anti-Americanization, internet censorship, religious fundamentalism) in 9.7 responds directly to the cultural homogenization described in 9.6. Brexit's immigration anxieties also connect to the demographic flows discussed there.

## Compare With

**[[Unit-8.7/Global Resistance to Established Power Structures Overview\|Global Resistance to Established Power Structures Overview]]**
Cold War-era resistance movements (decolonization, student protests, civil rights) targeted state and imperial power; 9.7 targets *economic* global power. Both demonstrate that globalization/integration produces resistance, but the targets and methods differ. AMSCO lists 8.7 as a direct comparison topic.

**[[Unit-6.3/Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview\|Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview]]**
The Zapatista uprising (1994) directly parallels 6.3's indigenous responses to colonial state expansion: in both cases, outside economic forces threaten subsistence communities. The method changed (internet activism in 1994 vs. armed rebellion in 1750–1900), but the structural dynamic — outside power disrupting local life — is the same.

**[[Unit-7.4/Economy in the Interwar Period Overview\|Economy in the Interwar Period Overview]]**
The interwar period (1920s–1930s) saw a parallel nationalist backlash against economic internationalism — tariff wars, protectionism, economic autarky. Brexit and anti-IMF protests echo this 1930s dynamic. AMSCO explicitly connects 17th-century mercantilism [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]] and 20th-century globalization, but the interwar period is the closest historical parallel.

**[[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview\|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]]**
AMSCO directs students to compare 17th-century mercantile economic practices with 20th-21st century globalization. Both systems integrated economies across vast distances; both generated resistance from communities whose livelihoods were disrupted. The logic of exploitative global trade structures recurs across centuries.

> [!exam-tip]
> If you see a prompt about "resistance" in the modern period, your thesis should categorize resistance by *type*: economic (labor/trade), environmental, cultural/identity, and political/sovereignty. Use one piece of evidence per category. 9.7 is almost always in the supporting paragraph, not the topic sentence — the topic sentence should name the *category* of resistance, not just "anti-globalization protest."
