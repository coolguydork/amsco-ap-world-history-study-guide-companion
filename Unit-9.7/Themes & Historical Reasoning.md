# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Unit 9 — Globalization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 688–695**

**AP Skills:** Causation, Comparison

## AP Theme Analysis

### ECN — Economics and Inequality

The economic critique of globalization held that free trade and international financial institutions (WTO, IMF, World Bank) systematically benefited wealthy corporations and nations while burdening workers and debtor countries. Evidence: the WTO's closed governance, IMF austerity conditions attached to loans, child labor in cocoa supply chains, Rana Plaza garment factory deaths, Amazon warehouse exploitation.

Anti-globalists demanded **fair trade** (equitable prices for producers), **debt relief/restructuring** (freeing poor nations from IMF debt spirals), and **sustainable development** (profit without foreclosing future generations). Reformers like Muhammad Yunus proposed microcredit as a way to extend globalization's benefits to the poor rather than abandoning it.

### CDI — Cultural and Demographic Interactions

Globalization homogenized culture (See [[Unit-9.6/Globalized Culture Overview|Topic 9.6]]), provoking resistance from communities seeking to preserve local identity. The **Uighur** suppression in China represents an extreme case: a state enforcing cultural conformity against a Muslim minority. Brexit incorporated cultural anxieties about immigration changing British identity. Religious fundamentalism — Islamic, Christian, Hindu — grew partly as a reaction to perceived Western cultural dominance.

Digital technology became a contested terrain: activists used social media to organize resistance (Battle of Seattle, Manal al-Sharif's driving video), but states like China and Saudi Arabia weaponized platforms for surveillance and suppression. **Weibo** embodies this ambiguity.

### GOV — Governance and Sovereignty

A recurring complaint was that supranational institutions (EU, WTO, IMF) overrode national self-determination. Brexit was the clearest democratic expression of this: voters chose sovereignty over economic integration. Resistance also appeared in government censorship — China blocking Twitter/Facebook and Xinjiang policies — and in developing-world protests against IMF structural adjustment programs.

> [!exam-tip]
> For the AP, the sovereignty argument cuts across left and right: leftists wanted national governments to protect workers from multinational corporations; conservatives wanted national governments to control immigration and law. Brexit united both strands under one vote.

## Think As a Historian: Compare Points of View (AMSCO p. 695)

AMSCO presents two contrasting views on globalization — a key AP source-analysis exercise.

**Phil Condit (Boeing CEO, 1999):** "We are moving toward a global economy. One way of approaching that is to pull the covers over your head. Another is to say: It may be more complicated — but that's the world I am going to live in, I might as well be good at it."

*Point of view:* Corporate/executive. Condit frames globalization as inevitable and adaptation as the only rational response. His perspective reflects the interests of a multinational manufacturer that benefits from global supply chains and international markets. Dismissal of resistance as "pulling the covers over your head" reveals contempt for critics.

**James P. Hoffa (Teamsters Union president, 1998):** "What's going on in this country? Unions stand against those trends. We've got to somehow insulate the robust American economy from this global economy that seems to want to devour our standard of living."

*Point of view:* Labor/working class. Hoffa frames globalization as predatory — it "devours" living standards. His constituency is American workers threatened by offshoring and wage competition from cheaper overseas labor. He positions the union as a defensive wall protecting an existing standard of living.

> [!tip]
> AP source analysis requires you to explain how **point of view shapes the argument**. Both men write in 1998–1999 — the peak of anti-WTO activism. Condit wants deregulation; Hoffa wants protection. Both are responding to the same trends, but their economic positions determine their conclusions.

## Causation: Why Resistance Emerged

The AP learning objective asks you to *explain the causes* of resistance. Think in three registers:

**Economic causes:** Globalization produced winners (multinationals, shareholders, consumers of cheap goods) and losers (displaced workers, subsistence farmers, debtor nations). Supply-chain scandals (child labor, Rana Plaza) made the costs visible. IMF austerity conditions sparked protests across the developing world.

**Cultural causes:** The spread of American consumer culture and Western values threatened local identities. Religious fundamentalism, from Islamic revivalism to Hindu nationalism, positioned itself as a defense of tradition against secular Western modernity. Immigration flows tied to economic globalization triggered nativist backlash in receiving countries.

**Political causes:** Supranational institutions (EU, WTO, IMF) exercised authority that bypassed national democratic processes. This generated resentment across the political spectrum — the left saw corporate capture; the right saw infringement on sovereignty. Brexit was the most dramatic democratic expression of political anti-globalization.

## Comparison: Forms of Resistance Across Time

| Resistance | Period | Primary Driver | Target | Method |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zapatista uprising | 1994 | Indigenous/economic | NAFTA | Armed uprising + internet |
| Battle of Seattle | 1999 | Labor/environment | WTO | Mass protest |
| Anti-IMF protests | 1988–2002 | Economic (debt) | IMF/World Bank | Mass protest |
| Brexit | 2016 | Nationalism/sovereignty | EU | Democratic referendum |
| China's internet censorship | 2009–present | State control | Social media | Government policy |
| Uighur suppression | 2010s–present | State ethnic policy | Minority identity | Mass surveillance/detention |

> [!warning]
> Don't write that all anti-globalization resistance was the same. The AP rewards specificity: labor unions wanted fair wages within global trade; nationalists wanted out of trade deals entirely; environmentalists wanted sustainable production rules. These are different — and sometimes incompatible — goals.
