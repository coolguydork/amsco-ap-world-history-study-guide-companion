# Resistance to Globalization

**Unit 9 — Globalization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 688–695**

**AP Themes:** ECN (Economics), CDI (Cultural & Demographic Interactions), GOV (Governance)
**AP Skills:** Causation, Comparison

**Essential Question:** Why have groups resisted globalization, and what forms has that resistance taken?

---

## The Big Picture

Globalization's economic benefits were unevenly distributed, and by the late 20th century a broad coalition of opponents — labor unions, environmentalists, NGOs, nationalists, and governments — had organized to resist its effects. Resistance was not monolithic: some groups opposed the exploitation of workers; others rejected cultural homogenization; still others defended national sovereignty from supranational institutions. The AP exam tests students' ability to explain *why* resistance emerged (causation) and to *compare* different forms of resistance across regions and time periods.

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 9.7 pairs naturally with Topics 9.4 (Economics in the Global Age), 9.5 (Calls for Reform), and 9.6 (Globalized Culture). Expect LEQ and DBQ prompts asking you to explain causes of resistance OR to compare anti-globalization with earlier resistance movements (e.g., 8.7, 6.3).

## The Roots of Globalization and Anti-Globalization

After World War II, international organizations drove global economic integration: GATT (1947), the European Economic Community, Mercosur, and ASEAN all lowered tariff barriers and promoted free trade. In 1995, the **World Trade Organization (WTO)** absorbed GATT and came to govern more than 90% of international trade. Its opaque governance and closed board meetings bred suspicion that the WTO served corporate interests rather than workers or the environment.

> [!tip]
> AMSCO explicitly connects 17th-century mercantilism to 20th-century globalization (See [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview|Topic 4.5]]) — use this comparison on the AP exam to demonstrate historical reasoning across periods.

## Forms of Resistance

### Labor and Consumer Rights

The most visible driver of anti-globalization sentiment was the human cost of global supply chains. Consumers purchasing cheap goods rarely saw who made them or under what conditions.

- **Child labor** in West Africa supplied much of the world's cocoa. Major chocolate companies repeatedly missed self-imposed deadlines (2005, 2008, 2010) to eliminate child labor from their supply chains; by 2015 the U.S. Dept. of Labor estimated 2 million children worked in dangerous cocoa-growing conditions.
- The **Rana Plaza factory** collapse in Dhaka, Bangladesh (2013) killed over 1,100 garment workers making clothing for Western brands, becoming a global symbol of corporate irresponsibility. Nobel laureate **Muhammad Yunus** called it "a symbol of our failure as a nation" and proposed an international minimum wage.
- In developed countries, workers at **Amazon** warehouses (600,000+ employees) described extreme productivity quotas enforced at the cost of bathroom breaks (2019), illustrating that exploitative conditions were not limited to the Global South.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse Muhammad Yunus's *Grameen Bank* (microcredit for the poor) with his comments on Rana Plaza. Both are exam-relevant but represent different angles: microcredit as a *reform* to globalization's inequality; Rana Plaza as an *indictment* of its human costs.

### Environmental Resistance

Critics of globalization pointed to greenhouse gas emissions from long-distance shipping and mass deforestation — Brazil cleared thousands of square miles of rainforest annually to expand cattle farms for export. Proponents argued that globalization could fund conservation: **ecotourism** in Costa Rica demonstrated that profit and environmental preservation were compatible.

### Threats to National Sovereignty: Brexit

Conservative resistance to globalization centered on sovereignty and immigration. In 2016, 52% of British voters chose to leave the EU — a decision nicknamed **Brexit**. Supporters argued the EU forced Britain to accept immigrants and undermined its right to self-governance. Prime Minister **Theresa May** could not negotiate a withdrawal acceptable to Parliament and resigned in 2019. Brexit formalized in 2020.

> [!exam-tip]
> Brexit is the AP's go-to example of **nationalist resistance to supranational integration**. Compare it with economic nationalism in the 1930s (See [[Unit-7.4/Economy in the Interwar Period Overview|Topic 7.4]]) or post-WWI rejection of the League of Nations.

### Economic Resistance: Anti-IMF and Anti-WTO Movements

Critics argued the IMF and World Bank systematically favored rich creditor nations over poor debtor ones. Key moments:

- **1988:** ~20,000 protesters at IMF/World Bank meetings in West Berlin
- **1999 "Battle of Seattle":** 40,000+ protesters shut down the WTO's millennium trade summit — one of the first social movements coordinated via the internet
- **2001–2002:** Anti-IMF protests in 23 countries

Anti-globalists demanded **human rights**, **fair trade**, **sustainable development**, and **debt relief/debt restructuring** for heavily indebted nations.

### Social Media, Censorship, and Digital Resistance

Anti-globalization activists used the internet to organize globally. But governments also used digital tools for repression:

- **China:** After 2009 Uighur-Han riots in Urumqi, China banned Twitter and Facebook, introducing state-controlled **Weibo** as a substitute — a platform that enabled both citizen pushback and government surveillance.
- **Saudi Arabia:** Officials used social media to harass activists. Women's rights campaigner **Manal al-Sharif** warned that platforms originally meant for liberation were being weaponized for oppression.

> [!tip]
> Weibo is a double-edged sword: it lets citizens express grievances AND allows the state to track them. This tension — technology as both resistance tool and control mechanism — is a recurring AP theme connecting 9.1 (technology), 9.5 (reform), 9.6 (culture), and 9.7.

## What Anti-Globalists Demanded

The anti-globalization movement coalesced around four core demands:
1. **Human rights** — basic freedoms for all people, regardless of nationality
2. **Fair trade** — equitable payment for producers in the Global South
3. **Sustainable development** — profit-making that doesn't foreclose future generations
4. **Debt relief / debt restructuring** — freeing poor nations from crippling IMF obligations
