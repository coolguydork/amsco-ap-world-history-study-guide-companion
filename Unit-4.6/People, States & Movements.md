# People, States & Movements

## Overview

The period 1450–1750 produced a global cast of resistance leaders, each responding to specific state pressures — colonial conquest, religious coercion, serfdom, or demographic displacement. Knowing who resisted, where, and why is essential for AP comparison and causation questions. *(AMSCO pp. 243–247)*

---

## Ana Nzinga (c. 1583–1663) — Queen of Ndongo and Matamba

**Who:** African queen of the Mbundu people; ruler of Ndongo (present-day Angola) from 1624.

**Context:** Portugal had raided the region for enslaved Africans since the 15th century. Neighboring African kingdoms also threatened Ndongo. By the 17th century, the Dutch and English had pushed Portugal out of South Asia, turning Portuguese attention more intensively toward Africa.

**Actions:** Initially allied with Portugal — accepted Christian baptism with the Portuguese governor as godfather — to secure protection. When Portugal broke the alliance, Nzinga fled west, seized the kingdom of Matamba, and pivoted to resistance: she incited rebellion in Ndongo, allied with the Dutch (Portugal's rivals), and offered freedom to enslaved Africans who joined her.

**Significance:** Built Matamba into a powerful and economically prosperous state. Ruled for decades. Demonstrates African diplomatic sophistication — willingness to use alliance, religion, and military force as tools — and the limits of European power over determined African rulers.

> [!exam-tip]
> Ana Nzinga is a go-to example for "African responses to European imperialism." She used Christianity tactically, allied with competing European powers, and created a refuge state for escaped enslaved people. Always frame her as **agent**, not victim.

## Yemelyan Pugachev (1742–1775) — Cossack Rebel Leader

**Who:** Cossack soldier who led the largest peasant revolt in Russian history.

**Context:** Catherine the Great (r. 1762–1796) consolidated the Russian state by rewarding the nobility with increased power over serfs in exchange for political loyalty. This left the peasant mass — already bound to the land by the 1649 law code — even more exposed and resentful.

**Actions:** Pugachev falsely claimed to be Peter III (Catherine's murdered husband) to attract followers. He built a massive coalition: Cossacks, serfs, ethnic minorities (Bashkirs, Tatars), and other discontented groups. At its height, the Pugachev Rebellion (1773–1775) controlled territory between the Volga River and the Urals.

**Outcome:** Russian army crushed the rebellion. Pugachev was captured and publicly executed in Moscow.

**Significance:** The rebellion shocked the Russian court and temporarily appeared to threaten the empire. Catherine's response was to strengthen noble-serf relations even further, deepening the oppression that had caused the revolt. A classic example of how state repression of revolt can intensify the underlying grievance.

## Popé (c. 1630–1692) — Leader of the Pueblo Revolt

**Who:** Pueblo religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh pueblo, New Mexico.

**Context:** Spanish colonial rule imposed forced religious conversion, extracted coerced labor from Pueblo peoples, and suppressed indigenous religious practices. A series of earlier revolts had been crushed.

**Actions:** Popé secretly organized a coordinated uprising across 19 Pueblo communities. On August 10, 1680, the revolt erupted: Pueblo and Apache fighters killed approximately 400 Spaniards, expelled the rest from present-day New Mexico, and dismantled the apparatus of Spanish Christianity — burning churches, bathing in rivers to "erase" baptisms, and invalidating Christian marriages.

**Outcome:** Spain did not reconquer the region until 1692. When it returned, it was forced to make significant concessions on religious practice.

**Significance:** The Pueblo Revolt is one of the most successful indigenous revolts against European colonialism in North American history. It demonstrates that Spanish colonial power was not invincible and that it rested on coerced compliance, not genuine consent.

## Queen Nanny (c. 1680s–c. 1733) — Maroon Leader, Jamaica

**Who:** Leader of the Windward Maroons of Jamaica; born in Africa (likely Akan/Asante).

**Context:** England seized Jamaica from Spain in 1655. Formerly enslaved Africans who had escaped in the chaos — called Maroons — had established independent mountain communities. English plantation society pressured and attacked these communities.

**Actions:** Queen Nanny united the Maroon communities on the island and led guerrilla resistance against British colonial forces during the First Maroon War (1728–1740). Her tactical brilliance — using the mountainous terrain to her advantage — kept British forces at bay for over a decade, eventually forcing a peace treaty that recognized Maroon autonomy.

**Significance:** National hero of Jamaica. Her story is both a resistance narrative and a demonstration of African-descended political organization and military capability in the Americas. Compare her to Ana Nzinga — both used political skill and military resistance to carve out autonomous spaces under colonial pressure.

## Metacom / King Philip (c. 1638–1676) — Wampanoag Leader

**Who:** Son of Massasoit (the Wampanoag leader who initially allied with Plymouth colonists); later sachem (leader) of the Wampanoag people.

**Context:** Decades of English colonial expansion relentlessly squeezed Native American land. English colonists used debt, alcohol, and legal manipulation to transfer indigenous land — a pattern described in the quote that opens Topic 4.6. English population growth and colonial ambition made peaceful coexistence increasingly impossible.

**Actions:** Led the final major military effort by indigenous peoples to expel the English from New England (1675–1676). The war devastated 12 English towns and drew in dozens of tribes — some (Mohegan, Pequot) allied with the English against him.

**Outcome:** English forces and their Native allies defeated and killed Metacom in 1676. His head was displayed on a pike in Plymouth for 25 years. The Wampanoag people were subjugated.

**Significance:** Metacom's War is the bloodiest (per capita) war in American history. It ended significant Native American resistance to English colonialism in New England, but at devastating cost to both sides.

## Key States & Movements

**Matamba** — African kingdom (present-day Angola/DRC border region) seized and built by Ana Nzinga as a base of resistance against Portugal. Became a refuge for escaped enslaved Africans.

**Maratha Empire** — Hindu warrior state that emerged from the Deccan plateau to challenge Mughal rule, starting in 1680. Fought the Mughals through the late 17th and 18th centuries. Eventually ended Mughal dominance over South Asia; lasted until 1818 when the British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] defeated it.

**Wampanoag Confederacy** — Indigenous alliance in southern New England, led by Metacom during King Philip's War. Included multiple tribal groups; ultimately failed when the alliance fractured and some Native groups sided with English colonists.

**Maroon Communities (Jamaica)** — Independent settlements of formerly enslaved Africans and their descendants in the mountains of Jamaica. Waged two wars against English colonial rule (1728–1740; 1795–1796). The First Maroon War ended in a treaty recognizing Maroon autonomy.

> [!tip]
> **Comparison ready-made by AMSCO:** Ana Nzinga (Africa) and Queen Nanny (Jamaica) both created autonomous political spaces under colonial pressure, both used alliances strategically, and both have been reclaimed as national heroes. This is an excellent cross-regional comparison for an SAQ.
