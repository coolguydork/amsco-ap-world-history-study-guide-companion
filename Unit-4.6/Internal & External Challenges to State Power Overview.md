# Internal & External Challenges to State Power

## The Big Picture

As empires expanded and consolidated power between 1450 and 1750, they generated intense resistance from people inside and outside their borders. The very mechanisms states used to maintain control — forced labor, religious conversion, territorial conquest, heavy taxation — sparked the revolts that threatened to undo them. Topic 4.6 catalogs these challenges across every major empire of the era. *(AMSCO pp. 243–247)*

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves to ask you to *compare* resistance movements across different empires or *explain the cause* behind a specific revolt. Always tie the revolt back to the specific state policy that provoked it.

## Resistance to Portugal in Africa

Portugal's empire in Africa relied on slave raids and territorial pressure. In **1624**, **Ana Nzinga** became ruler of Ndongo (present-day Angola). Surrounded by Portuguese slave raids and attacks from neighboring African peoples, Nzinga initially allied with Portugal — accepting baptism with the Portuguese governor as her godfather — in exchange for protection. When Portugal broke the agreement, Nzinga pivoted dramatically: she fled west, seized the state of Matamba, incited rebellion in Ndongo, allied with the Dutch (Portugal's rival), and offered freedom to enslaved Africans who joined her. She built Matamba into an economically powerful independent state and ruled for decades. Her story illustrates that African rulers could deploy sophisticated diplomatic and military strategies against European encroachment.

## Local Resistance in Russia

Unlike the external pressures facing Portugal, Russia's challenges came from within. Russian serfdom had intensified over the 14th and 15th centuries as Mongol tribute demands and noble power weakened peasant autonomy. A 1649 law code formally chained Russian serfs to the land where they were born, making them virtually the property of their landlords — bought, sold, and punished at will. This stood in stark contrast to Western Europe, where Elizabeth I had freed England's last serfs by 1574.

The **Cossacks** — fierce, semi-independent warriors living on the steppes near the Black Sea, many of them runaway serfs — both served as Russian mercenaries and led resistance. In **1773**, Cossack leader **Yemelyan Pugachev** launched a massive peasant rebellion against Catherine the Great, falsely claiming to be her murdered husband Peter III. He rallied discontented serfs, ethnic minorities, and fellow Cossacks, briefly controlling territory between the Volga River and the Urals. The Russian army eventually crushed the revolt and executed Pugachev. Ironically, Catherine responded by tightening her alliance with the nobility and intensifying serf oppression.

> [!tip]
> The Pugachev Rebellion (1773) is a great SAQ example: cause = Catherine gave nobles power over serfs; effect = massive revolt, then even harsher repression.

## Rebellion in South Asia

The [[Unit-3.1/European, East Asian & Gunpowder Empires Expand Overview|Mughal Empire]] centralized authority over most of the Indian subcontinent, spreading Persian culture and Islam over a predominantly Hindu population. Starting in **1680**, the **Marathas** — a Hindu warrior group from the Deccan plateau — fought a long series of battles against the Mughals. They established the Maratha Empire, which lasted until 1818 and effectively ended Mughal dominance over India.

## Revolts in the Spanish Empire

Spain's empire in the Americas used forced religious conversion and coerced labor — practices the indigenous Pueblo people of present-day New Mexico resisted for decades. In **1680**, the **Pueblo Revolt** (led by Popé) erupted: Pueblo and Apache fighters killed approximately 400 Spaniards, expelled the rest from the region, and systematically destroyed churches and Christian symbols. They bathed in rivers to erase their baptisms and invalidated Christian marriages. Spain didn't reconquer the area until 1692, and even then had to make concessions on religious practice. This revolt is one of the most successful examples of indigenous resistance to European colonialism in North American history.

## Struggles for Power in England and Its Colonies

England's colonial territories generated multiple forms of resistance:

**Maroon Wars** — When England seized Jamaica from Spain in 1655, it inherited a population of formerly enslaved Africans who had escaped into the mountains. These Maroons, led by figures like **Queen Nanny**, waged two wars against English colonial authority (1728–1740 and 1795–1796). Jamaica later recognized Nanny as a national hero.

**Gloucester County Rebellion (1663)** — The first recorded slave revolt in North America, in which enslaved Africans and white indentured servants in Virginia conspired together to demand freedom from the colonial governor. Authorities uncovered and suppressed the plot.

**Metacom's War / King Philip's War (1675–1676)** — The final major effort by indigenous peoples to drive the English from New England. Led by Metacom (called King Philip by the English), the war destroyed 12 towns and spread across the region. Some Native groups (Mohegan, Pequot) sided with the English. The war ended with the subjugation of the Wampanoag people and the consolidation of English control over New England.

**Glorious Revolution (1688)** — Within England itself, the Catholic king James II alienated Protestant elites with anti-Protestant policies. Parliament invited the Dutch Protestant William of Orange (James's nephew and son-in-law) to invade. James fled to France, and William and Mary II began their joint rule in 1689. The revolution strengthened Parliament's power, including a law barring Catholics from the English throne. It was called "Glorious" and "Bloodless" — though religious tensions between Protestants and Catholics continued to ripple across the empire.
