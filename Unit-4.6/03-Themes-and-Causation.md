# Themes and Causation — Unit 4.6: (4.6): Internal and External Challenges to State Power

#apwh #unit-4 #period-4

---

## Governance

Imperial states faced a fundamental governance challenge in this period: how to control vast, diverse, distant territories with limited communication technology and small European populations. Internal resistance — from Indigenous peoples, enslaved Africans, and creole elites — forced constant policy adjustment. The Pueblo Revolt (1680) compelled Spain to moderate some religious and labor demands after reconquest; Tupac Amaru II's revolt (1780) accelerated administrative reforms; maroon communities forced colonial governments to negotiate peace treaties. These are not stories of state failure but of governance adaptation under pressure. External competition — from Protestant European powers challenging Catholic Iberian dominance — also forced continuous military and diplomatic innovation.

---

## Social Structures

Resistance took different forms depending on the social position of those resisting. Enslaved Africans could resist individually (foot-dragging, sabotage) or collectively (organized rebellions, flight to maroon communities). Indigenous peoples could rebel militarily, as in the Pueblo Revolt, or resist culturally by preserving religious practices beneath Catholic surfaces. Creole elites resisted through political lobbying and, eventually, independence movements. Each form of resistance reflected the specific constraints and resources available to different social groups — demonstrating that social structure shapes the forms resistance can take.

---

## Cultural Developments

Cultural resistance was a pervasive and often overlooked form of challenge to colonial power. Enslaved Africans maintained their religious traditions through syncretism (Candomblé, Vodou) and oral culture. Indigenous peoples preserved language, agricultural knowledge, and ritual practice despite missionary suppression. The existence of maroon communities allowed African cultural practices to survive in less adulterated forms. These cultural resistances were not merely symbolic — they maintained identities and communities that would eventually fuel more explicitly political resistance.

---

## Economic Systems

External competition between European powers was fundamentally economic. Privateering targeted Spanish silver fleets because silver was the foundation of Spanish imperial power. Dutch and English competition for the Asian spice trade and Caribbean plantation colonies was driven by the enormous profits these trades generated. The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) was, at its core, a global war over colonial markets and territories. The losers of inter-imperial competition — Portugal displaced by the Dutch in Southeast Asia, Spain harassed by English privateers — experienced economic contraction; the winners gained commercial advantages that compounded over generations.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP essays on resistance should always address **continuity and change**: resistance to colonial power was *continuous* throughout the period, but the *forms* of resistance changed over time (from military revolt to cultural preservation to political argument). This nuance earns complexity points.
