# Key Terms & Concepts

## Core Concept: Resistance to State Power

The expansion of empires between 1450 and 1750 forced diverse populations — enslaved people, peasants, indigenous communities, and religious minorities — into subordination. This topic's vocabulary centers on the forms of resistance they mounted and the institutions that drove them to revolt. *(AMSCO pp. 243–247)*

---

**Serfdom**
A system of bound agricultural labor in which peasants were legally tied to the land and subject to the authority of landowners. Russian serfs could be bought, sold, and punished by their lords. A 1649 Russian law code formalized this system, extending it as Russia expanded eastward. Distinguished from slavery primarily by attachment to land rather than the person of the owner — though in practice Russian serfs were close to enslaved. Elizabeth I had ended serfdom in England by 1574; Russia maintained it until 1861.

**Mirs**
Russian village communes that exercised collective control over small landholders and peasant life. Even peasants who technically owned land were subject to the mir's regulations, limiting their freedom of movement and economic autonomy. The mir reinforced the broader system of social control that made Russian peasants so vulnerable.

**Cossacks**
Semi-independent warriors living on the treeless steppes southwest of Moscow near the Black Sea. Many were runaway serfs who had adopted the nomadic ways of neighboring Mongol descendants. Cossacks served as Russian mercenaries — helping expand the empire toward the Urals and into Siberia — but also led internal rebellions against the tsars. Their dual role (imperial tool and rebel force) makes them a rich exam example.

**Pugachev Rebellion (1773)**
A massive peasant uprising led by the Cossack Yemelyan Pugachev against Catherine the Great. Pugachev falsely claimed to be Peter III (Catherine's murdered husband), rallying discontented serfs, ethnic minorities, and Cossacks. The rebellion briefly controlled territory between the Volga River and the Urals before the Russian army crushed it and executed Pugachev. Catherine's response was to tighten noble control over serfs, worsening peasant conditions.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Pugachev Rebellion shows that increased state power often generated internal challenges. Cause: Catherine gave nobles power over serfs. Effect: rebellion → then even harsher repression. Classic CCOT structure.

**Ndongo / Matamba**
Ndongo was an African kingdom in present-day Angola, ruled by Ana Nzinga from 1624. After her alliance with Portugal collapsed, Nzinga fled west and seized Matamba, transforming it into an economically powerful independent state. Matamba represented African agency in the face of European encroachment — a state built partly on offering refuge to escaped enslaved Africans.

**Maratha Empire**
A Hindu warrior state that rose in the Deccan plateau region of India, challenging Mughal power from 1680 onward. The Marathas fought a series of battles against the Mughals, eventually ending Mughal dominance over the subcontinent. The Maratha Empire lasted until 1818. It represents an internal religious challenge to the Mughal Islamic state from a predominantly Hindu population.

**Pueblo Revolt (1680)**
An uprising of Pueblo and Apache peoples in present-day New Mexico against Spanish colonial rule. The revolt — organized by the Pueblo leader Popé — was provoked by forced religious conversion and coerced labor. The indigenous fighters killed approximately 400 Spaniards, expelled the rest, and destroyed churches. Spain did not reconquer the region until 1692, and when it did, it made concessions on religious practice. The Pueblo Revolt is one of the most successful instances of indigenous resistance to European colonialism.

**Maroon Wars**
Conflicts between English colonial authorities in Jamaica and communities of Maroons — descendants of Africans who had escaped slavery, initially from Spanish-era plantations. The Maroons had established independent settlements in Jamaica's mountains. Two major wars occurred: 1728–1740 and 1795–1796. Queen Nanny unified the island's Maroon communities and led resistance; Jamaica later honored her as a national hero.

**Metacom's War (King Philip's War, 1675–1676)**
The final major military effort by indigenous peoples to drive the English from New England. Led by Wampanoag leader Metacom (called "King Philip" by English colonists), the war spread across New England and destroyed 12 towns. Some Native groups (Mohegan, Pequot) allied with the English. The war ended with the subjugation of the Wampanoag and consolidation of English colonial power in the region.

**Gloucester County Rebellion (1663)**
The first recorded slave revolt in North America (Virginia). Enslaved Africans and white indentured servants jointly conspired to demand freedom from the colonial governor — an early example of cross-racial solidarity among the unfree. Authorities discovered and suppressed the plot before it could act.

**Fronde (1648–1653)**
A series of civil disturbances in France in which noble factions attempted to curb the growing absolute power of the monarchy (during the regency of the young Louis XIV). The Fronde ultimately failed, and Louis XIV's subsequent absolute rule became even more centralized. It represents an internal elite challenge to state power.

**Glorious Revolution (1688)**
The largely peaceful overthrow of the Catholic king James II of England by Parliament, which invited the Dutch Protestant William of Orange to take the throne. William and his wife Mary II (James's daughter) began their joint reign in 1689. The revolution strengthened Parliament's authority, codified limits on royal power, and barred Catholics from the English throne. Called "Glorious" and "Bloodless" — though it had real consequences for Catholic minorities in England and Ireland.

> [!tip]
> The Glorious Revolution is an internal challenge to state power (a king deposed by his own Parliament) that also had religious dimensions (Protestant vs. Catholic). It leads directly to constitutional monarchy and later Enlightenment ideas.

**Wampanoag / Mohegan / Pequot**
Indigenous peoples of New England. The Wampanoag, led by Metacom, led resistance in Metacom's War. The Mohegan and Pequot chose alliance with the English during the war. Their different choices illustrate that indigenous responses to colonialism were not monolithic — tribal politics and rivalries shaped alliances.

**State Sovereignty**
The emerging principle (formalized by the Peace of Westphalia, 1648) that each state has supreme authority within its own territory and that outside powers have no right to interfere in internal affairs. This principle grew partly out of the chaos of the Thirty Years War and the religious conflicts of the era.
