# Practice & Review

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 refer to the passages below. (AMSCO p. 248)*

> **Source 1:** "This small Christian clan stranded in a tiny corner of the earth, surrounded by half-savage Mohammedan tribes and by soldiers, considers itself highly advanced, acknowledges none but Cossacks as human beings, and despises everybody else. The Cossack spends most of his time in the cordon [i.e., frontier fort], in [military] action, or in hunting and fishing."
> — Leo Tolstoy, *The Cossacks: A Tale of 1852*, published in 1863

> **Source 2:** "That night he stayed at home and dreamed, of the [American] West. His memory, coupled with what he had heard and idealized by his imagination, conjured dim visions of what he had once known and forgotten of a land where men and conditions harked back to the raw foundations of civilization."
> — B. M. Bower, *The Lure of the Dim Trails*, 1907

---

**Question 1.** One similarity between Cossacks and American cowboys was that both

A) lived on the frontier of an expanding country  
B) were criminals forced into exile by their government  
C) often formed alliances with the native inhabitants of their region  
D) represented the spread of urban society

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Both Cossacks and American cowboys lived on the expanding frontier of growing nation-states — Cossacks on Russia's steppe frontier (expanding toward Siberia), cowboys on the American West. Both Tolstoy and Bower emphasize the remoteness and frontier character of these groups. B is wrong — Cossacks were often runaway serfs, not exiled criminals. C is wrong — Cossacks frequently conflicted with neighboring peoples rather than allying with them. D is the opposite of what the passages suggest (both describe isolated, pre-urban environments).

---

**Question 2.** Why would Tsar Ivan IV (Ivan the Terrible) use Cossacks to expand the Russian empire into Siberia?

A) They lived in a harsh climate and could withstand the Siberian winters.  
B) They lived near Muslims and therefore could negotiate with Siberian Muslims.  
C) They had a tradition of being fierce warriors.  
D) They were Ukrainian and would defend Ivan from rebellions by Siberian Russians.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The Cossacks' defining characteristic — noted in both the AMSCO narrative and Tolstoy's description — was their reputation as fierce, skilled warriors. Ivan IV hired them as mercenaries precisely for this reason. A is a distractor: Cossacks lived on the Black Sea steppes, not Siberia, so the climate argument doesn't hold. B conflates geography and negotiation in an implausible way. D is historically inaccurate.

**Question 3.** The conflict between the Cossacks and Catherine the Great known as the Pugachev Rebellion was most similar to

A) the Mongol conquests because it was a fight over control of wealth  
B) the English Civil War because it was a fight between a monarch and a legislature  
C) the Thirty Years' War because it was a fight dominated by religious beliefs  
D) the conflicts in Japan during the Heian period in which feudal lords controlled the lives of the serfs

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The Pugachev Rebellion was fundamentally about a hierarchical social structure in which lords (nobles) controlled the lives of serfs — with the central state backing that control. This closely parallels the Japanese feudal system where lords controlled peasant/serf labor. A is wrong — Pugachev wasn't primarily about wealth; it was about social freedom and the brutal conditions of serfdom. B is wrong — the Pugachev Rebellion involved peasants and Cossacks vs. the imperial state, not a legislative body vs. a monarch. C is wrong — religion was not a primary driver of the Pugachev Rebellion.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: Robert Torrez, Former New Mexico State Historian (AMSCO p. 249)*

> "The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 was one of the most significant events in New Mexico history. But 1680 was not the first time New Mexico's Pueblos had attempted to rebel against the Spanish government. Beginning with the Acoma Revolt of 1599, Spanish intolerance of Pueblo religious practices and a persistent abuse of Pueblo labor had prompted several revolts against the Spanish in the seventeenth century. These uprisings, however, were discovered and ruthlessly crushed before they could grow into broader action. . . . The Spanish remained at El Paso until 1692. For a while it appeared that the revolt had indeed succeeded. Popay and the other Pueblo leaders began a systematic eradication of all signs of Christianity and Spanish material culture. Everyone was to bathe in a ritual which washed away any trace of baptism, and Christian marriages were invalidated until reconfirmed by native tradition. . . . But it was easier to order the eradication of all vestiges of the Spanish presence than to accomplish it. Many items of material culture which had been introduced by the Spanish — iron tools, sheep, cattle, and fruit trees, for example — had become an integral part of Pueblo life. A few individuals, deeply influenced by the teachings of the Franciscans, rescued and hid the sacred objects of their adopted religion to await the eventual return of the Spanish friars."

**Question A.** Identify ONE way in which the Pueblo Revolts were **similar** to the battles between the Marathas and the Mughals.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Both the Pueblo Revolt and the Maratha–Mughal wars were responses by indigenous/Hindu populations to the religious and cultural impositions of an imperial power. In both cases, the imperial power attempted to spread its religion (Christianity / Islam) over a subordinate population that maintained a distinct religious identity (Pueblo religion / Hinduism), and that population used military force to resist. Both revolts were also long-term struggles — the Pueblo engaged in multiple revolts before 1680, and the Marathas fought the Mughals across multiple decades.

**Question B.** Identify ONE way in which the Pueblo Revolts **differed** from the Cossack Revolts.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> The Pueblo Revolt was an external challenge to colonial state power — an indigenous people resisting a foreign empire (Spain) that had conquered their homeland and imposed alien religious practices. The Pugachev/Cossack Revolt was an internal challenge within a unified Russian state — serfs and Cossacks who were already subjects of the empire rebelling against the conditions of their subjugation (serfdom, noble power), not against foreign conquest or alien religion. In short: one was resistance to colonialism; the other was a class/labor revolt within a state.

**Question C.** Compare Torrez's view of the long-term impact of Spanish rule in New Mexico with the long-term impact of Metacom's War.

> [!tip] Model Response — CCOT / Comparison
> Torrez argues that the long-term impact of Spanish rule in New Mexico was characterized by complex cultural blending: though the Pueblos successfully expelled Spain temporarily, Spanish material culture (iron tools, livestock, fruit trees) had become permanently integrated into Pueblo life, and some individuals retained Catholic religious attachments. The revolt "succeeded" politically but could not eradicate all cultural change. By contrast, the long-term impact of Metacom's War was near-total English political dominance — the Wampanoag were subjugated, English towns were rebuilt, and indigenous resistance to English colonialism in New England was broken. Where Torrez emphasizes cultural persistence and hybridization alongside political failure, Metacom's War resulted in more decisive political defeat with less evidence of reciprocal cultural integration.

## SAQ Self-Practice

**Question 2 from AMSCO p. 249** *(answer without looking — then check against your notes)*

> A) Identify ONE example of how social structures affected a state's ability to maintain order in settlements in the period 1500–1750.
>
> B) Identify ONE example of how economic structures affected a state's ability to maintain order in settlements in the period 1500–1750.
>
> C) Explain ONE historical situation in the period 1500–1750 in which state power was challenged by internal pressures.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> **A)** Russia's serfdom system (1649 law code) gave the nobility legal control over peasants, which helped the tsar maintain order by delegating enforcement to landlords — but also generated resentment that fueled the Pugachev Rebellion. Social hierarchy enabled short-term control while creating long-term instability.
>
> **B)** Demand for grain in Russia and Atlantic sugar/tobacco in the Americas both intensified labor extraction systems (serfdom; plantation slavery). These economic pressures increased coercion — contributing directly to revolts like the Pugachev Rebellion, Maroon Wars, and Gloucester County Rebellion. Economic extraction destabilized the social order states relied on.
>
> **C)** The Glorious Revolution (1688) is a clear internal challenge to state power: English parliamentary elites overthrew their own Catholic king (James II) and replaced him with a Protestant monarch (William of Orange) who agreed to constitutional limits on royal authority. The challenge came from within the political elite — not from colonized subjects — and permanently restructured the English state.

## Think As a Historian: Situate in Context *(AMSCO p. 250)*

The AMSCO skill exercise asks you to connect each revolt to the broader features of empire-building (from [[Unit-4.1/Technological Innovations Overview|Topic 4.1]]):

| Feature of Empire | How It Relates to 4.6 Revolts |
|-------------------|---------------------------------|
| Empires expand by conquest | Creates displaced/subjugated peoples → Pueblo, Metacom, Maroon revolts |
| Empires impose cultural/religious norms | Forced conversion generates religious resistance → Pueblo, Glorious Revolution, Marathas |
| Empires extract labor/tribute | Bound labor creates class revolt → serfdom/Pugachev, plantation slavery/Maroon Wars |
| Empires face rival powers | Resistance movements ally with empire's rivals → Nzinga allied with Dutch vs. Portugal |
