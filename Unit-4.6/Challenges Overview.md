# Topic 4.6 – Internal and External Challenges to State Power

**Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections | Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)**

## Learning Objectives

- Explain how the development of state power produced internal and external challenges in the period 1450–1750.
- Identify specific revolts, rebellions, and resistance movements across multiple regions.
- Analyze the causes and outcomes of challenges to state authority.
- Connect resistance movements to larger patterns of empire and labor exploitation.

## The Big Picture

As European states expanded their empires and Asian states consolidated their power, resistance arose everywhere — from within empires, from colonized peoples, and from marginal groups (serfs, enslaved peoples, indigenous nations). Understanding these challenges is essential for AP World History because they reveal the limits of state power and the human agency of people who refused to accept exploitation.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks about resistance to imperial or state power. For each rebellion in this topic, be able to answer: **Who resisted? Why? Against whom? What was the outcome?** These four questions make for strong analytical writing.

## Resistance to Portugal in Africa: Ana Nzinga

By the 17th century, the Dutch and English had pushed Portugal out of South Asia. Portugal looked to Africa — where it had conducted slave raids since the 15th century — to build a territorial colony (in what is now Angola).

**Ana Nzinga** became ruler of **Ndongo** in south-central Africa in 1624. She faced Portuguese slave raids and attacks from other African peoples. Her response was sophisticated:
- She initially allied with Portugal, converting to Christianity (with the Portuguese governor as her godfather), in exchange for protection and an end to slave raiding
- When the alliance broke down, she fled west and took over the state of **Matamba**
- She then incited a rebellion in Ndongo, allied with the Dutch, and offered freedom to runaway slaves who joined her

Ana Nzinga ruled for decades, building Matamba into an economically strong and diplomatically active state. She represents the complex, strategic response of African rulers to European expansion — using diplomacy, religion, alliance-switching, and military resistance.

> [!tip]
> Ana Nzinga is an excellent example for essays about resistance to European expansion. She wasn't just a military leader — she was a shrewd diplomat who used European rivalries (Portugal vs. the Netherlands) to protect her state. This shows the agency of African rulers in navigating (and sometimes exploiting) European competition.

## Resistance in Russia: Serfdom and Rebellion

While Portugal faced external challenges in Africa, Russia faced internal challenges from its peasant-serf system.

**Russian serfdom**: While conditions for serfs improved in Western Europe by the 14th century, Russia moved in the opposite direction. Wars weakened central authority; nobles grew more powerful; demand for grain increased, driving nobles to impose harsher conditions on serfs. A **law of 1649** chained Russian serfs to the land where they were born, ensuring service to landlords who could buy, sell, and punish them. Village communes (**mirs**) further controlled the small landholders.

**The Cossacks**: On the steppes southwest of Moscow, runaway serfs and descendants of Mongol nomads had formed fierce, independent warrior communities — the **Cossacks**. They were sometimes at odds with the tsarist government and sometimes hired as mercenaries. Cossacks were central to Russia's eastward expansion toward the Urals and Siberia.

**The Pugachev Rebellion (1773–1775)**: Cossack leader **Yemelyan Pugachev** launched a massive peasant uprising against Catherine the Great. He falsely claimed to be Catherine's murdered husband, Peter III, and gathered discontented peasants, ethnic minorities, and Cossacks — at one point controlling territory between the Volga River and the Urals. The Russian army eventually captured and executed Pugachev. The rebellion caused Catherine to **increase oppression of peasants** in exchange for noble support — the opposite of liberalization.

> [!warning]
> The Pugachev Rebellion is a counterintuitive story. The peasants' uprising led not to reform, but to *increased* oppression. This is a common pattern: failed revolts often strengthen the very systems they challenge, at least in the short term. The AP exam may ask about outcomes of rebellions, not just causes.

## Rebellion in South Asia: Maratha vs. Mughal

In the 16th and 17th centuries, the **Mughal Empire** controlled much of India. The Mughals had centralized government and spread Persian art, culture, and Islam. But much of the population remained Hindu.

**The Maratha** — a Hindu warrior group from the Deccan Plateau — fought the Mughals in a series of battles from 1680–1707, creating the **Maratha Empire** (a Hindu confederacy). The Maratha Empire lasted until 1818, effectively ending Mughal rule of India and opening the way for British colonial expansion.

*Significance*: This internal challenge to the Mughal Empire shows how religious identity (Hindu vs. Muslim) combined with regional political ambition to fragment even the most powerful Asian empires.

## Revolts in the Spanish Empire

### Pueblo Revolt (1680)
The **Pueblo** and **Apache** — two indigenous groups in what is now New Mexico — revolted against Spanish colonizers who were trying to force religious conversions. They killed about 400 Spaniards, destroyed churches, and drove the Spanish out of the region. The Spanish reconquered the area in 1692.

*Significance*: The Pueblo Revolt was one of the most successful indigenous revolts against European colonization. It forced Spain to modify its approach to indigenous governance in the region — and it temporarily preserved Pueblo religious and cultural practices.

### Maroon Wars (1728–1740 and 1795–1796)
**Maroons** were descendants of runaway enslaved Africans in Jamaica. They escaped their owners and formed independent settlements in the mountains. **Queen Nanny** — herself an escaped slave — united the Maroons of Jamaica into an effective military force, earning recognition as a national hero.

The Maroon wars against British colonial authority were partially successful: the British eventually negotiated peace treaties acknowledging Maroon autonomy in certain territories.

### Gloucester County Rebellion (1663)
The first recorded slave revolt in what is now the United States. In Virginia, enslaved Africans and **white indentured servants** conspired together to demand freedom from the governor. Authorities discovered the plot and arrested the conspirators before it could be carried out.

*Significance*: This reveals that resistance to colonial labor exploitation was not limited to enslaved Africans — poor whites also resisted. It also shows the colonial authorities' fear of cross-racial solidarity (after this, colonial laws increasingly separated the legal treatment of Black enslaved people from white indentured servants).

### Metacom's War / King Philip's War (1675–1678)
**Metacom** (called "King Philip" by the English), the Wampanoag leader, led the final major indigenous effort to drive the British from New England. The war spread throughout New England, destroying 12 towns. Some Native American groups (Mohegan, Pequot) sided with the English. The war ended with the subjugation of the Wampanoag people.

The English had used underhanded tactics — including getting indigenous peoples drunk to cheat them in bargains — to steadily encroach on Native American land. This combination of land loss, economic manipulation, and cultural pressure drove indigenous resistance.

## Struggles Within England: The Glorious Revolution (1688–1689)

Challenges to state power also came from within European countries themselves. In England:

**James II** (became king 1685) was Catholic in a Protestant country. His anti-Protestant measures enraged much of the population and Parliament. English nobles invited **William of Orange** (James's nephew and son-in-law) to invade and take the throne. James fled to France. In 1689, **William** and **Mary II** (both Protestant) began joint rule of England.

The **Glorious Revolution** (also called the Bloodless Revolution) was significant because:
- It **strengthened Parliament's power** over the monarch
- A law was passed forbidding Catholics from ruling England
- It established the precedent of **constitutional monarchy** — a king or queen whose power is limited by Parliament and law

*Significance for AP*: The Glorious Revolution is a key moment in the development of limited government and representative political institutions — ideas that would inspire the American and French Revolutions a century later.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Glorious Revolution is often tested in relation to later political transformations. Know that it: (1) limited royal power, (2) strengthened Parliament, (3) established religious tests for monarchy. Then connect it forward to the American and French Revolutions.

## AP Themes

**Governance**: State power was always contested. Even the most powerful empires faced challenges from colonized peoples, peasants, religious minorities, and rival ethnic groups. The outcome of these challenges shaped the long-term structure of empires.

**Social Structures**: Rebellions often crossed social lines — Gloucester County united enslaved Africans and white indentured servants; Pugachev united serfs, Cossacks, and ethnic minorities. These cross-group alliances reveal shared experiences of oppression.

**Cultural Developments**: Religious identity drove many challenges — Maratha Hindus vs. Muslim Mughals; Catholic James II vs. Protestant England; indigenous Pueblo peoples vs. forced Catholic conversion.
