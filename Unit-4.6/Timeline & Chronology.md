# Timeline & Chronology

## Key Dates — Internal & External Challenges to State Power

*(AMSCO pp. 243–247; curated from ref_4_6.md)*

| Date | Event | Significance |
|------|-------|--------------|
| 1517 | Luther posts 95 Theses | Protestant Reformation begins; fractures religious unity of Western Christendom — seeds the religious conflicts that drive multiple 4.6 revolts |
| 1534 | Henry VIII breaks with Rome | Church of England founded; state authority over religion established in England — sets stage for later Protestant–Catholic tension |
| 1574 | Elizabeth I frees last English serfs | Marks the divergence between Western Europe (ending serfdom) and Russia (intensifying it) |
| 1618–1648 | Thirty Years War | Devastating religious-political conflict in the Holy Roman Empire; reshapes European state boundaries |
| 1624 | Ana Nzinga becomes ruler of Ndongo | Begins Africa's most celebrated case of diplomatic and military resistance to Portuguese colonialism |
| 1648 | Peace of Westphalia | Established principle of state sovereignty; ended major religious wars in Europe |
| 1648–1653 | Fronde (France) | Noble faction civil war against growing royal power (regency of Louis XIV); ultimately failed |
| 1649 | Russian law code chains serfs to land | Formalizes Russian serfdom — direct cause of later peasant revolts |
| c. 1655 | England seizes Jamaica | Creates conditions for Maroon wars; enslaved population inherits from Spanish era |
| 1663 | Gloucester County Rebellion (Virginia) | First recorded slave revolt in North America; enslaved Africans + white indentured servants conspired together |
| 1675–1676 | Metacom's War (King Philip's War) | Final major indigenous effort to expel English from New England; 12 towns destroyed; Wampanoag subjugated |
| 1680 | Pueblo Revolt | Pueblo + Apache expel Spain from New Mexico; ~400 Spaniards killed; churches destroyed |
| 1680–1707 | Maratha–Mughal wars begin | Marathas challenge Mughal rule; eventually end Mughal dominance over South Asia |
| 1685 | James II becomes king of England | Catholic king; anti-Protestant policies trigger elite revolt |
| 1688 | Glorious Revolution | William of Orange invited to invade England; James II flees; Parliament strengthened |
| 1689 | William and Mary II begin joint rule | Protestant succession secured; constitutional monarchy reinforced |
| 1692 | Spain reconquers New Mexico | 12 years after Pueblo Revolt; returns with religious concessions |
| 1728–1740 | First Maroon War (Jamaica) | Queen Nanny leads guerrilla resistance; ends in treaty recognizing Maroon autonomy |
| 1773–1775 | Pugachev Rebellion | Largest peasant revolt in Russian history; Cossack-led; crushed by Russian army |
| 1795–1796 | Second Maroon War (Jamaica) | Second major conflict between Maroons and English colonial forces |
| 1818 | Maratha Empire ends | British East India Company defeats Marathas; British dominance over India established |

## Chronological Patterns to Know

**Early period (1500s):** Reformation sets up religious conflicts in Europe; Atlantic colonialism begins generating resistance in Africa and the Americas.

**Mid-period (1600s):** Revolts proliferate — Fronde, Nzinga, Metacom, Gloucester County, Pueblo Revolt, Glorious Revolution all cluster in this century. Peak of colonial expansion → peak of colonial resistance.

**Late period (1700s):** Pugachev Rebellion (1773) — internal Russian challenge. Maroon Wars continue. Maratha Empire still fighting until 1818.

> [!tip]
> Notice that resistance movements across different continents peak in roughly the same period (1640s–1690s): Fronde (1648), Metacom (1675), Pueblo (1680), Glorious Revolution (1688). This is not coincidence — it reflects the maturation of European empire-building creating pressure that exploded simultaneously in multiple places.

> [!exam-tip]
> The exam loves to ask you to **contextualize** a single revolt. For any revolt here, you can say: "This occurred in the broader context of European imperial expansion in the 16th–17th centuries, in which states used [forced labor / religious conversion / territorial displacement] to consolidate power, generating systematic resistance from those they subjugated."
