# Topic 4.6 – Key Terms and People

**Unit 4: Transoceanic Interconnections | Period 2 (c. 1450–c. 1750)**

## Key People

**Ana Nzinga (c. 1583–1663)**
Queen of Ndongo and Matamba (in present-day Angola). Resisted Portuguese slave raids through a combination of military resistance, diplomacy, religious conversion (as a negotiating tactic), alliance with the Dutch, and offering freedom to runaway enslaved people. Built Matamba into a strong, independent state. *Significance*: A premier example of African resistance to European expansion; demonstrates that African rulers were sophisticated agents, not passive victims.

> [!exam-tip]
> Ana Nzinga is one of the AP exam's favorite figures. She represents: African resistance, diplomatic agency, and the complexity of African-European relations (not simply victims vs. oppressors). She converted to Christianity as a political tool, not necessarily as a sincere conversion — this shows strategic thinking about European power.

**Yemelyan Pugachev (c. 1742–1775)**
Cossack leader who launched the Pugachev Rebellion (1773–1775) against Catherine the Great's Russia, falsely claiming to be the murdered Tsar Peter III to gain followers. His coalition of discontented serfs, Cossacks, and ethnic minorities briefly controlled large territories before being defeated. *Significance*: The largest peasant uprising in Russian history before the 20th century; its failure led to *increased* oppression rather than reform.

**Queen Nanny (c. 1686–1733)**
Escaped enslaved African woman who became a military leader of the Maroons in Jamaica, uniting runaway enslaved communities against British colonial authority. Recognized today as a Jamaican national hero. *Significance*: Example of women's leadership in resistance movements; shows enslaved Africans' active resistance to their condition.

**Metacom ("King Philip") (c. 1638–1676)**
Wampanoag leader in New England who led Metacom's War (1675–1678), also called King Philip's War — the last major indigenous military effort to drive the British from New England. Killed in 1676. *Significance*: Represents indigenous resistance; the failure of the war ended organized Native American resistance in New England and led to the subjugation of the Wampanoag.

**James II of England (1633–1701)**
Catholic English king who alienated Parliament and the Protestant establishment with anti-Protestant measures. Overthrown in the Glorious Revolution (1688). Fled to France. *Significance*: His removal demonstrated that in England, Parliament could override monarchical power — a precedent for constitutional governance.

**William of Orange (William III, 1650–1702)**
Dutch-born Protestant prince invited by English nobles to take the English throne in 1688. Landed in England; James II fled. Became king jointly with his wife Mary II. *Significance*: His reign institutionalized parliamentary power in England and established Protestant rule as a constitutional requirement.

## Key Events

**Pueblo Revolt (1680)**
Indigenous revolt in present-day New Mexico by Pueblo and Apache peoples against Spanish colonizers who were forcing religious conversions. Killed ~400 Spaniards; destroyed churches; drove Spain out of the region temporarily. Spain reconquered in 1692. *Significance*: One of the most successful indigenous revolts in colonial history; forced Spain to moderate religious coercion in the region.

**Maroon Wars (1728–1740 and 1795–1796)**
Military conflicts between British colonial forces and Maroon communities (descendants of escaped enslaved Africans) in Jamaica. Led partly by Queen Nanny. British eventually negotiated partial autonomy for Maroon communities. *Significance*: Example of enslaved people successfully resisting colonial authority; Maroon communities preserved elements of African culture.

**Metacom's War / King Philip's War (1675–1678)**
Major indigenous war against British colonization of New England. Widespread destruction on both sides; 12 towns destroyed. British won with help from allied Native American groups (Mohegan, Pequot). *Significance*: Ended organized indigenous resistance in New England; showed both the limits of indigenous power and the divisions among Native American groups.

**Pugachev Rebellion (1773–1775)**
Massive peasant uprising led by Cossack Yemelyan Pugachev against Catherine the Great in Russia. Temporarily controlled large territories in the Volga region and Urals. Crushed by the Russian army. *Significance*: The failed rebellion led Catherine to *increase* serf oppression — a lesson in how failed revolts can backfire.

**Gloucester County Rebellion (1663)**
First recorded slave revolt in what is now the United States. Enslaved Africans and white indentured servants in Virginia conspired together to demand freedom. Plot discovered before execution; conspirators arrested. *Significance*: Shows cross-racial solidarity in resistance to colonial labor exploitation; colonial authorities responded by legally separating the status of Black enslaved people from white laborers.

**Glorious Revolution (1688–1689)**
The bloodless replacement of Catholic King James II of England by Protestant rulers William and Mary. Parliament passed laws forbidding Catholics from ruling England. *Significance*: Strengthened parliamentary power; established the principle of constitutional monarchy; influenced later democratic revolutions.

**Fronde (1648–1653)**
Civil disturbances in France that attempted to curb the growing power of French royalty. Failed; Louis XIV used the memory of the Fronde to justify his policy of keeping nobles and common people in check through absolute monarchy.

## Key Concepts

**Sakoku**
Japan's policy of near-isolation from the rest of the world, implemented gradually in the early 17th century and lasting until the mid-19th century. Japan expelled most foreigners, banned foreign books, and prohibited Japanese from traveling abroad. Only Dutch merchants (in Nagasaki) and Chinese traders maintained limited contact.

**Cossacks**
Fierce warrior communities on the steppes of Russia, descended partly from runaway serfs and partly from Mongol nomadic traditions. Sometimes fought against the tsarist government; sometimes served as mercenaries. Key to Russia's eastward expansion toward the Urals and Siberia.

**Mirs**
Village communes in Russia that controlled peasant communities, including small landholders. Part of the system of social control over the serf population. *Significance*: Shows how Russian serfdom operated through community-level control, not just individual landlord-serf relationships.

**Maroons**
Communities of escaped enslaved people in the Caribbean and Americas who formed independent settlements, usually in mountains or forests inaccessible to colonial authorities. *Examples*: Jamaican Maroons, Brazilian quilombos. *Significance*: Represent active, organized resistance to slavery; preserved African cultural practices.

**Maratha Empire**
A Hindu confederacy created by the Maratha warrior group after defeating the Mughal Empire in a series of battles (1680–1707). Lasted until 1818, effectively ending Mughal rule of India. *Significance*: Shows how internal religious and regional conflict weakened even the most powerful Asian empires.
