# Unit 3 — Land-Based Empires (c. 1450–c. 1750)

> [!summary] Unit 3 Big Picture
> Between 1450 and 1750, a handful of powerful states rose to dominate the Eurasian landmass — not through conquest alone, but through **gunpowder, bureaucracy, and ideology**. The Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal Empires, along with Russia and Qing China, built the largest land-based empires the world had ever seen. They governed diverse populations through professional militaries, centralized administrations, and religious legitimacy. And they all, eventually, declined — as European maritime power began reshaping the world around them.

## Why Unit 3 Matters for the AP Exam

Unit 3 is one of the most **comparison-heavy** units on the AP exam. The College Board wants you to:
- Compare how different empires **expanded** (Topic 3.1)
- Compare how different empires **governed** (Topic 3.2)
- Analyze how **religion** both united and divided empires (Topic 3.3)
- Make **cross-empire comparisons** on military, taxation, legitimacy, and decline (Topic 3.4)

Almost every Period 2 LEQ asks you to compare at least two empires. Know the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal cold — and be ready to bring in Russia, China, Japan, the Aztecs, or the Incas for contrast.

## The Core Story of Unit 3

### Act 1 — The Rise (Topic 3.1)
The year 1450 opened a new era. The Gutenberg press, the end of the Hundred Years' War, and the waning of plague waves changed Europe. Across Eurasia, the collapse of Mongol khanates left power vacuums that Turkic warriors — armed with gunpowder — were perfectly positioned to fill. The Ottoman Empire seized Constantinople in 1453 with a 26-foot bronze cannon. The Safavids conquered Persia by 1501. Babur (Tamerlane's descendant) invaded India in the 1520s. Russia expanded east to Siberia. The Qing Dynasty unified China. By 1600, the entire Eurasian landmass was carved up among massive, powerful states.

### Act 2 — The Machinery of Empire (Topic 3.2)
Conquering was one challenge. Governing diverse, multiethnic empires was another. Every successful empire in this period invented mechanisms to do it: the Ottoman **devshirme** (forcibly recruiting Christian boys as loyal civil servants and Janissaries), France's **intendants**, China's **civil service exam**, Japan's **Tokugawa hostage system**, and the Mughal **zamindars**. All used religion, architecture, and art to project legitimacy — the Taj Mahal, the Suleymaniye Mosque, the Palace of Versailles.

### Act 3 — Faith as a Force (Topic 3.3)
Religion was the era's most powerful ideological force — and its most dangerous fault line. Christianity split into Catholic and Protestant factions, triggering decades of war culminating in the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648). Islam deepened its Sunni-Shi'a divide as the Ottoman and Safavid empires fought each other for control of the Middle East. In India, Akbar's tolerance gave way to Aurangzeb's intolerance — and the Mughal Empire fractured. Meanwhile, the Scientific Revolution began quietly building an entirely different framework for understanding the world.

### Act 4 — Comparison and Decline (Topic 3.4)
By 1750, the pattern was clear: all these empires had built on similar foundations (gunpowder, bureaucracy, religious legitimacy, tax extraction) and were eroding through similar weaknesses (weak successors, corrupt tax systems, overtaxed peasants, failure to modernize navies). The Gunpowder Empires that couldn't adapt — Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal — declined as European maritime power rose. Russia, which *did* modernize, survived.

## The Three Islamic Gunpowder Empires: Quick Reference

| | Ottoman | Safavid | Mughal |
|--|---------|---------|--------|
| **Location** | Turkey, Balkans, N. Africa | Persia (Iran) | India |
| **Religion** | Sunni Islam | Shi'a Islam | Sunni (Akbar: tolerant) |
| **Peak Ruler** | Suleiman I (1520–1566) | Shah Abbas I (1588–1629) | Akbar (1556–1605) |
| **Admin System** | Devshirme / Janissaries | Ghulams / "men of the pen" | Zamindars |
| **Iconic Monument** | Suleymaniye Mosque | Mosque of Isfahan | Taj Mahal |
| **Cause of Decline** | Weak sultans; harem politics | Ineffectual successors; Afghan revolt | Aurangzeb's intolerance; British penetration |

## AP Themes Covered in Unit 3

- **Governance** — devshirme, civil service exam, zamindars, intendants, Tokugawa shogunate, absolutism vs. constitutional monarchy
- **Economic Systems** — tax farming, tribute systems, zamindar corruption, Ming silver currency
- **Cultural Developments** — Taj Mahal, Suleymaniye Mosque, Versailles; Protestant Reformation; Scientific Revolution
- **Social Structures** — caste system (India); serfdom (Russia); slave soldiers (Janissaries, Ghulams); samurai (Japan)
- **Technology & Environment** — gunpowder as the defining technology; Gutenberg printing press enabling Reformation
- **Religion** — Protestant Reformation; Counter-Reformation; Sunni-Shi'a conflict; Sikhism; Akbar's tolerance vs. Aurangzeb's intolerance
