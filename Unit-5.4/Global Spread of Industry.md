# Topic 5.4 — Global Spread of Industry

## Two Stories at Once

Topic 5.4 tells a tale of two industrial trajectories happening simultaneously in the 19th century:

1. **Industrialization spreading** — Europe, North America, Russia, Japan
2. **Deindustrialization occurring** — India, Egypt, and other regions under European pressure

Understanding both stories — and *why* each happened — is essential for AP essays on this topic.

> [!exam-tip] When writing about the spread of industrialization, always explain the *mechanism* of spread: Was it private investment? State action? Technology transfer? Colonial pressure? The AP rewards specific causal reasoning.

---

## Story 1 — Industrialization Spreading

### Belgium and France
**Belgium** was the first country after Britain to industrialize, benefiting from proximity to Britain, access to British machinery and expertise, and its own coal deposits.

**France** lagged for two key reasons:
- Sparse urban population meant limited factory labor
- The French Revolution (1789–1799) consumed capital and government attention for over a decade

France did industrialize eventually — but more slowly, and with greater state involvement than Britain's market-driven model.

### Germany — From Fragmentation to Industrial Giant

Before 1871, "Germany" was dozens of separate states. Each state had its own currency, laws, and tariffs — making large-scale industrial investment risky and market expansion difficult.

**The Zollverein** (customs union, 1834) began breaking down trade barriers between German states, paving the way for unification.

**After German Unification (1871) under Otto von Bismarck:**
- A single national market emerged overnight
- The Ruhr Valley's massive coal and iron deposits could now fuel truly national industrial growth
- Germany invested heavily in technical education and state-supported industry
- Within a generation, Germany was producing **more steel than Britain**

> [!tip] Germany's rapid post-1871 industrialization is the best example of how political unification can unlock economic transformation. Compare with Italy (also unified in the 1860s–70s, also saw industrial gains).

### The United States — Industrial Giant by 1900

The United States had all the ingredients:
- **Vast natural resources** (coal, iron, timber, agricultural land)
- **Growing population** through massive immigration (especially from Southern and Eastern Europe, and from China for railroad construction)
- **Transcontinental Railroad** (1869) — linked coasts, opened interior markets, and required massive steel production
- **[[Laissez-faire]] policy** — government mostly stayed out of business decisions (but heavily subsidized railroads with land grants)

By 1900, the United States produced **more manufactured goods than any country in the world** — surpassing Britain, the original industrial leader.

> [!warning] Don't forget the human cost of American industrialization: brutal factory conditions, child labor, exploitation of immigrant workers, and the forced labor of Chinese railroad workers. The economic miracle had serious social dimensions.

### Russia — Top-Down, State-Driven

Russia's industrialization looked very different from Britain's or America's:

**Who drove it?** The Russian state (under Tsar Alexander III and his finance minister Sergei Witte), not private entrepreneurs.

**Focus:** Railroads above all else.
- 36,000+ miles of railroad track by 1900
- The **Trans-Siberian Railroad** — an engineering marvel connecting Moscow to Vladivostok on the Pacific coast
- Railroad construction required steel → steel production grew
- By 1900, Russia was the **4th largest steel producer** in the world

**The fundamental tension:** Despite this industrial growth, Russia's economy remained **overwhelmingly agricultural**. Most Russians were peasants. Industry was concentrated in a few cities (St. Petersburg, Moscow). This tension between a modern industrial sector and a backward agrarian economy was a key cause of the 1917 revolutions.

> [!exam-tip] Russia is a great example for CCOT essays: industrial *change* (railroads, steel) alongside economic *continuity* (agricultural majority, peasant society, autocratic government).

### Japan — The Most Remarkable Transformation

Japan's industrialization is perhaps the most dramatic story in 19th-century world history.

**Before 1853:** Japan had been largely isolated from Europe for two centuries under the Tokugawa shogunate. Traditional, feudal, and agricultural.

**The shock of 1853:** American Commodore Matthew Perry arrived with steam-powered warships and forced Japan to open its ports to trade. Japanese leaders saw immediately that their military technology was generations behind.

**The [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] (1868):**
- Young samurai and reformers overthrew the shogunate and restored imperial rule under Emperor Meiji
- The new government launched a top-down program of **defensive modernization**:
  - Sent hundreds of students abroad to study Western science, law, and industry
  - Hired Western experts to advise Japanese industry, military, and universities
  - Built railroads, textile factories, and steel works
  - Created a modern conscript army and navy (modeled on Germany and Britain respectively)
  - Adopted a constitution (1889) modeled partly on the Prussian constitution

**Results by 1900:** Japan had industrialized so successfully that it:
- Defeated **China** in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95)
- Defeated **Russia** in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) — shocking the world
- Colonized Korea and Taiwan
- Joined the ranks of imperial powers

> [!tip] Japan's success shows that industrialization was not exclusively a Western phenomenon — but it required deliberate, sustained state action and willingness to borrow selectively from the West.

## Story 2 — Deindustrialization

### The Mechanism
Deindustrialization in Asia and the Middle East happened through two related mechanisms:
1. **Market competition** — cheap, machine-made European goods undercut traditional hand-crafted production
2. **Colonial policy** — deliberate regulations, tariffs, and institutional controls that favored European industry over local production

### India — Three Industries Destroyed

**Textiles (the most important case):**
India had the world's most sophisticated hand-loom textile industry for centuries. Indian cotton cloth was famous across the globe. Then:
- British machine-made textiles became vastly cheaper per yard
- India, as a British colony, could not impose protective tariffs on British imports
- When Indian factories in Bombay began competing effectively, Lancashire mill owners lobbied Parliament
- Parliament imposed a **5% equalizing duty** on Indian-manufactured cloth — making Indian products artificially more expensive and protecting British competitors
- Result: Indian textile competitiveness was undermined by colonial policy, not just market forces

**Iron Works:**
- Steep tariffs under East India Company rule made Indian iron uncompetitive
- After the Rebellion of 1857, British authorities closed mines (fearing lead production would enable future rebellions)
- The **Arms Act of 1878** restricted Indian access to metals
- Iron mining in Rajasthan abandoned; extinct by early 19th century

**Shipbuilding:**
- Indian shipbuilding had flourished through political alliances in the late 17th century
- British mismanagement steadily eroded the industry
- East India Company absorbed the Indian Navy (1830); Royal Navy took full control (1863)
- Effectively ended independent Indian shipbuilding

> [!warning] India is one of the AP's favorite examples of how [[colonialism]] *actively prevented* development in colonized regions. This is NOT simply a story of India "falling behind" — it's a story of deliberate suppression.

### Egypt — Lost Markets

Egypt had exported fine carpets and silks to Europe in the 18th century. By the mid-19th century:
- European machine-made goods flooded Egyptian markets
- Egypt lacked both the tariff authority and political independence to protect its producers
- Egyptian manufacturers lost both their **export markets** and their **domestic market** to European competition

Unlike India, Egypt's deindustrialization was more a product of price competition than overt colonial policy — though European political pressure limited Egypt's options.

## Synthesis: What Determined the Outcome?

| Factor | Led to Industrialization | Led to Deindustrialization |
|---|---|---|
| Political independence | Yes (could set tariffs, direct investment) | — |
| Colonial status | — | Yes (policies favored colonizer) |
| State capacity | Key in Russia, Japan | Lacking in colonized regions |
| Access to capital | Essential | Drained by colonialism |
| Market access | Domestic + export | Domestic undercut by cheap imports |
| Defensive modernization | Japan (successful) | Egypt, India (not attempted or blocked) |
