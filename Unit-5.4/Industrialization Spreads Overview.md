# Industrialization Spreads

**AP Themes**: ECN · TEC · GOV
**Period**: 3 (c. 1750–1900) | **Printed Pages**: 304–309

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam tests **two sides** of this topic: (1) *why* industrialization spread to some regions and (2) *why* manufacturing *declined* in colonized regions like India and Egypt. Both sides are equally exam-relevant.

## Why Industrialization Spread

After Britain industrialized, the pattern spread outward — but not uniformly. Countries that successfully industrialized shared several preconditions: access to coal and iron deposits, water transportation (rivers, canals, coastal access), capital to invest in machinery, an available labor force, and either natural advantages or a state willing to compensate through deliberate policy.

## France and Germany

France was **delayed** by two structural problems: sparsely populated urban centers (limiting factory labor) and the French Revolution (1789) plus the subsequent Napoleonic Wars (ending 1815), which drained the capital and attention of French elites.

Germany was **politically fragmented** into small states until unification in **1871** under Otto von Bismarck. Once unified, however, Germany industrialized rapidly through **state-directed** investment and quickly became a leading producer of **steel and coal**.

> [!tip]
> Germany is the clearest example of state-directed industrialization in Western Europe. Compare with Japan and Russia — all three used government policy to accelerate industrial growth, in contrast to Britain's more market-driven approach.

## The United States

The United States began its industrial revolution in the **19th century** and was a leading industrial force by **1900**. A key factor was **human capital**: waves of immigrants from Europe and East Asia, combined with domestic rural-to-urban migrants, provided the large labor force needed for American factories.

## Russia

Russia industrialized with a focus on **railroads and exports**. By 1900 Russia had more than 6,000 miles of railroad, including the **Trans-Siberian Railroad** — stretching from Moscow to the Pacific Ocean — which opened trade with China and Japan. Russia's **coal, iron, and steel** industries grew primarily in the **1880s**, and by 1900 Russia was the **world's fourth-largest steel producer**. Even so, the economy remained overwhelmingly agricultural until the Communist seizure of power in 1917.

**Sergei Witte**, Russia's finance minister in the 1890s, was the architect of this industrialization. His secret memorandum to Tsar Nicholas II (c. 1899) argued that Russia was effectively a "colony" of Western industry — exporting cheap raw materials and importing expensive finished goods — but that Russia's independence and strength meant it could and should industrialize to become a metropolis in its own right.

## Japan

Japan was the **first Asian country to industrialize**, despite having had the least contact with Europe since the **17th century** (Japan's *sakoku* isolation policy). In the **mid-19th century**, Japan pursued **defensive modernization** — consciously adapting Western technology and institutions to *protect* traditional culture while building military and economic strength sufficient to resist Western domination.

The trigger was **Commodore Matthew Perry**'s arrival in 1853–1854, which forced Japan to open to trade and demonstrated its military vulnerability. The result was the **[[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]]** (1868), which transformed Japan into a leading world power within four decades. For more detail on Japan's industrialization, see **Topic 5.6**.

## Decline of Manufacturing Outside Europe

While Europe's share of global manufacturing grew, Asia and the Middle East saw their share **decline** — not simply because they failed to industrialize, but because **colonial policies actively suppressed their industries**.

### India: Shipbuilding

Indian shipbuilding initially revived in the **late 17th century** through political alliances. But British colonial mismanagement in the 17th–18th centuries undermined it. Britain designated East India Company ships as the **Indian Navy** (c. 1801), which was then **disbanded in 1863** when the Royal Navy assumed complete control of the Indian Ocean.

### India: Iron and Metalworking

During **company rule** (EIC control of India, 1757–1858), steep British **tariffs** caused the decline of Indian metalworking. After the **Indian Rebellion of 1857**, Britain began closing mines, fearing they supplied lead for ammunition. The **Arms Act of 1878** further restricted access to minerals and firearms production. By the early 20th century, mines in the mineral-rich state of **Rajasthan** were abandoned and the mining industry was practically extinct. Even after company rule ended in 1858, these conditions persisted for decades — the apparent inaccessibility of India's minerals reflected decades of deliberately abandoned infrastructure, not natural scarcity.

### India: Textiles

As India's textile industry flourished, it undercut British mills in **Lancaster**. Mill owners pressured the colonial government to impose an "equalizing" **5% tax** on all textiles produced at the more than 80 mills in **Bombay**, undermining their profitability and confirming that colonial policy served metropolitan interests at the direct expense of colonial industry.

### Egypt: Textiles

In the 18th century, Egypt exported **carpets, silks, and other textiles** to Europe. By the mid-19th century, the surge in European (especially British) textile production had destroyed Egypt's export market and even much of its domestic market.

## Agricultural Products and Global Trade

A defining feature of 19th-century global economics was the division between **industrialized producers of finished goods** and **raw-material exporters**:

| Product | Producers | End Users |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Wheat | Russia, Britain | Britain (food) |
| Rubber | Brazilian Amazon | Britain (tires, footwear, fabrics) |
| Palm Oil | West Africa, Indonesia | Britain (cooking oil, soap) |
| Sugar | Caribbean Islands, Brazil | Britain (refined sugar) |
| Cattle & Hogs | United States, Ireland, Argentina | Britain, United States (meat) |
| Cotton | United States | Britain (textiles) |

*(AMSCO p. 305)*

This pattern — colonial and semi-colonial regions supplying raw materials while industrialized Britain processed and consumed them — is the economic structure of 19th-century imperialism.

> [!warning]
> Do not characterize the decline of Asian manufacturing as an inability to industrialize. AMSCO explicitly states these regions continued producing goods — their *share* declined because European output grew massively AND because colonial policies actively dismantled local industries.
