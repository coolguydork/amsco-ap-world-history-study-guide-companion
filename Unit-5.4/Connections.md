# Connections

## Builds On

**[[Unit-5.3/Industrial Revolution Begins Overview|Industrial Revolution Begins Overview]]** — The spread of industrialization presupposes Britain's prior industrialization. The preconditions (coal, capital, enclosures, cottage industry → factory), the key technologies (spinning jenny, water frame, steam engine), and Adam Smith's laissez-faire framework are all established in [[Unit-5.3/Industrial Revolution Begins Overview|Topic 5.3]]. Topic 5.4 asks the next question: given those conditions, why did industrialization spread to *some places* and not others? The answer reveals that capital, resources, and water transport were necessary but not sufficient — state willingness to *direct* the process proved equally decisive.

**[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|Mercantilism]]** — The colonial economic logic driving Britain to deindustrialize India and Egypt was mercantilism's 19th-century successor: the metropole extracts raw materials from colonies, processes them into finished goods, and sells them back. Even as Britain officially embraced free trade after the 1840s, colonial India was denied those same free-trade benefits in practice (as the 5% "equalizing" tax on Bombay textile mills makes clear).

**[[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|Joint-Stock Companies]]** — The British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] was the institutional vehicle for company rule in India (1757–1858). Its tariff policies, mine closures, and eventual replacement by Crown rule are central to the deindustrialization story in Topic 5.4.

**[[Unit-5.2/Nationalism & Revolutions Overview|Nationalism & Revolutions Overview]]** — German unification (1871, covered in [[Unit-5.2/Nationalism & Revolutions Overview|Topic 5.2]]) is the political precondition for German industrialization. Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] parallels the nationalist movements of [[Unit-5.2/Nationalism & Revolutions Overview|Topic 5.2]]: both involve elites using a political crisis to build a powerful modern state. The connection between nationalism and industrialization runs through both topics.

## AP Threads Running Through This Topic

**Technology transfer** — Japan and Germany both acquired technology from more advanced industrial societies and adapted it to their own contexts. Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] is the clearest example of deliberate, state-directed technology transfer as a national survival strategy — send delegations abroad, hire foreign experts, adapt selectively, then build domestic capacity.

**Nationalism and industrialization** — Both German unification (1871) and Japan's Meiji Restoration (1868) demonstrate that nationalist state-building and industrialization reinforced each other. A unified or modernizing state could direct investment, protect infant industries, and mobilize resources for development in ways that fragmented or colonized societies could not. This thread connects [[Unit-5.2/Nationalism & Revolutions Overview|Topic 5.2]] (nationalist revolutions) → Topic 5.4 (industrialization spreading via state direction) → Topics 6.1–6.2 (industrialized states pursuing empire).

**Colonialism and deindustrialization** — The decline of Indian and Egyptian manufacturing is not a separate story from European industrialization — it is the *other side of the same process*. British colonial policy deliberately created raw-material suppliers and destroyed industrial competitors. Naming this dynamic (active suppression rather than passive underdevelopment) is the key to earning AP complexity credit on LEQs and DBQs about this period. See [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism|Mercantilism]] for the economic logic behind metropolitan dominance of colonial markets.
