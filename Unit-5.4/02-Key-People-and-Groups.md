# Key People and Groups — Unit 5.4: Industrialization Spreads

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## The Meiji Oligarchs (Japan, 1868–1912)
**Region:** Japan | **Period:** 1868–1912

- A group of ambitious samurai reformers (including Ōkubo Toshimichi, Itō Hirobumi, and Yamagata Aritomo) who led the Meiji Restoration, abolished the feudal domains, and built a modern state.
- Sent delegations abroad (Iwakura Mission, 1871–1873) to study Western legal, educational, and industrial systems — then selectively adopted what was useful.
- Built state-owned factories, railroads, and banks, then *sold* them to private interests (zaibatsu — industrial conglomerates) at favorable prices — combining state initiation with private operation.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Meiji oligarchs represent the AP's most important case of state-directed modernization. Know their strategy: (1) abolish feudalism, (2) build state infrastructure, (3) send students/delegations abroad, (4) transfer to private ownership. Contrast with China's failure to do any of these.

## Otto von Bismarck (1815–1898)
**Region:** Prussia/Germany | **Period:** 1862–1890

- As Prussian Minister-President and then German Chancellor, directed both German political unification (1871) and the state-managed industrialization that followed.
- Used protective tariffs ("iron and rye" coalition — manufacturers + landowners) to shield German industry, and state investment in railroads and banking to build industrial infrastructure.
- Also introduced Europe's first **social welfare legislation** (health insurance 1883, accident insurance 1884, old-age pensions 1889) — not from idealism but to undercut socialist organizing by meeting workers' basic needs.

## Cornelius Vanderbilt (1794–1877) and Jay Gould (1836–1892)
**Region:** United States | **Period:** Mid-to-late 19th century

- American "robber barons" who built and consolidated railroad empires using aggressive financial tactics, political corruption, and monopoly power.
- Vanderbilt's New York Central and Gould's Union Pacific represented the pattern of American industrialization: private capital, minimal regulation, enormous fortunes, and working-class exploitation.
- Their careers illustrate the Social Darwinist ideology that justified extreme wealth concentration as the natural result of competition.

---

## Samuel Slater (1768–1835)
**Region:** United States | **Period:** Late 18th–early 19th century

- British textile worker who memorized the designs of Arkwright's machinery and emigrated to the United States (1789), where he built the first American textile mill (Pawtucket, Rhode Island, 1793).
- Known in Britain as "Slater the Traitor" for smuggling industrial knowledge — illustrating how technology diffusion worked despite British attempts to prevent it.
- His mills used child labor from local farm families — establishing early American factory culture.

---

## Sergei Witte (1849–1915)
**Region:** Russia | **Period:** 1890s–1900s

- Russian Finance Minister who directed rapid industrialization in the 1890s, attracting French investment, building the Trans-Siberian Railroad, and establishing state-owned heavy industry.
- His strategy produced rapid industrial growth but also inflamed social tensions: concentrated factory proletariat + repressive autocracy = revolutionary pressure.
- His policies set the stage for the Russian Revolution by creating an industrial working class in a political system that refused to reform.

---

## Chinese Reformers and the Self-Strengthening Movement (1861–1895)
**Region:** China | **Period:** 1860s–1890s

- Chinese officials (including Li Hongzhang and Zeng Guofan) who advocated adopting Western military technology while preserving Confucian social and political institutions.
- Built arsenals, shipyards, and some modern industries — but failed to reform the imperial examination system, land tenure, or bureaucratic structure.
- Defeated by Japan in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895) — humiliatingly demonstrating that military technology alone, without institutional reform, was insufficient.

---

## Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919)
**Region:** United States | **Period:** 1870s–1900s

- Scottish-born immigrant who built Carnegie Steel into the world's largest steel producer through vertical integration (controlling coal mines, iron ore, railroads, and mills).
- Embodied both the spectacular wealth generation and the brutal labor exploitation of American industrial capitalism (Homestead Strike, 1892).
- His later philanthropy (libraries, Carnegie Hall) established the "Gospel of Wealth" philosophy — that the wealthy have a duty to distribute their surplus for public benefit.
