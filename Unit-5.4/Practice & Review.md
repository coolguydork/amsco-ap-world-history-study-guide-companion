# Practice & Review

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 307–308 (PDF pp. 371–372)*

**Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage:**

> "The economic relations of Russia with western Europe are fully comparable to the relations of colonial countries with their metropolises. The latter consider their colonies as advantageous markets in which they can freely sell the products of their labor and of their industry and from which they can draw with a powerful hand the raw materials necessary for them. This is the basis of the economic power of the governments of western Europe…
>
> But there is a radical difference between Russia and a colony: Russia is an independent and strong power. She has the right and the strength not to want to be the eternal handmaiden of states which are more developed economically. She should know the price of her raw materials and of the natural riches hidden in the womb of her abundant territories… She wants to be a metropolis herself.
>
> On the basis of the people's labor, liberated from the bonds of serfdom, there began to grow our own national economy, which bids fair to become a reliable counterweight to the domination of foreign industry."
>
> *Sergei Witte, Russian finance minister, secret report to Tsar Nicholas II on industrialization, c. 1899*

---

**Question 1.** In the above passage, what policy is the Russian Minister of Finance urging?

A. Maintaining current political and commercial relationships
B. Supporting independent Russian industrial development
C. Relying on foreign industry for economic growth
D. Using military strength to prevent foreign industrial domination

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Witte explicitly argues that Russia should stop being the "handmaiden" of Western industry and develop its own national economy. Option A is the opposite of his argument. Option C describes what Russia currently does and what Witte wants to change. Option D misreads the passage — Witte emphasizes economic independence, not military confrontation.

---

**Question 2.** What shift in economic focus did the emancipation of the serfs foster?

A. It created a pool of laborers who could more easily move to cities and work in industry.
B. It increased the power of nobles who could then travel more widely and bring new ideas to Russia.
C. It encouraged population growth, which increased tax revenues.
D. It established a new consumer base that could afford to purchase imported manufactured goods.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Witte's passage explicitly cites "the people's labor, liberated from the bonds of serfdom" as the foundation of Russia's new national economy. Emancipated serfs became mobile wage workers who could relocate to industrial cities — the necessary precondition for factory labor. Options B, C, and D are not supported by the passage or by historical evidence on the effects of serf emancipation.

---

**Question 3.** What was a critical strategy in the industrialization of Russia?

A. Building an infrastructure of canals
B. Controlling the settlement of Siberia
C. Expanding the national railroad network
D. Developing a free trade policy

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The AMSCO narrative explicitly states that Russia industrialized by "focusing particularly on railroads and exports." The Trans-Siberian Railroad was the centerpiece of Witte's industrialization program. Option A (canals) better describes Britain and early American development. Option D is wrong — Russia used tariff protection, not free trade. Option B (Siberia settlement) was an effect of the Trans-Siberian Railroad, not the primary strategy.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 308–309 (PDF pp. 372–373)*

### SAQ 1 — Meiji Charter Oath (1868)

**Source:**
> "By this oath we set up as our aim the establishment of the national weal on a broad basis and framing of a constitution and laws.
> 1. Deliberative assemblies shall be widely established and all matters decided by public discussion.
> 2. All classes, high and low, shall unite in vigorously carrying out the administration of affairs of state.
> 3. The common people, no less than the civil and military officials, shall each be allowed to pursue his own calling so that there may be no discontent.
> 4. Evil customs of the past shall be broken off and everything based upon the just laws of Nature.
> 5. Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world so as to strengthen the foundations of imperial rule."
>
> *The Charter Oath of the Meiji Restoration, 1868*

**A.** Identify ONE way in which the Charter Oath affected the development of Japan's industrialization.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The Charter Oath's directive to "seek knowledge throughout the world" provided the official mandate and philosophical justification for Japan's deliberate technology transfer from Western nations. Under this principle, the Meiji government sent official delegations (including the Iwakura Mission, 1871–1873) to study Western factories, legal systems, railroads, and educational institutions, then systematically adapted what they found to Japanese conditions. This state-directed acquisition of foreign technology was the engine of Japan's rapid industrialization.

**B.** Explain ONE way in which the passage reflects change in Japanese political or social structures in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — CCOT
> The oath's provision that "all classes, high and low, shall unite in vigorously carrying out the administration of affairs of state" signals a break from the rigid Tokugawa feudal hierarchy, in which social classes (samurai, farmers, artisans, merchants) were strictly separated and political power was monopolized by the military (samurai) class. The Meiji Restoration's abolition of formal feudal class distinctions and opening of government service to broader participation represented a fundamental restructuring of Japan's social order — one necessary to mobilize the talent and labor needed for rapid industrial transformation.

**C.** Explain ONE historical situation in the period 1750–1900, other than the one illustrated in the passage, that led to changes in Japan.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization
> Commodore Matthew Perry's arrival in Japan with a U.S. naval squadron in 1853–1854, forcing Japan to sign trade agreements under threat of industrial-era military force, demonstrated Japan's vulnerability in a way that centuries of isolation had concealed. The inability to resist Perry's steam-powered warships convinced Japanese elites that the *sakoku* isolation policy was no longer viable — that Japan must either modernize rapidly or risk the colonial fate of India and China. This realization, catalyzed by Perry's visit, directly drove the political coalition that overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate and established the Meiji government in 1868.

### SAQ 2 — Comparative Industrialization

**A.** Identify ONE way in which revolution affected industrialization in France and the United States.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> In France, the French Revolution (1789) and the subsequent Napoleonic Wars consumed the capital and attention of French elites, delaying investment in industrial development and slowing France's industrialization relative to Britain and Germany. In the United States, the aftermath of the Civil War (1865) had the opposite effect: it consolidated a national market, drove massive railroad construction, and attracted waves of immigrant labor — making revolution an accelerant for U.S. industrialization rather than a delay.

**B.** Explain ONE way in which industrialization affected Japan and Britain differently in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> British industrialization emerged primarily from private capital and market forces — a bottom-up process driven by individual industrialists and merchants with relatively minimal government direction. Japanese industrialization under the Meiji state was deliberately top-down: the government imported foreign technology, built state-owned factories and railroads, and then transferred them to private interests. Britain industrialized first and generated the model; Japan industrialized last among the major powers in this period and consciously *adapted* rather than spontaneously developing its own path. Both produced leading industrial powers, but through structurally opposite processes.

**C.** Explain ONE way in which political policies and cultural traditions influenced the Asian or Middle Eastern share in global manufacturing.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> British colonial policy in India directly suppressed India's share of global manufacturing. The East India Company imposed steep tariffs on Indian metalworking during company rule (1757–1858), then closed mines after the 1857 Rebellion to deny insurgents access to lead. The Arms Act of 1878 further restricted mineral production. Simultaneously, the colonial government forced a 5% "equalizing" tax on Bombay textile mills to protect Lancashire competitors. These deliberate policy interventions — not Indian cultural traditions or technological incapacity — drove the decline of India's manufacturing share in the 19th century.

## Historical Reasoning Practice

### Connect Imperialism and Industrialization

*Think As a Historian — AMSCO p. 309*

> [!exam-tip]
> This TAH asks you to connect two major AP themes: **industrialization** (Units 5.3–5.5) and **imperialism** (Units 6.1–6.2). The evidence in Topic 5.4 points to a mutually reinforcing relationship — neither could have developed to the same scale without the other.

**Framework for your response:**

Industrialization created demand for raw materials that European domestic production could not meet, driving colonial expansion to secure overseas supply chains (rubber, cotton, palm oil, sugar). Colonial profits provided capital for further industrial investment. Industrialized military technology — steam warships, repeating rifles, railroads — made colonial conquest faster and cheaper to enforce. And colonial control suppressed non-European manufacturing, guaranteeing captive markets for European finished goods. Both processes fed each other: industrialization made imperialism feasible; imperialism made industrialization more profitable.

**Exam application:** This connection is frequently tested via DBQ documents pairing industrial statistics with colonial administrative documents, or MCQs asking about the economic rationale for New Imperialism. The answer is almost always: industrialized states needed raw materials for factories AND markets for finished goods — imperialism provided both.
