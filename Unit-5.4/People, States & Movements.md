# People, States & Movements

## Key Individuals

**Sergei Witte (1849–1915)**
Russian finance minister who directed Russia's industrialization in the 1890s. His secret memorandum to Tsar Nicholas II (c. 1899) described Russia as economically colonized by the West and argued for aggressive state-led industrialization using the labor freed by Alexander II's serf emancipation. Witte's primary tool was **railroad construction** — Russia built over 6,000 miles of track, including the Trans-Siberian Railroad, under his tenure. By 1900 Russia was the world's fourth-largest steel producer. Witte is a model exam figure for state-directed industrialization. *(AMSCO pp. 305, 307)*

**Tsar Alexander II (r. 1855–1881)**
Russian tsar who **emancipated the serfs in 1861**, freeing bonded laborers and creating a mobile working class that could migrate to industrial centers. Witte later identified this as the precondition for Russian industrialization — without free laborers, factory labor was impossible. Alexander's reforms partially modernized Russia but did not transform it; the economy remained overwhelmingly agricultural into the 20th century. His emancipation edict is a key example of a political decision enabling economic change. *(AMSCO p. 307)*

**Commodore Matthew Perry (1794–1858)**
American naval commander who arrived in Japan in **1853–1854** with a fleet of steam-powered warships, forcing Japan to sign trade agreements and end its *sakoku* isolation. Perry's demonstration of industrial-era military technology made Japan's vulnerability unmistakable and directly triggered the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] — Japanese elites concluded that modernization was the only alternative to colonization. *(AMSCO p. 305)*

**Meiji Emperor (Mutsuhito, r. 1868–1912)**
The figurehead of the **Meiji Restoration**, under whose reign Japan transformed from a feudal society to an industrialized world power within four decades. The **Charter Oath of 1868** (the SAQ source for this topic) set out five principles: deliberative assemblies, broad class participation in governance, freedom of occupation, rejection of feudal customs, and worldwide pursuit of knowledge. In practice, the Meiji government sent delegations abroad (notably the Iwakura Mission, 1871–1873) to study Western industry, law, and military organization, then selectively adapted them — the essence of **defensive modernization**. *(AMSCO p. 305)*

**Otto von Bismarck (1815–1898)**
Chancellor who unified Germany through three wars (against Denmark 1864, Austria 1866, France 1870–71), creating the German Empire in **1871**. German unification ended the political fragmentation that had delayed industrialization. Under Bismarck, state-directed investment rapidly made Germany a leading steel and coal producer. See also [[Unit-5.2/[[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] & Revolutions Overview|Nationalism & Revolutions Overview]] for the political history of German unification.

## Movements and Institutions

**British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC)**
The [[Concept Notes/Joint-Stock Companies|joint-stock company]] that ruled India from 1757 to 1858 (**company rule**). The EIC systematically deindustrialized India through tariff policy, mine closures, and legislative restrictions, transforming India from a manufacturing economy into a supplier of raw materials. After the **Indian Rebellion of 1857**, the British Crown replaced the EIC with direct colonial rule — but the suppression of Indian industry continued.

**Indian Rebellion of 1857 (Sepoy Mutiny)**
A major uprising against EIC rule by Indian soldiers (sepoys), rooted in resentment of colonial exploitation and triggered by specific military grievances. The aftermath accelerated India's deindustrialization: the Crown closed mines (fearing lead supplies for insurgents), imposed the Arms Act (1878), and tightened control over mineral resources. The Rebellion is thus a turning point in both Indian political and industrial history. *(AMSCO p. 306)*

**[[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] (1868)**
Japan's state-directed transformation following the forced opening by Commodore Perry. The Meiji government abolished feudal class structures, built state-owned railroads and arsenals, imported foreign experts, and sent Japanese delegations abroad to acquire industrial and military knowledge. This "revolution from above" made Japan the only Asian country to industrialize in the 19th century while retaining national sovereignty — a sharp contrast with India and Egypt, which lost sovereignty precisely when it mattered most for industrial development.
