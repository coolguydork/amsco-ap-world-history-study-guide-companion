# Key Terms & Concepts

## Economic & Industrial Terms

**Company rule**
The period of British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC) control over parts of the Indian subcontinent, from **1757 to 1858**. During company rule, the EIC imposed steep tariffs that deindustrialized India's metalworking, shipbuilding, and textile industries. Company rule ended when the British Crown assumed direct control after the 1857 Indian Rebellion. The period is a prime exam example of how colonial governance actively suppressed economic development in colonized regions. *(AMSCO p. 306)*

**Human capital**
The workforce — the collective skills, labor, and productive capacity of a population. The United States drew on waves of European and East Asian immigrants plus rural-to-urban migrants as its primary source of human capital for industrial growth in the 19th century. *(AMSCO p. 305)*

**State-directed industrialization**
A model in which the government (rather than private markets) leads industrial development through railroad construction, tariff protection, and direct investment. Germany, Russia, and Japan all used state-directed industrialization to catch up with Britain. This contrasts with Britain's more laissez-faire, market-driven approach — see [[Unit-5.3/Key Terms & Concepts|Key Terms & Concepts]] (Adam Smith, laissez-faire) for that model. *(AMSCO pp. 304–306)*

**Defensive modernization**
The strategy of adopting Western technology and institutions in order to *protect* traditional culture and political independence — not to become Western, but to build enough military and economic strength to resist Western domination. Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] is the clearest example; the Ottoman Tanzimat reforms are a parallel case. *(AMSCO p. 305)*

**Deindustrialization**
The process by which a previously manufacturing-capable region loses that capacity. In 19th-century India and Egypt, deindustrialization resulted not from natural backwardness but from deliberate colonial policies (tariffs, mine closures, legal restrictions) that destroyed competitive industries to benefit the colonial metropole. A key concept for understanding why industrialization was *uneven* globally.

**Corvée labor**
Unpaid or forced labor owed to a state or landlord — distinct from chattel slavery. Appears as a labor system in colonial contexts; becomes more central in **Topic 6.2** (colonialism and labor systems). Connected to the broader theme of how industrial capitalism coexisted with or transformed pre-existing coerced labor systems.

## Transportation & Infrastructure

**Trans-Siberian Railroad**
Russia's massive railroad stretching from **Moscow to the Pacific Ocean**, built primarily in the **1880s–1900s**. It connected Russia's commercial and industrial areas, drove demand for coal, iron, and steel, and opened trade with China and Japan. By 1900 Russia had over 6,000 miles of railroad. The Trans-Siberian was both the instrument and the symbol of Russian industrial development under **Sergei Witte**. *(AMSCO p. 305)*

## Political & Legal Terms

**Arms Act (1878)**
A British colonial law in India that restricted access to minerals and firearms production. Passed in the aftermath of the 1857 Rebellion, it was one of several policies that systematically dismantled India's mining and metalworking industries. By the early 20th century, mines in mineral-rich **Rajasthan** were abandoned. The Arms Act exemplifies how colonial governance used law — not just tariffs — to prevent colonized populations from developing military or industrial capacity. *(AMSCO p. 306)*

## Key Figures

**Sergei Witte (1849–1915)**
Russia's finance minister in the 1890s and the primary architect of Russian industrialization. In a secret memorandum to Tsar Nicholas II (c. 1899), Witte argued that Russia was economically subordinate to Western Europe — a "colony" supplying raw materials and buying manufactured goods. He urged Russia to use the labor freed by serf emancipation (1861) to build an independent national economy. Witte's key instrument was **railroad construction**, especially the Trans-Siberian Railroad. *(AMSCO pp. 305, 307)*

> [!exam-tip]
> The Witte passage appears as an MCQ source. Key arguments: (1) Russia currently resembles a colony economically; (2) Russia has the right and strength not to remain that way; (3) serf emancipation created the labor base for industry. The correct answer to "what policy is Witte urging?" is **independent Russian industrial development** — not military confrontation, not free trade, not maintaining the status quo.
