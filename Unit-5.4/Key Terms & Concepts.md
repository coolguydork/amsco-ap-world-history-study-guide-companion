# Topic 5.4 — Key Terms & Concepts

> [!exam-tip] Topic 5.4 vocabulary is all about *context*: knowing a term isn't enough — you need to know which country it applies to and why it mattered there.

---

## Core Concepts

### Defensive Modernization
A strategy in which a non-Western country deliberately adopts Western technology, institutions, and methods in order to *protect* its own sovereignty and culture from Western domination. Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] is the clearest example. The goal is selective adoption — take the technology, reject the subjugation.

> [!tip] Contrast defensive modernization with countries that *did not* modernize (Ottoman Empire's slower adaptation, Qing China's resistance) and were colonized or forced into unequal treaties.

### Human Capital
The skills, knowledge, and labor capacity of a workforce. In the United States, massive **immigration** from Europe and East Asia (plus internal migration from rural areas) supplied the human capital that powered its industrial rise.

### Trans-Siberian Railroad
A massive railroad project connecting **Moscow to the Pacific Ocean** across Siberia. Completed by Russia around 1900. Key purposes:
- Facilitate trade with China and Japan
- Connect Russia's vast interior to export markets
- Project Russian military and economic power eastward

### Defensive Industrialization (top-down vs. bottom-up)
**Bottom-up industrialization** (Britain, U.S.): driven by private entrepreneurs, merchants, and investors responding to market incentives.
**Top-down industrialization** (Russia, Japan): driven by state direction, government investment, and national security concerns. The state built railroads, created banks, and funded key industries.

### Deindustrialization
The *decline* of existing manufacturing sectors — often caused by foreign competition or deliberate colonial policy. Happened in India and Egypt as a result of British industrial dominance and colonial governance.

### Meiji Restoration
The 1868 political transformation in Japan in which Emperor Meiji reclaimed power from the Tokugawa shogunate. The Meiji government launched rapid modernization — building railroads, factories, a modern army and navy — making Japan the first Asian industrial power. See [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]].

## Country-Specific Terms

### Ruhr Valley
Germany's primary coal and steel region. After German unification (1871), the Ruhr became the engine of Germany's industrial boom — comparable to Britain's Lancashire and Yorkshire.

### Tokugawa Shogunate
The Japanese military government that ruled from 1603 to 1868 and enforced a policy of isolation from Europe. Its overthrow in the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] opened Japan to industrialization.

### East India Company (British)
A royally chartered trading company that came to govern much of India (1757–1858). Under Company rule, Indian industries — shipbuilding, iron, textiles — were undercut through tariffs, regulations, and direct takeover. After the Rebellion of 1857, the British Crown took direct control.

### Rebellion of 1857 (Indian Mutiny / Sepoy Mutiny)
An uprising against British East India Company rule in India. After it was suppressed, the British Crown assumed direct governance of India (the British Raj). The rebellion led to even harsher restrictions on Indian industry, including the closure of iron mines.

### Arms Act of 1878
A British colonial law in India restricting Indian ownership of weapons. In the context of deindustrialization, it further curtailed Indian iron production by limiting access to metals.

### Lancashire
The British county at the heart of the textile industry. Lancashire mill owners lobbied the British government to impose the 5% equalizing tax on Indian textiles, protecting their competitive advantage.

## Comparative Framework

Use this table to organize your comparisons for essays:

| Country | Start of Industrialization | Key Driver | Key Obstacle | Outcome by 1900 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Britain | 1760s | Geography, coal, colonial capital | None — it was first | Global industrial leader |
| France | Late 18th–early 19th c. | Some coal, merchant capital | Revolution, sparse urban labor | Significant but behind Britain/Germany |
| Germany | After 1871 | Ruhr coal/iron, state support | Pre-1871 fragmentation | Major industrial power, leader in steel |
| USA | 19th century | Immigration, vast resources, railroads | Internal divisions (Civil War) | World's leading industrial nation by 1900 |
| Russia | 1890s primarily | State-driven railroads, steel | Agricultural economy, autocratic government | 4th largest steel producer; still mostly agrarian |
| Japan | 1870s (after Meiji) | State direction, defensive modernization | Isolation, lack of resources | First Asian industrial power; military victories vs. China, Russia |
| India | Declined | — | British colonial policy, tariffs, company rule | Deindustrialized in textiles, iron, shipbuilding |
| Egypt | Declined | — | European competition, no tariff protection | Lost textile export and domestic markets |
