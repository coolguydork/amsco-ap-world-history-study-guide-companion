## Administrative Systems

**Timar System** — The Ottoman practice of granting cavalry soldiers (sipahis) the right to collect taxes from a defined territory (timar) in exchange for military service; similar to European feudalism. (Significance: created a loyal military class tied to the empire's tax structure; over time, as Janissaries became more important, the timar system declined — a key internal change within the Ottoman Empire.)

**Tax Farming** — A system in which the government sells the right to collect taxes in a given region to a private individual (tax farmer), who keeps a profit. (Significance: used widely by the Ottomans; it produced immediate revenue for the state but often led to over-taxation of peasants and regional corruption — an internal weakness of many empires.)

**Grand Vizier** — The chief minister and second-most powerful official in the Ottoman Empire, appointed by the sultan to run day-to-day administration. (Significance: under weak sultans, grand viziers could become the de facto rulers of the empire; the Köprülü Grand Viziers (1656–1703) essentially saved the empire during a period of weak sultans — an example of institutional resilience.)

**Ulama** — Islamic religious scholars who interpreted and administered Islamic law (sharia) in the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires. (Significance: the ulama served as both religious authorities and judicial/administrative officials; they legitimized rulers' authority but could also challenge it — a constant tension in Islamic empires.)

**Ghulam** — Slave soldiers in the Safavid military system, recruited and trained similarly to Ottoman Janissaries; Shah Abbas I expanded the ghulam corps to reduce dependence on Turkic tribal leaders. (Significance: the ghulam system is the Safavid equivalent of the devshirme — a key example to compare across empires on the AP exam.)

**Eight Banners System** — The Qing military organization that divided Manchu, Mongol, and Han Chinese soldiers into eight color-coded units (banners), each serving as a standing army and a social unit for the Manchu community. (Significance: the banners were the military foundation of Qing power; as they became increasingly ceremonial over time, the Qing had to rely more on Han Chinese forces — an important internal change.)

**Green Standard Army** — The Qing military force composed of Han Chinese soldiers, separate from the Eight Banners; used primarily for policing and internal security. (Significance: reflects the Qing strategy of incorporating Han military capacity while keeping it separate from Manchu military institutions — a careful balance of integration and separation.)

**Haremlik/Selamlik** — The Ottoman division of imperial and elite households into the women's quarters (haremlik) and men's public spaces (selamlik). (Significance: reflects the gendered spatial organization of Ottoman elite society; the imperial harem was not merely a sexual space but a politically important one — mothers of princes could wield enormous influence through the "sultanate of women.")

**Sultanate of Women** — A period in Ottoman history (roughly 1520–1656) during which the mothers and wives of sultans wielded significant political influence, often acting as regents or power brokers. (Significance: challenges simplistic views of women as purely subordinate in Islamic empires; demonstrates that political power operated through family and household structures, not just formal offices.)

## Cultural Legitimation & Architecture

**Taj Mahal** — The mausoleum built by Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658) for his wife Mumtaz Mahal in Agra, India; completed c. 1648. (Significance: the most iconic monument of Mughal imperial culture; its Persian-influenced design blending Islamic and Indian elements symbolizes Mughal syncretism and imperial grandeur; built by tens of thousands of workers — an expression of state power as well as devotion.)

**Süleymaniye Mosque** — The imperial mosque complex built in Istanbul by Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent (completed 1558), designed by chief architect Sinan. (Significance: a statement of Ottoman grandeur and Islamic piety; rivals the Hagia Sophia in scale; Sinan's genius combined Byzantine dome architecture with Ottoman minarets — visual evidence of how empires blend conquered cultures with their own.)

**Isfahan** — The Safavid capital developed by Shah Abbas I; its central Naqsh-e Jahan Square (World-Adorning Square) contained a royal mosque, a great bazaar, and a palace — the empire expressed in stone. (Significance: the design of Isfahan communicated Safavid power, Shia Islamic identity, and Persian cultural greatness simultaneously; "Isfahan is half the world" was a Persian saying about its magnificence.)

**Forbidden City** — The imperial palace complex in Beijing, originally built by the Ming Dynasty and maintained by the Qing; access was restricted to the imperial family and high officials. (Significance: the spatial organization of the Forbidden City expressed the Confucian hierarchy — the emperor at the center, everything radiating outward in ranks; the Qing maintained it unchanged to demonstrate continuity with Chinese imperial tradition.)

**Sinan** — The chief Ottoman architect under Suleiman and Selim II; designed over 300 buildings including the Süleymaniye Mosque and the later Selimiye Mosque. (Significance: Sinan's work represents the synthesis of Byzantine and Islamic architectural traditions; his buildings are the visual embodiment of Ottoman imperial culture.)

**Din-i-Ilahi ("Divine Faith")** — A syncretic spiritual movement promoted by Mughal Emperor Akbar that drew elements from Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, and Christianity; never spread beyond the court. (Significance: reflects Akbar's unusually eclectic approach to religion; illustrates that imperial power could be legitimized through *creating* new spiritual frameworks; the Din-i-Ilahi faded after Akbar's death — showing the limits of top-down religious innovation.)

**Ibadat Khana ("House of Worship")** — The building in Fatehpur Sikri where Akbar convened interfaith religious debates between Muslim, Hindu, Jain, Zoroastrian, and later Christian scholars. (Significance: a physical symbol of Akbar's religious curiosity and political strategy; unique in the world at its time — no other emperor of this era hosted formal interfaith dialogue.)

## Economic Administration

**Silk Trade (Safavid)** — The Safavid state established a royal monopoly on the production and export of Persian silk, one of the most valuable commodities in the global luxury market. (Significance: silk revenue funded Shah Abbas I's military reforms and cultural projects; the Safavids cultivated European (especially English and Dutch) trading partners to sell silk and bypass Ottoman middlemen.)

**Canton System** — The Chinese (Qing) system of restricting European trade to the single port of Canton (Guangzhou), where licensed merchants (the cohong) controlled all commerce with foreigners. (Significance: the formal Canton System was established in 1757, just after the Unit 3 period — use it as a bridge concept rather than a central 1450–1750 example. It reflects China's longstanding desire to control contact with Europeans on its own terms, and it eventually became a source of conflict with Britain in the 19th century — a direct setup for Unit 6 content.)
