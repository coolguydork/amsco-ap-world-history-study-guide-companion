# Economy in the Interwar Period

**AMSCO pp. 480–492 | AP Period 4 | Themes: ECN, GOV, CDI**

> [!exam-tip]
> This topic is the engine that drives everything in Unit 7. The Great Depression → political radicalism → WWII chain is one of the AP exam's favorite causation questions. Know at least three different governmental responses (U.S. New Deal, Soviet Five-Year Plans, fascist corporatism/autarky) and be able to compare them.

## Essential Question

How did economic instability after World War I contribute to political extremism and the rise of fascism?

## Big Picture

Not long after the trauma of WWI, the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression|Great Depression]] (1929–1939) struck the global economy with catastrophic force. Mass unemployment and collapsing prices destroyed faith in liberal capitalism and empowered authoritarian alternatives on both the left (Soviet communism) and the right (fascism). The key story of Topic 7.4 is how governments responded — and how those responses shaped the political world that produced WWII.

The interwar period produced three broad response models:

**1. Liberal Democracy + Keynesian Stimulus (U.S., France)**
British economist [[John Maynard Keynes]] rejected laissez-faire orthodoxy. He argued that during downturns, governments must inject demand into the economy through public spending, even if that means [[deficit spending]]. FDR's [[New Deal]] (1933–1939) put this into practice with relief programs, public works, and financial reform. It stabilized American democracy even if it did not fully end the Depression.

**2. Fascist Corporatism and Autarky (Italy, Germany, Spain, Portugal)**
Fascists rejected both liberal capitalism and communism. In Italy, Mussolini's [[corporatism]] organized the economy into state-supervised industry-labor-government blocs. Germany and Italy pursued economic autarky (self-sufficiency) and military rearmament as the path out of depression. The [[Spanish Civil War]] (1936–1939) became a proxy battleground for fascism vs. democracy, ending in Franco's victory. The bombing of [[Guernica]] by the Nazi Luftwaffe shocked the world.

**3. Communist Command Economy (Soviet Union, Mexico's hybrid)**
Stalin abandoned Lenin's [[New Economic Plan (NEP)]] in 1928–29 and launched the first [[Five-Year Plan]], forcing rapid industrialization through state command. [[collectivize|Collectivization]] herded peasants into [[kolkhoz|kolkhozy]], causing mass famine. [[gulag|Gulags]] imprisoned opponents. The [[Politburo]] became Stalin's instrument of terror. Meanwhile, Mexico's [[Lázaro Cárdenas]] pursued a hybrid model — nationalizing the oil industry via [[PEMEX]] (1938) to break foreign economic control, an example of economic nationalism in the colonial world.

## Weimar Germany and Hyperinflation

Germany's path to fascism began with economic humiliation. WWI reparations forced the [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic\|Weimar Republic]] to print money, producing catastrophic [[inflation]] in the early 1920s — savings wiped out overnight. The Great Depression (1929) then drove mass unemployment. The combination of economic desperation, national resentment over Versailles, and a weak democratic system enabled Hitler's rise to power by 1933.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse hyperinflation (early 1920s Germany) with the Great Depression (1929–1930s). They are two separate economic crises — both contributed to Nazi rise, but at different times.

## The Soviet Alternative

The Soviet Union presented itself as proof that a planned economy could avoid capitalist crises. While the West suffered through the Depression, Soviet industrial output grew dramatically under the Five-Year Plans — at staggering human cost. Stalin purged political rivals, collectivized agriculture by force (causing the [[Concept Notes/Holodomor\|Holodomor]] famine in Ukraine, ~3.5 million deaths), and sent millions to gulags. His [[totalitarian state]] left no sphere of life outside government control.

> [!tip]
> The AP exam often asks you to compare responses: U.S. New Deal vs. Soviet Five-Year Plans vs. Italian corporatism. All three expanded government economic control — but with radically different political systems underneath.

## Latin America: Economic Nationalism

The Depression devastated colonial and semi-colonial economies dependent on commodity exports. In Mexico, [[Lázaro Cárdenas]] responded with sweeping land reform and oil nationalization. His 1938 speech cited foreign companies' "tax exemptions, economic privileges, and governmental tolerance" as exploitation — framing nationalization as liberation. [[PEMEX]] became a symbol of economic sovereignty. This pattern — state seizure of foreign-owned resources — would become common across the decolonizing world in the mid-20th century.
