# Practice & Review

**AMSCO pp. 480–492**

## Multiple-Choice Questions

**Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage:**

> "It has been repeated *ad nauseam* [to a sickening degree] that the oil industry has brought additional capital for the development and progress of the country. This assertion is an exaggeration. For many years throughout the major period of their existence, oil companies have enjoyed great privileges for development and expansion, including customs and tax exemptions and innumerable prerogatives. . . . Potential wealth of the Nation, miserably underpaid native labor, tax exemptions, economic privileges, governmental tolerance — these are the factors of the boom of the Mexican oil industry. . . . It was therefore necessary to adopt a definite and legal measure to end this permanent state of affairs. . . . It is necessary that all groups of the population be imbued with a full optimism and that each citizen . . . develop a greater activity from this moment on, in order to create new resources which will reveal that the spirit of our people is capable of saving the nation's economy by the efforts of its own citizens."
>
> — President Lázaro Cárdenas of Mexico, speech announcing state control of the Mexican oil industry, 1938

---

**1.** The speech most directly reflects which of the following developments in the early twentieth century?

A) The spread of communist ideology from the Soviet Union to Latin America  
B) Economic nationalism as a response to foreign economic imperialism  
C) The decline of US influence in Latin America following WWI  
D) Mexico's attempt to join the Allied powers during WWII

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Cárdenas explicitly criticizes foreign oil companies' "tax exemptions, economic privileges, and governmental tolerance" — classic language of economic nationalism: a state asserting control over its own resources against foreign corporate exploitation. (A) is wrong — Cárdenas was not communist; the PRI was a post-revolutionary nationalist party. (C) is wrong — U.S. influence actually extended in this period through investment. (D) is wrong — this has nothing to do with WWII alliance politics.

---

**2.** According to Cárdenas's description in the speech, which of the following MOST directly contributed to the activities of foreign oil companies in Mexico?

A) Foreign economic imperialism supported by favorable government policies  
B) Mexico's lack of technological capacity to extract oil domestically  
C) The communist influence of the Soviet Union on Mexican workers  
D) The military power of the United States compelling Mexico to allow foreign companies

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Cárdenas specifically cites "customs and tax exemptions, innumerable prerogatives, economic privileges, governmental tolerance" as the mechanisms enabling foreign oil companies — all examples of state policies favoring foreign business. The speech does not mention technology gaps (B), communist influence (C), or U.S. military compulsion (D).

---

**3.** Which of the following best describes the broader historical context of the speech?

A) The Great Depression had made Latin American states more dependent on foreign investment  
B) The Mexican Revolution had already established a precedent for state control of strategic industries  
C) The League of Nations had mandated that member states nationalize key industries  
D) Fascist Italy had pressured Mexico to nationalize its oil to deny resources to Britain

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The 1917 Mexican Constitution (product of the Mexican Revolution) had established that subsoil resources belonged to the Mexican nation — Cárdenas was acting on this constitutional framework. (A) is backwards — the Depression actually accelerated nationalism *against* foreign dependence. (C) is invented. (D) is invented.

## Short-Answer Questions

**SAQ 1** — Use the passage below to answer all parts.

> "To Comrades Kuraev, Bosh, Minkin and other Penza communists:
> The revolt by the five kulak [derogatory term referring to the class of prosperous peasants] volosts [a territorial-administrative unit] must be suppressed without mercy. The interest of the entire revolution demands this. . . .
> 1. You need to hang — hang without fail, so that the public sees at least 100 notorious kulaks, the rich, and the bloodsuckers.
> 2. Publish their names.
> 3. Take away all of their grain.
> 4. Execute the hostages. . . .
> This needs to be accomplished in such a way that people for hundreds of miles around will see, tremble, know and scream out: let's choke and strangle those blood-sucking kulaks."
>
> — Telegram, Vladimir Lenin, August 11, 1918

**(A)** Identify ONE historical development after 1900 that reflects the Soviet hanging order from Vladimir Lenin.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A
> Stalin's forced collectivization of agriculture (1929–1933) directly reflects Lenin's violent approach toward the kulaks. Stalin ordered the "liquidation of the kulaks as a class," seizing their land and grain, executing or deporting hundreds of thousands to gulags, and deliberately starving millions in Ukraine (the Holodomor). This continued Lenin's policy of terrorizing peasants who resisted state control of food production.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which government economic intervention in Russia is similar to government economic intervention in the United States after 1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B
> Both Soviet Russia and the United States (under FDR's New Deal) dramatically expanded government control over the economy in response to crises. Both governments created large-scale public works programs to employ people — the Soviet Union used forced labor on massive infrastructure projects under the Five-Year Plans, while the U.S. used voluntary programs like the CCC and WPA to employ millions during the Depression. In both cases, the state took a directive role in economic planning that would have been unthinkable before WWI.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which government economic intervention in Russia differs from that in Italy after 1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C
> The Soviet system abolished private property and replaced market mechanisms entirely with state ownership and central planning, while Italian Fascism (under Mussolini's corporatism) preserved private ownership of businesses and farms but subjected them to state direction through government-supervised industrial corporations. Soviet intervention was ideologically driven by communist goals of eliminating capitalist classes; Italian intervention was driven by nationalist goals of maximizing state power while maintaining a modified capitalist structure. In the Soviet system, independent trade unions were replaced by party-controlled organs; in Italy, unions were dissolved but replaced by corporatist bodies nominally including both employers and workers.

**SAQ 2** — No stimulus

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which colonized states were affected by the Great Depression.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A
> Colonial economies — structured to export raw materials to industrialized powers — were devastated when global commodity prices collapsed during the Great Depression. Countries like India, Malaya, and various African colonies saw export revenues plummet when demand for rubber, cotton, tin, and agricultural products dried up, leading to mass unemployment and economic hardship. This sharpened resentment of colonial economic structures and accelerated anticolonial nationalist movements in the 1930s and 1940s.

**(B)** Explain ONE way that the ideas of John Maynard Keynes differ from the ideas of Adam Smith.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B
> Adam Smith argued that market economies are self-regulating — that supply and demand naturally reach equilibrium without government intervention (laissez-faire). Keynes rejected this view, arguing that during depressions, markets can become stuck in low-output equilibria with high unemployment because people save rather than spend. Keynes believed governments must step in to stimulate "aggregate demand" through deficit spending and public works, even if this means running budget deficits — a direct challenge to Smith's principle of government non-interference.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which Japan responded to the economic crisis after 1900 differently from the United States.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C
> Japan responded to the economic crises of the early 20th century (including the Great Depression) through military expansion and imperial conquest rather than domestic economic reform. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and launched a full-scale war in China in 1937, seeking to control resource-rich territories and new markets for Japanese industry. The United States, by contrast, responded to the Depression with the inward-focused New Deal — domestic economic programs, banking regulation, and social welfare — without pursuing territorial expansion. Japan's militarist response intensified international tensions and was a direct path toward WWII; the U.S. response sought stability through domestic restructuring.

## Think As a Historian: Significance of Historical Situation

*(AMSCO pp. 491–492)* — U.S. Secretary of State Cordell Hull's letter protested Mexico's oil nationalization, arguing it violated international law requiring "prompt, adequate, and effective" compensation for expropriated property.

**Key questions to practice:**

1. In Hull's view, what would allow for expropriation of private property? *(Prompt, adequate, and effective compensation — and use of "recognized and orderly methods" within existing law, not extralegal seizure)*

2. What is Hull asking the Mexican government to do? *(Stop the nationalization or compensate the oil companies fully; conform to international legal norms rather than creating a precedent of confiscation)*

3. Why would the United States be interested in preserving good relations with Mexico at this historical moment? *(WWII is approaching; the U.S. needed Latin American allies; FDR's Good Neighbor Policy sought influence through diplomacy rather than military intervention; Nazi Germany was buying Mexican oil — keeping Mexico friendly was strategically vital)*

> [!exam-tip]
> When the AP gives you a government document protesting another nation's economic policy, ask: what economic interest is at stake, and what is the speaker's historical context? Hull speaks for U.S. corporations with investments in Mexico. Cárdenas speaks for Mexican national sovereignty against foreign exploitation. Both positions reflect rational interests — the AP rewards students who can articulate both.
