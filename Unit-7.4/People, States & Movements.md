# People, States & Movements

**AMSCO pp. 480–492**

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves to pair these leaders as comparison prompts: FDR vs. Stalin (both expanded state economic power, but very different systems), or Mussolini vs. Hitler (both fascist, but different racial ideologies). Know what makes each response similar AND different.

## Leaders of the Democratic Response

**Franklin Delano Roosevelt (FDR)**
U.S. president (1933–1945). Faced the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression|Great Depression]]'s mass unemployment and banking collapse upon entering office. Created the [[New Deal]], a sweeping Keynesian program of relief, recovery, and reform. Key programs: CCC (conservation jobs for young men), WPA (arts and infrastructure), TVA (regional electricity), Social Security Act, FDIC, SEC. The only U.S. president elected four times; permanently enlarged the federal government's economic role. Also led the U.S. through most of WWII after Pearl Harbor. Key quote: condemned "business and financial monopoly, speculation, reckless banking, class antagonism, sectionalism, war profiteering."

**John Maynard Keynes**
British economist (1883–1946). His 1936 *General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* argued that free markets do not automatically self-correct during depressions — they can get stuck in low-output equilibria. Governments should inject demand through [[deficit spending]] and public works. His ideas underpinned the New Deal, post-WWII welfare states, and remain foundational to macroeconomic policy. Represents the intellectual alternative to both laissez-faire capitalism and Soviet communism.

> [!tip]
> On the AP exam: when asked about the New Deal's ideological basis, cite Keynes. When asked what made FDR's response different from fascism or communism, emphasize that the New Deal preserved democracy and private property while expanding government regulation.

## Leaders of the Fascist Response

**Benito Mussolini**
Italian dictator (in power 1922–1943). Created the world's first fascist state. Built a [[corporatism|corporatist]] economy where the state directed both employers and workers through government-supervised industrial blocs. Suppressed labor unions and political opposition. Allied with Hitler; supported Franco in the Spanish Civil War, sending troops and aircraft (including the bombing of Guernica). His aggressive imperialism (invasion of Ethiopia, 1935) exposed the League of Nations' weakness.

**Adolf Hitler**
Nazi dictator of Germany (in power 1933–1945). Rose to power exploiting Great Depression mass unemployment and Weimar Germany's fragility. Rearmament and government spending ended German unemployment (but at the cost of democracy, Jewish rights, and eventually world war). Intervened in the Spanish Civil War via the Luftwaffe, gaining valuable combat experience. Represents the most extreme endpoint of the interwar political crisis.

**Francisco Franco**
Spanish general who launched the Nationalist rebellion against the elected Spanish Republic in July 1936, triggering the [[Spanish Civil War]]. Received decisive support from Nazi Germany (Luftwaffe, including Guernica) and Fascist Italy. Won the war by April 1939 and ruled Spain as an authoritarian dictator until 1975. His regime was Catholic-conservative rather than racially fascist — he stayed neutral in WWII. A key example of how fascism spread beyond Germany and Italy.

**António de Oliveira Salazar**
Portuguese dictator (in power 1932–1968). Established the *Estado Novo* ("New State") — a fascist-adjacent authoritarian regime featuring government censorship, abolition of political parties, imprisonment of opponents, and [[hypernationalism]]. Supported Franco in the Spanish Civil War. Portugal's Catholic conservative authoritarianism mirrored Spain's and illustrates the broader interwar trend toward authoritarian alternatives to liberal democracy.

## Leaders of the Communist Response

**Joseph Stalin**
Soviet leader (in power 1924–1953). After Lenin's death, Stalin outmaneuvered rivals to seize control of the [[Politburo]] and the Soviet state. Abandoned Lenin's pragmatic NEP and launched the first [[Five-Year Plan]] (1928), rapidly industrializing the USSR through command economy directives. Forced [[collectivize|collectivization]] of agriculture caused the Holodomor famine. The Great Purges (1936–38) executed hundreds of thousands and sent millions to [[gulag|gulags]]. Created a [[totalitarian state]] that controlled all aspects of Soviet life. His brutal methods and results became the defining comparison case for authoritarian economic development.

**Vladimir Lenin** *(background context)*
Bolshevik revolutionary leader; founded the Soviet state after the 1917 Revolution. Introduced the [[New Economic Plan (NEP)]] in 1921 as a pragmatic compromise to stabilize the devastated post-Civil War economy. Died 1924; his NEP was abandoned by Stalin within a few years.

## Leaders of Economic Nationalism

**Lázaro Cárdenas**
Mexican president (1934–1940). Carried out sweeping land redistribution under the agrarian reform provisions of the Mexican constitution. Most famously, nationalized Mexico's oil industry in 1938 over fierce resistance from U.S. and British oil companies, creating [[PEMEX]] (Petróleos Mexicanos). His 1938 speech cited foreign companies' exploitation of "native labor, tax exemptions, economic privileges, and governmental tolerance" as justification. His presidency is the AP's key example of economic nationalism in Latin America — using state ownership of strategic industries to assert sovereignty against foreign imperialism.

> [!tip]
> Note the U.S. response: FDR's "Good Neighbor Policy" led the U.S. to accept Mexico's nationalization without military retaliation — a significant departure from earlier U.S. interventionism in Latin America. Connect this to the broader shift in U.S. foreign policy in the interwar period.

## States & Movements

**Soviet Union (U.S.S.R.)**
The world's first communist state. Under Stalin's Five-Year Plans, it grew from a primarily agricultural economy into a major industrial power in less than a decade — at the cost of millions of lives. Presented itself to the world as proof that planned economies could succeed where capitalism failed. Supported the Republican side in the Spanish Civil War (sending advisers and equipment). Becomes a major superpower after WWII.

**Fascist Italy**
Mussolini's Italy (1922–1943) pioneered the fascist model: single-party rule, suppression of opposition, state control of the economy through corporatism, aggressive nationalism, and imperial expansion (Libya, Italian Somaliland, Ethiopia). Allied with Nazi Germany via the Rome-Berlin Axis (1936). Intervened in the Spanish Civil War. Represents the first and in some ways more moderate version of fascism compared to Nazi Germany.

**Nazi Germany**
Hitler's Germany (1933–1945) took fascism to its genocidal extreme. Rearmament spending ended the Depression-era unemployment, giving Hitler popular legitimacy. The Nuremberg Laws stripped Jews of citizenship (1935). Military rearmament in violation of Versailles began the road to WWII. Germany's intervention in Spain — especially the Luftwaffe's bombing of Guernica — previewed the tactics of the coming world war.

**The Spanish Republic and Civil War**
The Second Spanish Republic (1931–1939) was a fragile democracy caught between left-wing and right-wing extremes. The Popular Front's 1936 election victory triggered Franco's Nationalist military uprising. The resulting Civil War (1936–1939) became a proxy war: Hitler and Mussolini backed Franco's Nationalists; the Soviet Union aided the Republicans; international volunteers (the International Brigades) fought on the Republican side. Franco's victory in 1939 added another authoritarian state to Europe on the eve of WWII.

**Mexico's Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI)**
Founded in 1929 to consolidate power after the Mexican Revolution. Dominated Mexican politics for 71 years (until 2000). Under Cárdenas, the PRI implemented land reform and economic nationalism while maintaining single-party control. Represents a common post-revolutionary pattern in the Global South: a nominally reformist party that monopolizes power in the name of the revolution.

> [!warning]
> A common AP trap: the Spanish Civil War's sides have confusing names. The "Nationalists" (Franco) were the REBELS against the elected government. The "Republicans/Loyalists" were the DEFENDERS of the elected democracy. Don't let the word "Nationalist" imply they were the legitimate government.
