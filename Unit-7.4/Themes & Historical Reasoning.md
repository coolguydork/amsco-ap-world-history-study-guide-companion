# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**AMSCO pp. 480–492**

## AP Themes: ECN, GOV, CDI

### ECN — Economic Systems Under Stress

The [[Concept Notes/Great Depression|Great Depression]] was the defining economic event of the interwar period, exposing the fragility of liberal capitalist economies. When market mechanisms failed to self-correct, states intervened — but in radically different ways. The U.S. New Deal preserved capitalism while regulating it more heavily. The Soviet Five-Year Plans replaced capitalism entirely with a command economy. Fascist corporatism claimed to transcend capitalism by subordinating it to the state. All three responses permanently enlarged the role of government in economic life — a major continuity running from the 1930s through the post-WWII era.

The Depression also devastated colonial and semi-colonial economies dependent on commodity exports. This drove economic nationalism in Latin America: Cárdenas's oil nationalization in Mexico was a direct response to the vulnerability of export-dependent economies to foreign corporate power. This pattern anticipated the wave of decolonization-era nationalizations in Asia and Africa after WWII.

### GOV — Political Responses to Economic Crisis

The deeper story of Topic 7.4 is how economic crisis reshapes political systems. The Great Depression's mass unemployment and social despair created the conditions for authoritarian takeover in countries where democracy was already weak (Germany, Italy) or nonexistent (Soviet Union). The pattern is causal: economic catastrophe → loss of faith in liberal institutions → turn to strongmen promising order, jobs, and national greatness.

The contrasting case — the United States — shows that strong democratic institutions and a reformist response (New Deal) could survive economic crisis without turning fascist. This contrast is a favorite AP comparison prompt.

### CDI — Cultural & Ideological Conflict

The Spanish Civil War crystallized the ideological conflict of the era: democracy vs. [[Concept Notes/Fascism|fascism]], with communism as a third player. The bombing of Guernica made this abstract conflict visceral — a modern air force deliberately targeting civilians. Picasso's painting transformed the event into a global symbol of fascist barbarism. The international response (volunteers, propaganda, foreign intervention) shows that this was understood as an ideological war, not merely a local Spanish conflict.

The Soviet Union presented its command economy as an ideological alternative to capitalism — a propaganda victory during the Depression years when Western capitalism appeared to have failed. The appeal of communism to Western intellectuals in the 1930s must be understood in this context.

## AP Skill: Causation

**The Depression → Political Radicalism chain:**

The Great Depression → mass unemployment and social desperation → collapse of faith in liberal democracy → rise of fascism (Germany, Italy, Spain) and communist appeal (Soviet Union, Western intellectuals). This causation argument is the central narrative of Topic 7.4 and appears constantly on the AP exam.

Key causal layers to know:
- WWI reparations → Weimar hyperinflation → German middle-class resentment → Nazi constituency
- Wall Street Crash (1929) → global credit contraction → unemployment worldwide → destabilization of democratic governments
- Economic despair → popular support for strongmen promising "order" and national revival
- Soviet alternative model → appeal of communism during Depression (but note: this appeal coexisted with Stalin's terror)

**Multiple causes of fascism's rise:**
The AP expects nuance — not just "the Depression caused fascism." Pre-existing factors mattered enormously: weak democratic traditions in Germany and Italy, humiliation of Versailles, fear of communist revolution among elites, and long-standing traditions of nationalism and militarism. The Depression was the trigger; these were the underlying conditions.

## AP Skill: Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

**What changed:** The role of the state in economic life expanded dramatically and permanently across all political systems. Before WWI, most governments adhered to laissez-faire orthodoxy. After the Depression, activist government — whether Keynesian, fascist, or communist — became the norm.

**What continued:** Imperial economic exploitation of colonized peoples continued, as Cárdenas's speech made clear. Foreign corporations extracted resources from Mexico, paying low wages, receiving tax exemptions, and using political connections to maintain dominance — the same patterns seen during the height of imperialism. Economic nationalism (PEMEX) was a new response to an old problem.

**Long-term continuity:** The expanded welfare state, government regulation of financial markets, and state-owned enterprises in strategic industries were all Depression-era innovations that persisted through the Cold War and beyond.

## Historical Perspectives

**Historiography on the New Deal:**

Historians debate whether the New Deal actually ended the Great Depression. Liberal historians (Arthur Schlesinger Jr.) praise it as saving American capitalism and democracy. Conservative historians argue it prolonged the Depression by creating uncertainty for investors. Economic historians like Christina Romer find that WWII spending — not the New Deal — was what truly ended unemployment. The AP doesn't require you to resolve this debate, but knowing it exists shows sophisticated historical thinking.

**Historiography on Fascism's Origins:**

Historians debate whether fascism emerged primarily from economic crisis (the Depression enabled Hitler) or from longer-term structural factors (German authoritarianism, hyperinflation, Versailles resentment). Hannah Arendt's *Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951) connects [[Concept Notes/Nazism\|Nazism]] to imperialism and bureaucratic dehumanization. More recently, historians like Robert Paxton have emphasized fascism as a political *behavior* — a set of practices — rather than a fixed ideology. For the AP, the key point is that economic crisis alone does not produce fascism; pre-existing political weaknesses matter.

**Historiography on Stalin:**

Western historians emphasize the terror, famine, and gulags as evidence that Stalinism was totalitarianism comparable to Nazism. Soviet-era historians presented the Five-Year Plans as heroic industrialization. Revisionist historians (Sheila Fitzpatrick) have examined how ordinary Soviet citizens navigated and sometimes benefited from the system. The AP typically tests whether students understand both the scale of Soviet industrialization AND its human cost.

## Think As a Historian: The Mexican Oil Nationalization

The AMSCO Think As a Historian document (pp. 491–492) presents U.S. Secretary of State Cordell Hull's letter protesting Mexico's oil nationalization. Hull argued that international law requires "prompt, adequate, and effective" compensation for expropriated property. Cárdenas countered that Mexico had every right to control its own resources to break foreign exploitation.

This exchange illustrates a recurring tension in world history: the conflict between international property law (protecting foreign investors' rights) and national sovereignty (the right of states to control their own resources). The AP uses this as a lens for understanding economic nationalism more broadly — from 1930s Mexico to post-WWII decolonization.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Cárdenas speech and Hull letter are primary sources the AP has used in DBQ and SAQ contexts. Practice identifying the argument each source makes and the historical situation that shaped it.
