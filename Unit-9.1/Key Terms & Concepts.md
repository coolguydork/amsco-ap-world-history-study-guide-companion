# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 9.1 — Advances in Technology and Exchange After 1900 | AMSCO pp. 633–640**

---

**radio**
Wireless communication technology developed by Guglielmo Marconi (first transatlantic signal, 1901); became the first form of mass media broadcasting. Radio connected distant populations through shared news, music, and culture — a precursor to the later internet in creating simultaneous global audiences. On AP essays, radio illustrates the acceleration of cultural diffusion in Period 4.

**internet**
A global network of interconnected computers emerging from U.S. military research (ARPANET) during the Cold War; became a public communication tool by the late 1990s. The internet accelerated globalization by enabling near-instant sharing of information across borders, fueling both economic integration and cultural homogenization. Frequently paired on the AP with the Arab Spring as evidence of technology enabling political change.

**[[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]**
Agricultural transformation beginning in the 1960s that introduced high-yield crop varieties (especially wheat and rice), expanded irrigation, and increased use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides, dramatically increasing food production in Asia, Latin America, and parts of Africa. Associated with Norman Borlaug. The Green Revolution reduced famine in the short term but concentrated landholding and caused environmental damage — a classic AP nuance point.

**crossbreeding**
The deliberate breeding of two plant or animal varieties to produce a hybrid offspring with desired traits such as higher yield or disease resistance. A key early technique of the Green Revolution, crossbreeding produced new strains of wheat and rice central to the food supply increases of the 1960s–70s. Distinct from genetic engineering, which came later and operates at the DNA level.

**genetic engineering**
The direct manipulation of an organism's DNA to alter its basic characteristics, used to create GMOs resistant to pests, drought, or disease. Represents the next phase of agricultural technology beyond crossbreeding. Raises ongoing debates about food sovereignty, loss of biodiversity, and corporate control of seed supplies — themes that connect to AP questions about globalization's trade-offs.

**shipping containers**
Standardized metal boxes that transfer seamlessly between ships, trucks, and trains, revolutionizing global freight logistics after the 1950s. By drastically reducing loading costs and transit times, containers made intercontinental manufacturing supply chains economically viable and drove the explosion of consumer goods trade. A key material driver of late-20th-century economic globalization.

**antibiotic**
A substance (originally derived from fungi or bacteria) that kills or inhibits bacterial infections; penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928, was the first. Antibiotics transformed medicine by making previously fatal bacterial diseases treatable and contributed directly to the 20th-century population explosion by sharply reducing mortality. Fleming warned that overuse would produce resistant strains — a problem that became a major public-health concern by the late 20th century.

**birth control**
Methods to prevent pregnancy, most significantly the oral contraceptive pill developed by Gregory Pincus in the early 1950s and approved in the U.S. in 1960. Reliable birth control gave women greater agency over reproduction and contributed to declining fertility rates in developed countries. Frequently connected on the AP exam to women's rights movements, the demographic transition, and gender equity debates.

**fertility rates**
The average number of children born per woman in a population. Declining fertility rates — driven by birth control, urbanization, and rising women's education — produced aging populations in wealthy nations. Meanwhile, many developing nations experienced continued population growth, creating demographic contrasts central to AP Period 4 analysis and to arguments about globalization's uneven effects.

**vaccine**
A biological preparation that stimulates the immune system to protect against a specific disease. Though vaccines date to 1796, post-1900 government and international campaigns distributed them at scale: smallpox was eradicated by 1980, polio and measles became rare. The WHO estimated vaccines prevented ~3 million deaths annually in the 21st century. International vaccine campaigns exemplify the post-1945 trend of global health cooperation through organizations like the WHO.

> [!exam-tip]
> For LEQ/DBQ evidence, pair terms: **crossbreeding + Green Revolution** = agricultural causation; **antibiotic + vaccine** = medical causation of population growth; **internet + shipping containers** = material drivers of globalization. These pairings let you write efficiently under time pressure.
