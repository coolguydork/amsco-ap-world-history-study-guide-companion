## Key Figures

**Norman Borlaug (1914–2009)**
American agronomist and the central figure of the [[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]. Working through the Rockefeller Foundation's agricultural research programs in Mexico, Borlaug developed high-yield, disease-resistant wheat varieties through crossbreeding. His work expanded to South Asia in the 1960s, dramatically increasing wheat production in India and Pakistan and averting feared famines. He was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. In his Nobel lecture, Borlaug himself warned that the Green Revolution's success was "temporary" unless population growth was also curbed — a nuanced assessment the AP frequently tests through primary source excerpts.

> [!exam-tip]
> Borlaug appears in MCQ passages and as DBQ document context. Know: (1) his method was crossbreeding, not genetic engineering; (2) he won the Nobel *Peace* Prize, not science; (3) he acknowledged the Green Revolution's limits — it bought time, not a permanent solution.

**Alexander Fleming (1881–1955)**
Scottish biologist who discovered penicillin in 1928 when he noticed that a fungus (*Penicillium*) was killing bacteria in his lab — a famous example of scientific accident leading to major discovery. Penicillin, the first antibiotic, saved enormous numbers of lives during World War II and transformed civilian medicine afterward. Fleming won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945. His Nobel acceptance speech warned presciently about antibiotic resistance — a warning now recognized as prophetic.

> [!tip]
> Fleming → penicillin → WWII lives saved → post-war population growth is a tight causation chain useful for SAQ part (B) or LEQ evidence.

**Gregory Pincus (1903–1967)**
American scientist who developed the oral contraceptive pill in the early 1950s by working with hormones that suppressed ovulation. The pill was approved by the U.S. government for widespread use in 1960. Pincus's work had sweeping social consequences: declining fertility rates in the developed world, transformation of sexual practices, and greater female participation in education and the workforce. His work is directly connected to AP arguments about gender equity and women's rights.

**Tim Berners-Lee (b. 1955)**
British computer scientist at CERN who invented the World Wide Web in 1989 — the system of linked pages and hyperlinks that made the internet accessible to the general public. While the internet's underlying infrastructure (ARPANET) was a U.S. military project, Berners-Lee's WWW turned it into a global communication and commercial network. His invention is arguably the most consequential single technological act of the late 20th century.

**Yuri Gagarin (1934–1968)**
Soviet cosmonaut who became the first human in space on April 12, 1961. Gagarin's flight was a pivotal moment in the Cold War Space Race, demonstrating Soviet technological capability and intensifying U.S. investment in science and engineering. The Space Race is part of the broader AP narrative of state-directed technological competition producing civilian spin-offs (satellite communications, GPS, early computing).

## Key Organizations & States

**United States (Defense Department / NASA)**
The U.S. military funded ARPANET — the precursor to the internet — as a Cold War communications resilience project. NASA's Apollo program (funded by the U.S. government) landed humans on the moon in 1969, representing peak state-directed technology investment. The U.S. also drove the pharmaceutical and agricultural technology revolutions through university research funding and the Rockefeller Foundation.

**World Health Organization (WHO)**
The UN's specialized health agency, founded in 1948, coordinated global vaccine distribution campaigns. WHO-led efforts eradicated smallpox by 1980 and dramatically reduced polio and measles worldwide. The WHO is a primary example for AP essays on the role of international institutions in promoting human welfare in the post-1945 world (connects to [[Unit-9.8/Institutions Developing in a Globalized World Overview|Topic 9.8]]).

> [!tip]
> When the AP asks about institutions enabling globalized cooperation, the WHO on vaccines is a strong, specific piece of evidence — much more precise than vague references to "the UN."
