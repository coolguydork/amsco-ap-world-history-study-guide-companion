## Multiple-Choice Practice

*Source: AMSCO, pp. 637–638*

**Questions 1–3** refer to the following passage:

> "For the underprivileged billions in the forgotten world, hunger has been a constant companion, and starvation has all too often lurked in the nearby shadows. To millions of these unfortunates, who have long lived in despair, the Green Revolution seems like a miracle that has generated new hope for the future. . . .
>
> The Green Revolution has won a temporary success in man's war against hunger and deprivation; it has given man a breathing space. If fully implemented, the revolution can provide sufficient food for sustenance during the next three decades. But the frightening power of human reproduction must also be curbed; otherwise the success of the Green Revolution will be ephemeral [temporary] only."
>
> — Norman Borlaug, agricultural scientist, Nobel lecture, 1970

---

**Question 1.** According to this excerpt, Dr. Borlaug feared that

(A) the Green Revolution had not succeeded in reducing world hunger
(B) scientists had gone too far in altering plant species
(C) the world's population would begin to decline due to efforts to curb hunger
(D) global population could once again overtake the available food supply

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> Borlaug explicitly says the Green Revolution's success will be "ephemeral" unless population growth is curbed — meaning he fears population will outrun food supply again. (A) is directly contradicted — he praises the Green Revolution as a success. (B) is not in the passage. (C) inverts his concern (he fears overpopulation, not depopulation).

---

**Question 2.** One criticism of the process discussed in this passage is that

(A) engineering cannot change a grain's genetic characteristics
(B) it ignores new techniques of irrigating and fertilizing farmlands
(C) it could increase the number of pollinating insects, such as bees and beetles
(D) its costs make small farmers unable to compete with large-scale farming

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> AMSCO explicitly states that many small farmers could not afford the new fertilizers and pesticides, leaving them unable to compete with large landowners. (A) is factually wrong — genetic engineering does alter characteristics. (B) inverts reality — the Green Revolution *used* irrigation and fertilizers extensively. (C) inverts the bee concern — genetic engineering *threatened* pollinators, not increased them.

---

**Question 3.** Which most directly led to the increase in food supply as part of the Green Revolution?

(A) The colonization of Africa by several European powers and resulting control over food production
(B) The crossbreeding and genetic engineering of crops, such as wheat, rice, and other grains
(C) The decolonization of Africa and subsequent repurposing of arable farmlands for the production of cereal grains
(D) The domestication of new types of beasts of burden to help farm laborers cultivate land

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Crossbreeding and genetic engineering to produce high-yield crop varieties is the defining mechanism of the Green Revolution. (A) and (C) both involve Africa but have no direct connection to the Green Revolution's methods. (D) describes pre-modern agricultural technology, not a Green Revolution technique.

## Short-Answer Practice

*Source: AMSCO, pp. 639*

**SAQ 1.** Use the passage below to answer all parts.

> "Gender equity is defined by the World Health Organization as, 'the fairness and justice in the distribution of benefits and responsibilities between women and men.' When women can have control over their fertility, they have control of their lives which creates a society where gender equity can occur with all genders benefiting from the same resources. Contraceptive use promotes gender equality by improving the accessibility of education, employment and health care to women. If women are using contraception to postpone or prevent a pregnancy from occurring, they are able to stay in school and seek gainful employment upon completion."
>
> — Linda Hanes, *The Birth of Development*, 2016

**(A)** Identify ONE way Hanes claims that contraceptive use promotes gender equality.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identify
> Hanes argues that contraceptive use promotes gender equality by enabling women to remain in school and pursue employment. When women can postpone or prevent pregnancy, they gain access to education and economic opportunity on terms more equal to men.

**(B)** Explain ONE historical development that supports Hanes's argument.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explain/Support
> The introduction of the oral contraceptive pill in 1960 supports Hanes's argument. After U.S. government approval of the pill, women's workforce participation rose significantly across subsequent decades, from roughly 20% in the early 20th century to nearly 45% by the 1990s. This correlation — rising contraceptive access alongside rising female employment — reflects the causal mechanism Hanes describes: fertility control enables women to prioritize education and careers.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which practices described by Hanes changed the world from 1900 to the present.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explain/Change
> Birth control practices contributed to declining fertility rates across the developed world, fundamentally reshaping demographics. In nations with widespread contraceptive access, the average number of children per woman dropped significantly over the 20th century, producing aging populations that altered workforce structures, social welfare systems, and immigration patterns. This demographic shift represents a major continuity-and-change development that persists into the present.

**SAQ 2.** Answer all parts.

**(A)** Identify ONE medical innovation that increased the ability of humans to live longer lives from 1900 to the present.

> [!tip] Model Response — Identify
> The development of antibiotics, beginning with Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928, dramatically extended human lifespans by making previously fatal bacterial infections treatable.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which energy technologies changed the world from 1900 to the present.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explain/Energy
> The widespread adoption of petroleum as a primary energy source after 1900 enabled faster and cheaper transportation of goods, accelerating global trade. Oil-powered ships, trucks, and aircraft created the logistical infrastructure for economic globalization — but also produced greenhouse gas emissions that contributed to climate change, illustrating the trade-off between technological progress and environmental sustainability.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which new types of communication affected how people obtained information from 1900 to the present.

> [!tip] Model Response — Explain/Communication
> The internet, which became publicly accessible in the 1990s, transformed how people accessed information by shifting from centralized broadcast media (radio, television controlled by governments or corporations) to decentralized, user-generated content. Social media platforms allowed individuals to both consume and produce information in real time, as demonstrated during the Arab Spring (2010s), when protesters used social media to coordinate demonstrations and share footage of government repression across national borders.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

*In one to three paragraphs, explain how the development of new technology changed the world since 1900.*

> [!tip] Model Response
> Since 1900, technological advances in communication, agriculture, and medicine have fundamentally transformed how humans live, work, and interact across the globe. In communication and transportation, innovations from radio to the internet progressively compressed distance until geographic barriers became nearly irrelevant to the exchange of ideas and goods. Shipping containers and air travel made global supply chains economically viable, while the internet enabled instant cross-border communication that accelerated cultural diffusion and, in some cases, political mobilization — as seen during the Arab Spring.
>
> Agricultural technology, particularly the Green Revolution's high-yield crops developed through crossbreeding, dramatically increased food production and reduced the risk of famine in Asia and Latin America. Medical innovations — antibiotics, vaccines, and reliable birth control — extended lifespans and reshaped demographic patterns. Antibiotics and vaccines reduced mortality from infectious disease, contributing to a global population explosion; birth control pills gave women greater reproductive agency and contributed to declining fertility rates in the developed world, reshaping gender roles and workforce composition.
>
> Yet each technological advance carried costs and trade-offs. The Green Revolution displaced small farmers and damaged soil; fossil fuels produced climate change; antibiotics generated resistant bacterial strains. Norman Borlaug's own warning — that the Green Revolution bought only "a breathing space" — captures the central tension: technology creates new possibilities while also generating new problems that future generations must solve.
