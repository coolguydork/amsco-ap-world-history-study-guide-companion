# Advances in Technology & Exchange

**Unit 9 — Globalization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 633–640**

**Essential Question:** How have advances in technology transformed communication, transportation, and exchange since 1900?

**AP Themes:** TEC (Technology), ECN (Economy), CDI (Cultural Diffusion), ENV (Environment)
**AP Skills:** Causation, Continuity and Change Over Time

---

## The Big Picture

Topic 9.1 opens Unit 9 by establishing *why the world feels smaller after 1900*. Three overlapping revolutions — in communication/transportation, agriculture, and medicine — compressed distance, multiplied food supplies, and extended human lifespans simultaneously. The AP exam treats this topic as a **causation** anchor: every subsequent Unit 9 topic (disease spread, environmental strain, economic globalization, cultural homogenization) traces back to the technologies introduced here.

The core argument: technology after 1900 accelerated globalization so rapidly that it created both enormous benefits (reduced famine, longer lives, instant communication) and new risks (antibiotic resistance, environmental damage, widening inequality between those who could and could not access the new technologies).

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently pairs this topic with a primary source (like the Norman Borlaug Nobel lecture) and asks you to explain ONE cause or ONE effect of a specific technology. Practice moving from the general ([[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]) to the specific (crossbreeding → higher-yield wheat → reduced famine in India and Mexico) and back to the thematic (technology → globalization → increased exchange).

## Communication & Transportation

Radio, developed in the early 1900s, was the first true mass medium, connecting distant populations through news and culture. By mid-century, television extended radio's reach visually. The most transformative leap came in the late 20th century: the **[[Concept Notes/Internet\|internet]]**, originally developed by the U.S. Defense Department as ARPANET during the Cold War, became a public tool by the 1990s and rewired global commerce, politics, and culture.

Mobile phones and social media (Twitter, Facebook) put information creation in individual hands. The political consequences were immediate: videos of police brutality sparked accountability movements; the **Arab Spring** (2010s) saw social media amplify anti-government protests across North Africa and the Middle East.

Transportation accelerated in parallel. Air travel moved roughly 2 million passengers daily by the late 20th century. **Shipping containers** — standardized metal boxes that transfer seamlessly between ships, trucks, and trains — slashed global freight costs after the 1950s, making intercontinental manufacturing supply chains economically viable.

> [!tip]
> The internet → Arab Spring connection is a favorite SAQ and DBQ prompt. It illustrates technology enabling political change — link it to the AP theme of Governance (GOV) alongside Technology (TEC).

## The Green Revolution

In the mid-20th century, scientists developed new varieties of wheat, rice, and other grains through **crossbreeding** (hybridizing two plant varieties) and, later, **genetic engineering** (directly manipulating DNA). Combined with expanded irrigation, chemical fertilizers, and pesticides, the result was a dramatic increase in global food production, especially in Asia and Latin America. Norman Borlaug, the American agronomist who developed high-yield wheat, won the Nobel Prize and is called the "father of the Green Revolution."

The Green Revolution was not without costs. Small farmers often could not afford new fertilizers or machinery, forcing many off their land and concentrating holdings among large landowners. Fertilizers and pesticides degraded soil and water systems. Genetic engineering raised concerns about declining pollinator populations (bees) and the loss of traditional seed varieties.

> [!warning]
> Don't oversimplify the Green Revolution as purely positive. The AP exam rewards nuance: it increased food production but deepened inequality between large and small farmers, and caused environmental damage. Borlaug himself warned in 1970 that population growth could overwhelm the gains.

## Energy Technologies

In 1900, coal dominated global energy. Petroleum and natural gas expanded rapidly during the 20th century, fueling industrial output and enabling cheap transportation. Nuclear research from the 1930s–40s (originally military) was redirected to civilian power plants by the 1950s.

All fossil fuels are non-renewable and produce greenhouse gases that trap heat in the atmosphere, contributing to climate change. Nuclear power, while producing minimal carbon emissions, generates radioactive waste and carries accident risks. By the late 20th century, renewable energy (wind, solar) supplied roughly 5% of global output — a growing but still minor share.

## Medical Innovations

Three medical revolutions extended human lifespans and reshaped demographics after 1900:

**Antibiotics:** Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928 — the first **antibiotic**. Deployed widely in World War II, antibiotics saved countless soldiers from wound infections. Post-war civilian use reduced mortality from bacterial diseases dramatically. Fleming warned, however, that overuse would select for antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains — a problem that became acute by the late 20th century.

**Birth Control:** In the early 1950s, Gregory Pincus developed the oral contraceptive pill, approved for widespread use in the U.S. in 1960. Reliable **birth control** reduced **fertility rates** globally, transformed gender roles, and gave women greater agency over reproduction. By 2018, over 300 million women used modern contraception.

**Vaccines:** Though vaccines date to 1796, post-1900 government and international campaigns distributed them at scale. [[Concept Notes/Smallpox\|Smallpox]] was eradicated by 1980; polio and measles became rare. The WHO estimated vaccines prevented ~3 million deaths annually by the 21st century.

> [!exam-tip]
> Medical innovations → declining fertility rates → demographic transition is a high-frequency CCOT pattern. The AP expects you to connect birth control to women's rights movements and to the contrast between aging wealthy-nation populations and growing developing-nation populations.
