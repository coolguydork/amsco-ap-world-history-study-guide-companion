# AP Exam Common Traps

These are the misconceptions, false assumptions, and terminology confusions that cost students points on the AP World History exam. Compiled from `[!warning]` callouts across all 71 topic notes.

**How to use this document:** Read through once before your first full practice exam. Return to the relevant period section the night before the exam. If you can articulate *why* each trap is wrong — not just that it is — you're ready.

---

## Period 1 (c. 1200–1450) — Units 1–2

### Unit 1

**The civil service exam did NOT create equality.** *(1.1)*
The poor were underrepresented, and by the late Song the bureaucracy had grown so expensive that it drained the empire's surplus wealth — contributing to its eventual decline. Don't romanticize the exam system as meritocratic in the modern sense.

**The Yuan Dynasty belongs to Topic 2.2, not 1.1.** *(1.1)*
In Topic 1.1, you only need to know that Mongol conquest ended the Song (1279) and that the Ming expelled the Mongols (1368). Save the Pax Mongolica, the plague, and Kublai Khan for Unit 2.

**The fall of Baghdad (1258) ended the Abbasid Caliphate — but not Islam.** *(1.2)*
This is one of the most significant dates in Period 1. The political structure collapsed; the cultural and religious civilization did not. Dar al-Islam continued to expand after 1258. Don't conflate the collapse of a state with the collapse of a civilization.

**Islam did not introduce the veil.** *(1.2)*
Head and face coverings were already common in Central Asia and the Byzantine Empire before Muhammad. Islam codified an existing practice, not an entirely new one. The AP may ask about the origins of practices attributed to Islam.

**The Abbasid Caliphate didn't collapse all at once.** *(1.2)*
It was progressively hollowed out — Seljuks took real power in the 11th century, the Crusades added pressure in the 11th–12th centuries, Baghdad's trade position eroded — before the Mongols delivered the final blow in 1258. Understand the process, not just the date.

**Do not overstate Islamic tolerance.** *(1.2)*
The dhimmi system was a pragmatic governance tool, not modern pluralism. Non-Muslims paid extra taxes, faced legal disadvantages, and could face discrimination. "Toleration" in this context means "permitted to exist," not "treated equally."

**Bhakti ≠ Sufism.** *(1.3)*
Both are mystical, both are inclusive — but Bhakti is a Hindu movement and Sufism is Islamic. They are parallel developments within different religions. The AP may ask you to compare them; do not confuse them.

**Rajput ≠ Chola.** *(1.3)*
Rajput kingdoms: Hindu, decentralized, northern India. Chola Dynasty: Hindu, more centralized, southern India. They represent two very different political patterns within the same region.

**The Inca had no alphabetic writing — but they did have quipu.** *(1.4)*
Don't say the Inca had "no writing." Quipu served recording functions for census data, tribute records, and possibly narrative. The distinction matters for AP arguments about communication, record-keeping, and state complexity.

**Don't skip North American cultures.** *(1.4)*
Cahokia's scale (comparable to contemporary European cities) and matrilineal succession patterns are favorite AP points. These cultures appear less frequently than Maya/Aztec/Inca but can appear on MCQs and SAQs.

**"Stateless" does not mean chaotic.** *(1.5)*
In the context of African societies, "stateless" means governance was organized at the kinship/village level rather than through a formal state apparatus. Many such societies were remarkably stable and well-governed within their scale. Don't treat "no formal state" as "no civilization."

**Mali's major story is in Topic 2.4, not 1.5.** *(1.5)*
For Topic 1.5, you need: Sundiata as founder, Mansa Musa's hajj as a display of wealth. Timbuktu, Songhai, trans-Saharan trade expansion — those are [[Unit-2.4/Trans-Saharan Trade Routes Overview|Topic 2.4]].

**The Great Schism (1054) ≠ the Western Schism (1378–1417).** *(1.6)*
The Great Schism split Catholic and Orthodox Christianity. The Western Schism was when multiple claimants contested the papacy simultaneously. The AP exam period covers 1200–1450, so the Western Schism is in-period. Know which is which.

**Topic 1.7 is a skills topic, not a content topic.** *(1.7)*
It does not introduce new historical facts. The content comes from Topics 1.1–1.6. Your job is to practice organizing that content for comparison arguments. Students who treat it as new material to memorize miss the point.

**False parallelism is the most common comparison error.** *(1.7)*
Don't assume that because two regions share one feature (both used religion to legitimize power, both traded extensively), their situations were essentially the same. Always specify *how* the feature worked differently in each context.

### Unit 2

**The Silk Roads were not invented in Period 2.** *(2.1)*
They originated in the Han Dynasty era (c. 200 BCE). What changed after 1200 was the scale, security, and commercial sophistication of trade on routes that had existed for over a millennium. Don't write as if the Mongols created the Silk Roads.

**The Pax Mongolica spread the plague.** *(2.2)*
The same roads that carried silk and ideas also carried the bubonic plague, which moved from southern China through Central Asia to Europe, killing perhaps one-third of Europe's population. The AP loves asking about this unintended consequence of connectivity.

**Zheng He was not a commercial explorer like Columbus.** *(2.3)*
He was primarily on tribute missions — projecting Ming imperial prestige — not seeking trade routes. The contrast with European exploration is a high-frequency AP comparison. Columbus sought profit; Zheng He sought deference.

**The trans-Saharan trade moved enslaved people — before the Atlantic slave trade.** *(2.4)*
Don't let the Atlantic Slave Trade crowd out this earlier system. Trans-Saharan slavery predated and differed from the Atlantic trade in scale, organization, and demographic impact.

**Always specify mechanism AND result when writing about diffusion.** *(2.5)*
Don't just write "trade spread ideas." Write "Buddhism spread via Silk Roads merchants and monks (*mechanism*), resulting in the syncretic Chan Buddhism in China (*result*)." Generic claims will not earn AP evidence points.

**The Little Ice Age ≠ the Black Death.** *(2.6)*
Both stressed Europe c. 1300–1350, but they are distinct. The Little Ice Age is climate-driven; the Black Death is disease-driven. Both reduced agricultural output, but through different mechanisms. Don't conflate them.

**Patriarchy was dominant — exceptions don't disprove the pattern.** *(2.7)*
Southeast Asia, West Africa, and the Mongol Empire did offer women more autonomy than other regions. The AP expects you to acknowledge exceptions *while* recognizing that patriarchy was the dominant system across virtually all regions in this era.

## Period 2 (c. 1450–1750) — Units 3–4

### Unit 3

**"Gunpowder Empires" means only the three Islamic empires.** *(3.1)*
The term specifically refers to the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires. Russia and China also used gunpowder and expanded in this era, but they are analyzed separately. Calling Russia or China a "Gunpowder Empire" in an AP essay will cost you points.

**Devshirme boys were not simply enslaved laborers.** *(3.2)*
They received elite educations and could rise to the highest offices in the empire. The Janissaries were the most powerful military force of their era. The system is better understood as forced recruitment into privileged service — a critical distinction for both MCQs and essays.

**The Mughal Empire was Sunni, not Shia.** *(3.3)*
The Safavids are the Shia empire. The Mughals were Sunni, but under Akbar they practiced wide religious tolerance. Don't conflate the two gunpowder empires on this point — it's a frequent MCQ trap.

**The Protestant Reformation was not the only significant religious development of 1450–1750.** *(3.3)*
The AP expects you to discuss Islamic schisms and new religions (Sikhism) with equal weight. The Learning Objective says "various belief systems" — plural. Eurocentric focus on the Reformation alone misses the scope of the prompt.

**Peace of Augsburg (1555) ≠ Peace of Westphalia (1648).** *(3.3)*
Augsburg ended the first German religious war and recognized only Lutheranism. Westphalia ended the Thirty Years' War and added Calvinism. Students routinely mix these up. Key difference: Augsburg = two options; Westphalia = three.

### Unit 4

**Specific technologies have specific origins.** *(4.1)*
Compass = China. Astrolabe = improved by Islamic scholars. Lateen sail = Arab/Indian Ocean. Gunpowder = China. Only the caravel is genuinely a Portuguese synthesis of multiple traditions. Never attribute a technology to the civilization that *used* it most visibly rather than the one that invented it.

**China did not "fail" to explore because it lacked technology.** *(4.2)*
China had the technology. The withdrawal after Zheng He was a deliberate political decision by the Ming court, driven by Confucian scholars who opposed merchants and overseas ventures. Avoid framing this as inability — it was a choice. This is a critical distinction for SAQ/LEQ responses.

**Portuguese trading post empire ≠ Spanish territorial empire.** *(4.2)*
Portugal extracted profit through trade control; Spain extracted it through land, labor, and tribute. Compare their administrative methods when asked about European expansion — they were fundamentally different models.

**Disease, not weapons, caused the demographic collapse of indigenous Americans.** *(4.3)*
Weapons and gunpowder helped conquer indigenous peoples, but disease caused the majority of deaths — estimates range from 50–90% population decline in the Americas within a century of contact. The AP tests this distinction explicitly.

**The "Middle Passage" is the second leg of triangular trade, not the whole route.** *(4.4)*
The Middle Passage refers specifically to the ocean crossing from Africa to the Americas. The "middle" is the second leg: Europe → Africa (first) → Americas (second, the Middle Passage) → Europe (third).

**Mercantilism ≠ capitalism.** *(4.4 / 4.8)*
Mercantilism = government-directed accumulation of bullion and trade surpluses through colonial extraction. Free-market capitalism = private capital and market pricing. Joint-stock companies bridge them — they're privately owned (capitalist) but operate under state charters (mercantilist). Both were operating simultaneously; they're not simply sequential.

**Bartolomé de las Casas is not simply a hero.** *(4.4)*
He initially suggested importing African enslaved labor as a substitute for indigenous labor (though he later recanted). His writing must be read in light of his purpose and audience — a key AP sourcing skill.

**Don't conflate the short-term and long-term effects of the slave trade on Africa.** *(4.5)*
Short-term: demographic disruption and violence. Long-term: Columbian Exchange crops partially offset population decline, but economic dependence set the stage for 19th-century imperialism. The AP may ask you to distinguish these timescales.

**Criollos ≠ peninsulares.** *(4.7)*
Criollos were of European descent but born in the Americas — below peninsulares in the casta system despite being ethnically European. This distinction is tested frequently. The resentment of criollos at being outranked by peninsulares is a major cause of Latin American independence movements.

## Period 3 (c. 1750–1900) — Units 5–6

### Unit 5

**The Enlightenment ≠ the Scientific Revolution.** *(5.1)*
The Scientific Revolution (16th–17th c.) changed how people understood nature. The Enlightenment applied that method — reason and empiricism — to society, politics, and economics. The Enlightenment builds on the Scientific Revolution but is a distinct movement with different subjects and implications.

**The French Revolution was not a steady march toward democracy.** *(5.2)*
It lurched between constitutionalism, radical terror, and military dictatorship before Napoleon took power. The AP may ask you to identify which *phase* of the revolution is being described. Know the phases: moderate constitutionalism → radical Terror → Thermidorian reaction → Directory → Napoleon.

**Realpolitik is not the same as opportunism.** *(5.2)*
Cavour and Bismarck had clear nationalist goals — they just pursued them through manipulation rather than idealism. The AP may ask you to explain how realpolitik differed from Romantic nationalism (Mazzini's approach): same goal, very different methods.

**The Industrial Revolution had structural preconditions, not just clever inventions.** *(5.3)*
The AP rewards noting the role of colonial wealth (from slavery) and the enclosure movement in creating available workers and capital. "Clever inventors" alone is an insufficient explanation for why industrialization began in Britain.

**Don't characterize the decline of Asian manufacturing as an inability to industrialize.** *(5.4)*
These regions continued producing goods. Their relative share declined because European output grew massively AND because colonial policies actively dismantled local industries (e.g., British tariffs destroying Indian textiles). Distinguish internal limitations from external suppression.

**The Bessemer Process produces steel, not iron.** *(5.5)*
Iron came first (First Industrial Revolution); steel mass production is a marker of the Second Industrial Revolution. Don't confuse the two: First = textiles, steam, iron (Britain-led, c. 1760–1840); Second = steel, oil, electricity, chemicals (US/Germany dominant, c. 1870–1914).

**Muhammad Ali (the Egyptian ruler) ≠ the 20th-century boxer.** *(5.6)*
On the AP exam, "Muhammad Ali" in the context of 19th-century industrialization always refers to the Egyptian governor (c. 1805), who modernized Egypt's military and economy through state-directed industrialization.

**Tanzimat (Ottoman) ≠ Meiji (Japan) — same era, very different outcomes.** *(5.6)*
Both were 19th-century state modernization programs. Tanzimat (c. 1839–1876) largely failed to halt decline; Meiji (1868+) succeeded dramatically. The difference: Japan had a coherent, centralized state willing to radically restructure society. The Ottomans faced internal resistance and external debt.

**Laissez-faire ≠ mercantilism.** *(5.7)*
Laissez-faire = no government interference, free markets. Mercantilism = government-directed trade and protectionism. The shift from mercantilism to laissez-faire is itself an AP-testable change for 1750–1900.

**The Meiji Restoration did not *cause* Japan's industrialization.** *(5.8)*
The Restoration created the political conditions for rapid industrialization — but the decision to industrialize was a deliberate government policy choice made *after* 1868. Restoration = political transformation; industrialization = subsequent policy decision.

**Socialism ≠ communism.** *(5.8)*
In Marx's framework, socialism is the transitional stage (state controls production) and communism is the final goal (no classes, no state). On the AP exam, "Marxism" refers to the entire analytical framework — the AP is not asking you to distinguish the two end-states, but to understand the critique of industrial capitalism.

**Industrialization both changed and reinforced patriarchy — it didn't simply liberate women.** *(5.9 / 5.10)*
Women's economic role became more visible and essential, yet legal and political subordination continued. The dominant pattern for 1750–1900 is *continuity* of legal and political gender inequality despite economic change. Don't confuse growth of the women's rights movement with actual achievement of suffrage (which mostly comes after this period).

### Unit 6

**Social Darwinism is a misapplication of Darwin's theory.** *(6.1)*
Darwin's work concerned biological species over millions of years; Social Darwinists falsely applied it to human races to justify conquest. The AP expects you to know this distinction — it's a frequent point-of-view question on DBQs featuring Social Darwinist texts.

**The Berlin Conference did not begin colonization — it formalized it.** *(6.1 / 6.2)*
Colonization was already underway. The Conference (1884–85) formalized rules for a process in motion, and it is better analyzed as evidence of nationalist competition among European powers than as evidence that colonization started there. Also: China was never formally colonized — it experienced economic domination through spheres of influence while retaining its own government.

**The Spanish-American War ≠ the Philippine-American War.** *(6.3)*
The Spanish-American War (1898) transferred colonial authority over the Philippines from Spain to the US. The Philippine-American War (1899–1902) was the Filipino resistance to that transfer. The first ended Spanish colonialism; the second began American colonialism.

**Colonial railroads were not a gift to colonized peoples.** *(6.4)*
They were designed to serve colonial extraction and military control, not local economic development. Indian and African workers built them at suppressed wages. This is a primary example of the "tools of empire" question on the AP — evaluate critically, don't describe as generously beneficial.

**The Open Door Policy (1899) was not altruistic.** *(6.5)*
It proposed that all foreign powers have equal trading access to China — specifically to prevent any single power from monopolizing China and cutting the US out of the market. Frame it as US economic self-interest, not benevolent multilateralism.

**Slavery did not "end" in the 19th century all at once.** *(6.6)*
It ended gradually and unevenly: US (1865), Cuba (1886), Brazil (1888). The continuation of slavery explains why contract and indentured labor became the primary replacement systems. Don't treat abolition as a single global moment.

**The Irish Great Famine was not purely a natural disaster.** *(6.6)*
British colonial policy — continued food exports during starvation, eviction of tenant farmers — was a contributing cause. Frame it as both environmental (blight) and political (colonial indifference and exploitation).

**Rising living standards in industrial Europe ≠ rising living standards globally.** *(6.8)*
For colonized regions, participation in the world economy often meant dependence, vulnerability to market shocks, and extraction of wealth — not development. The AP tests the distinction between the European core and the colonial periphery explicitly.

**Indentured labor ≠ chattel slavery.** *(6.8)*
Indentured laborers had legal contracts and theoretically retained rights; enslaved people were property with no legal personhood. However, conditions of indenture were often exploitative. The AP may ask you to compare and contrast — acknowledge both the legal distinction and the practical similarity.

## Period 4 (c. 1900–present) — Units 7–9

### Unit 7

**WWI was not caused solely by German aggression.** *(7.2)*
Austria-Hungary started the actual shooting war with Serbia. Germany's blank-check support escalated it. The alliance system generalized it. AMSCO specifically notes Germany was blamed unfairly for the full war at Versailles — understanding this is key to explaining why the Treaty created instability.

**The Fourteen Points ≠ the Treaty of Versailles.** *(7.3)*
Wilson *proposed* the Fourteen Points, but the final treaty ignored most of them. The harsh peace Clemenceau imposed — not Wilson's vision — created the conditions for WWII. Don't write that the peace settlement reflected Wilson's principles.

**Hyperinflation (early 1920s Germany) ≠ the Great Depression (1929+).** *(7.4)*
They are two separate economic crises, both contributing to Nazi rise, but at different times. Hyperinflation destroyed the savings of the German middle class in the early 1920s; the Depression destroyed employment in the early 1930s.

**The "Nationalists" in the Spanish Civil War were the rebels, not the government.** *(7.4)*
The Nationalists (Franco) were the rebels against the elected government. The Republicans/Loyalists were the defenders of the elected democracy. The naming is counterintuitive — "Nationalist" does not mean "legitimate."

**The Young Turks began as reformers.** *(7.1)*
They wanted constitutionalism and modernization. Their turn toward ethnic nationalism and scapegoating of Armenians came as the empire weakened under external pressure. Reform movements can become authoritarian when they feel threatened — a pattern the AP tests in multiple contexts.

**Don't conflate the 1917 Mexican Constitution with the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution.** *(7.1)*
Two very different events in the same year. The Mexican Constitution was a reform document enshrining revolutionary goals (land reform, labor rights, secular state). The Bolshevik takeover was a violent seizure of power establishing a communist state.

**Gandhi was not simply anti-Western.** *(7.5)*
He was an English-trained lawyer who used British legal concepts to challenge British law. His genius was turning the empire's own values (rule of law, Christian morality, Enlightenment liberalism) against its practice. Essays that describe him as purely rejecting the West miss his complexity.

**Appeasement was a calculated strategy, not cowardice.** *(7.6)*
Leaders who remembered WWI's devastation believed Hitler had limited, rational aims. It failed because those assumptions were wrong. The AP rewards nuanced answers that explain the logic behind a policy before explaining why it failed. "Cowardice" is a moral judgment, not a historical explanation.

**Anti-Comintern Pact (1936) ≠ German-Soviet Nonaggression Pact (1939).** *(7.6)*
They are essentially opposites in purpose. The Anti-Comintern Pact expressed anti-communism (Germany + Japan). The Nonaggression Pact was strategic cynicism (Germany + the USSR). Students frequently confuse these.

**Stalingrad (1942–43) did not end WWII.** *(7.7)*
Stalingrad was a turning point — it shifted momentum. The war in Europe lasted two more years, ending in May 1945. Know the difference between a turning point and the war's conclusion.

**MAIN is a starting point, not a complete answer.** *(7.9)*
Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism — this is a useful mnemonic for WWI causes. But AP prompts require you to argue which cause was most significant and support it with evidence, not just list all four.

### Unit 8

**The US and USSR were not allies who mysteriously became enemies.** *(8.1)*
The US had deep anti-communist views throughout the 1920s–1930s; the wartime alliance was the exception forced by Hitler, not the normal state of relations. The Cold War hardened gradually from Yalta (1945) through Potsdam into the Truman Doctrine (1947) — not at a single moment.

**Roosevelt didn't "give away" Eastern Europe at Yalta.** *(8.1)*
Soviet troops already occupied most of Eastern Europe, and the US public was unlikely to support military confrontation with the USSR. Roosevelt had limited leverage — Stalin held the facts on the ground. Understand the geopolitical reality before making a moral judgment about the conference.

**Don't reduce Stalin to simply "evil dictator."** *(8.1)*
His demand for an Eastern European buffer zone had genuine strategic logic — the USSR had been invaded from the west twice in living memory (WWI and WWII). Understanding his perspective explains *why* the Cold War happened, not just that it happened.

**The Cold War was ideological, not just strategic.** *(8.2)*
Both sides genuinely believed their system was superior and worth spreading globally. Don't reduce it to a simple power struggle — the ideological dimension explains why proxy wars in places like Vietnam and Angola mattered to both superpowers.

**MAD required both sides to believe the other would actually retaliate.** *(8.2)*
Mutually Assured Destruction is a strategic doctrine, not just "they'd both die." The logic only works if both sides maintain credible second-strike capability and believe the other will use it. This is why arms races and nuclear submarines mattered — they preserved credibility.

**The Berlin Wall was built to keep East Germans in, not Westerners out.** *(8.3)*
The mass emigration — 2.5 million between 1945–1961 — was both an embarrassment to communist ideology and an economic drain on East Germany. The Wall's purpose was internal control, not external defense.

**Proxy wars and decolonization wars often overlapped but are not the same.** *(8.3)*
Vietnam's war against France was a decolonization struggle; the American phase was a Cold War proxy conflict. Local causes (land reform, anti-colonialism) drove the former; superpower rivalry drove the latter. The AP may ask you to analyze which framing better explains a specific document.

**Key Cold War dates to memorize.** *(8.3)*
Korean War ends: **1953** (not 1950). Vietnam War US withdrawal: **1973**; South Vietnam falls: **1975**. Cuban Missile Crisis: **1962** (not 1961 — that's the Bay of Pigs). These distinctions matter on MCQs.

**Great Leap Forward (1958–62) ≠ Cultural Revolution (1966–76).** *(8.4)*
The GLF targeted the economy and peasant agriculture (result: famine, ~30–45 million deaths). The Cultural Revolution targeted intellectuals, party officials, and traditional culture (result: purges, educational collapse). Different campaigns, different targets, different death tolls.

**Communist states were rivals, not a unified bloc.** *(8.4)*
From 1961 onward, the Sino-Soviet split produced border skirmishes and competition for global influence. Do not assume communist states acted together — the split is a key complexity the AP expects you to know.

**Decolonization was won, not granted.** *(8.5)*
Colonial powers resisted in most cases: the French fought bloody wars in Indochina and Algeria; Portugal held on until 1975. Independence was the result of sustained resistance, not metropolitan generosity. Don't write that colonial powers "gave" independence.

**Independence did not automatically mean democracy.** *(8.5)*
Most new states became one-party states or military dictatorships within a few years of independence. The AP asks why — emphasize ethnic division engineered by colonial borders, Cold War pressure to choose sides, and lack of democratic institutions under colonial rule.

**Decolonization had major continuities.** *(8.5)*
Colonial borders remained. Economic structures remained extractive. Authoritarian governance continued under new flags. Don't write about decolonization as a clean break — the AP rewards students who identify what persisted alongside what changed.

**SDI was never built.** *(8.8)*
Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative was a proposal, not a functioning system. Its significance was psychological and economic: it forced Soviet planners to consider how to respond to a threat they lacked the resources to counter, contributing to pressure for internal reform.

**The Prague Spring failed — and that's why it matters.** *(8.8)*
Soviet tanks crushed it. Its significance is that the repression further delegitimized communist governments in the eyes of Eastern Europeans. A *failed* uprising can still be historically significant — this is a key AP analytical skill.

**The Cold War amplified authoritarianism in the Third World, but colonialism created it.** *(8.9)*
Don't conflate Third World revolutions as Soviet-driven — many independence movements were primarily nationalist. And don't argue the Cold War caused authoritarianism in developing nations — colonialism had already embedded authoritarian structures. The Cold War extended and amplified them.

### Unit 9

**The Green Revolution was not purely positive.** *(9.1)*
It increased food production but deepened inequality between large and small farmers, caused environmental damage from pesticide use, and created dependence on expensive inputs. Borlaug himself warned in 1970 that population growth could overwhelm the gains. The AP rewards nuance.

**HIV ≠ AIDS.** *(9.2)*
HIV is the virus; AIDS is the advanced stage of the disease. A person can be HIV-positive for years without having AIDS, especially with antiretroviral treatment. The AP exam expects this precision.

**Population growth is a cause of environmental stress; global warming is an effect.** *(9.3)*
Population growth → deforestation and water scarcity. Greenhouse gas emissions from industrialization → global warming. Trace causal chains precisely — don't mix causes and effects.

**Kyoto Protocol ≠ Paris Agreement.** *(9.3)*
Kyoto (1997): binding targets but excluded developing nations and was never ratified by the US. Paris (2015): broader participation but less binding. Both showed international climate cooperation is possible but politically fragile.

**Free-market ideology did not automatically produce economic development.** *(9.4)*
Chile reduced poverty only after combining free trade WITH government social programs. Pure neoliberalism had brutal costs — Pinochet's Chile is an example of economic liberalization imposed by military dictatorship with extreme human rights abuses.

**Legal rights ≠ actual political participation.** *(9.5)*
Women winning voting rights did not mean universal equal political participation. In Pakistan, women gained voting rights in 1947 but cast only 10% of votes as late as 2018. The AP tests the gap between legal rights and lived reality.

**Tiananmen 1989 ≠ May Fourth 1919 — but they're connected.** *(9.5)*
Both were student-led protests in China demanding political reform; both were suppressed. Key difference: 1919 was nationalist/anti-imperialist; 1989 was pro-democracy within a communist state. The AP may ask you to compare them — know the distinction.

**Globalization ≠ Americanization.** *(9.6)*
Cultural flows are multidirectional. Bollywood, K-pop, anime, and reggae are examples of non-Western culture achieving global reach. "Americanization" describes one current of globalization, not the whole thing.

**Social media is both a tool of liberation AND a tool of control.** *(9.6)*
Always give both sides. The Arab Spring demonstrated mobilization potential; Chinese internet censorship demonstrates state control. These are complementary, not contradictory, examples of the same technology.

**Muhammad Yunus's microcredit and Rana Plaza are different angles on globalization.** *(9.7)*
Microcredit is a *reform* to globalization's inequality; Rana Plaza is an *indictment* of its human costs. Both are exam-relevant but represent distinct arguments about what globalization does to the poor.

**Anti-globalization resistance was not monolithic.** *(9.7)*
Labor unions wanted fair wages within global trade. Nationalists wanted out of trade deals entirely. Environmentalists wanted sustainable production rules. These are different — and sometimes incompatible — positions. Don't flatten them into a single "resistance" category.

**The UN has a mixed peacekeeping record — do not overstate its effectiveness.** *(9.8)*
Successes: Namibia, Cambodia, El Salvador. Notable failures: Rwanda (1994, 800,000 killed despite peacekeeping forces present) and Somalia. Rwanda is the strongest evidence for arguments about the limitations of international institutions — connect it to Security Council veto deadlock and the principle of national sovereignty.

**This era was not purely progressive.** *(9.9)*
The AP rewards arguing that changes were uneven, benefiting developed nations more than developing ones, and that new technologies produced both positive outcomes (vaccines) and negative consequences (environmental damage, inequality). The developed/developing divide cuts across every domain and earns complexity points.

**Democracy did not simply "win" after the Cold War.** *(9.9)*
The AP explicitly rewards complexity here. Post-Cold War optimism about liberal democracy gave way to new challenges: authoritarian resurgence in Russia and China, democratic backsliding in Hungary and Turkey, and rising populism in Western democracies themselves. Don't write a triumphalist narrative about the post-1991 era.

## Quick Reference: The Most-Tested Traps

These appear on the AP exam most frequently based on how often they show up across practice materials:

1. **Gunpowder Empires = Ottoman + Safavid + Mughal only** (not Russia, not China)
2. **Disease > weapons for indigenous demographic collapse in the Americas**
3. **Fourteen Points ≠ Treaty of Versailles** (Wilson proposed; Clemenceau imposed)
4. **Decolonization was won, not granted** — colonial powers resisted
5. **Communist states were rivals** (Sino-Soviet split, 1961+)
6. **Zheng He ≠ Columbus** (tribute mission vs. commercial exploration)
7. **Mercantilism ≠ capitalism** (state-directed vs. market-directed)
8. **The Berlin Conference formalized ongoing colonization, not began it**
9. **Social Darwinism misapplied Darwin** (human races ≠ biological species)
10. **Legal rights ≠ lived reality** (women's suffrage, decolonization, civil rights)
