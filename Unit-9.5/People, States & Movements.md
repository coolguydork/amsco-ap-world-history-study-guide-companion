# People, States & Movements

**AP Themes:** GOV, CDI, SIO | **AMSCO pp. 667–678**

---

## Key Figures

**Nelson Mandela (1918–2013)**
Leader of the African National Congress (ANC) and the global symbol of anti-apartheid resistance. Mandela was imprisoned in 1964 for life for agitating against apartheid; his 27-year imprisonment inspired worldwide divestment campaigns, boycotts, and UN sanctions against South Africa. Released in 1990 by F. W. de Klerk, Mandela negotiated the transition to multiracial democracy and was elected South Africa's first Black president in 1994. His Nobel Peace Prize (1993, shared with de Klerk) recognized both men's roles in the peaceful transition. On the AP exam, Mandela is the go-to example of sustained, eventually successful non-violent resistance to state oppression.

**Eleanor Roosevelt (1884–1962)**
U.S. First Lady and diplomat who chaired the UN committee that drafted the Universal Declaration of [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|Human Rights]] (1948). Her leadership made the UDHR a document that balanced Western civil-political rights with the economic and social rights demanded by postcolonial nations. AP significance: exemplifies how individuals can shape international institutions.

**Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986)**
French feminist philosopher whose landmark work *The Second Sex* (1949) argued that womanhood was a socially constructed role imposed on women, not a biological destiny. Her work influenced the second-wave feminist movements of the 1960s–70s across Europe and North America.

**Desmond Tutu (1931–2021)**
Anglican Archbishop of Cape Town and Nobel Peace Prize laureate (1984) who led religious opposition to apartheid inside South Africa. His moral authority helped sustain the anti-apartheid movement during its darkest years and gave international legitimacy to the cause. After apartheid ended, he chaired the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. Quote: "I am not interested in picking up crumbs of compassion — I want the full menu of rights."

**F. W. de Klerk (1936–2021)**
South Africa's last apartheid-era president. Recognizing that apartheid was economically and morally unsustainable, de Klerk released Mandela (1990), legalized the ANC, and negotiated the transition to democracy. Though he came from the white establishment, de Klerk's willingness to reform from within was essential to preventing civil war. He shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Mandela in 1993.

**Malala Yousafzai (b. 1997)**
Pakistani activist for girls' education who survived a Taliban assassination attempt in 2012 and became the youngest Nobel Peace Prize laureate (2014). Her case illustrates that restrictions on women's education persist as a global human rights issue and that individual voices can reshape international discourse.

**Léopold Sédar Senghor (1906–2001)**
Senegalese poet, philosopher, and co-founder of the Negritude movement. His poetry celebrated African cultural identity against French assimilation policies. He later served as the first president of independent Senegal (1960–1980), representing both intellectual and political dimensions of African decolonization.

> [!tip]
> **Senghor + Negritude** is an intersection of CDI (Cultural & Demographic Interaction) and GOV (Governance/Independence) — a strong choice for SAQs asking for examples of cultural responses to imperialism.

**W. E. B. Du Bois (1868–1963)**
African American scholar and activist who pioneered concepts of the "color line" (global racial hierarchy) and [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]]. Co-founded the NAACP; organized Pan-African Congresses in the 1910s–20s. His intellectual framework influenced both the U.S. civil rights movement and African independence leaders like Kwame Nkrumah.

**Wangari Maathai (1940–2011)**
Kenyan environmental and political activist who founded the [[Concept Notes/Green Belt Movement\|Green Belt Movement]] in 1977 to combat deforestation in postcolonial Kenya. The movement mobilized rural women to plant trees and reclaim environmental governance. She won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004 — the first African woman — connecting environmental rights to human rights.

**Pope Francis (b. 1936)**
First Latin American pope (elected 2013). Reversed the Vatican's historical opposition to liberation theology, validating the movement's call for the Church to serve the poor and challenge oppressive political structures. His election symbolized the shift in global Catholic weight toward the Global South.

## Key States

**South Africa (apartheid era, 1948–1994)**
The world's most extreme example of institutionalized racial segregation after WWII. The white National Party minority (15% of population) controlled the government, economy, and military. Apartheid used pass laws, residential segregation, and language suppression to maintain racial hierarchy. International pressure — UN expulsion (1974), economic sanctions, sports boycotts, divestment campaigns — combined with domestic ANC resistance to force negotiations in the late 1980s.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves South Africa for comparison essays: compare anti-apartheid tactics with Indian independence, compare the TRC with the Nuremberg Trials, or compare South Africa's peaceful transition with more violent decolonizations.

**China (CCP, post-1978)**
After Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms opened China to market forces, the country became an economic powerhouse — but the Chinese Communist Party refused political liberalization. It censored media, suppressed opposition parties, and required NGOs to register with the state. The Tiananmen Square massacre (June 4, 1989) demonstrated the CCP's determination to maintain political control regardless of economic opening.

**India (post-independence)**
India's 1949 Constitution outlawed caste discrimination and established the caste reservation system — significant legal reforms driven by the reformist legacy of B. R. Ambedkar (a Dalit leader who helped draft the constitution). Despite constitutional protections, social discrimination against Dalits persisted well into the 21st century, illustrating the gap between formal legal reform and social reality.

## Key Movements

**Anti-Apartheid Movement (global, 1960s–1994)**
A transnational coalition including the ANC inside South Africa, international divestment campaigns (universities and corporations withdrawing investments), sports boycotts, and state-level sanctions. Key moments: Nelson Mandela's imprisonment (1964), South Africa's UN expulsion (1974), global Free Mandela concerts (1980s), and the transition to democracy (1990–1994). A model of how international pressure combined with domestic resistance can end a repressive regime.

**Global Feminism (1900–present)**
A series of organized movements demanding equal political, economic, and social rights for women. Key milestones: suffrage movements (1893–2015), CEDAW (1979), and the Women's March (2017). The movement was global in scope but uneven in outcomes — legal rights often preceded actual social equality, especially in non-Western contexts.

**[[Concept Notes/Liberation Theology\|Liberation Theology]] Movement (Latin America, 1950s–present)**
A Catholic reform movement that combined Christian ethics with socialist economics, arguing the Church had a moral obligation to help the poor achieve social justice. Spread through Latin America; persecuted by military dictatorships in Brazil, Argentina, El Salvador, and Nicaragua. Notable figures: Oscar Romero (martyred in El Salvador), Hugo Chavez in Venezuela.

**Negritude Movement (Francophone Africa/Caribbean, 1930s–60s)**
Intellectual and cultural movement among French-speaking African and Caribbean writers that celebrated African identity and rejected assimilation. Directly challenged colonial ideology that African cultures were inferior. Influenced the broader decolonization movement and Black Power/Black pride movements of the 1960s.

**Pro-Democracy Movement in China (1989)**
Student- and worker-led movement centered in Beijing's Tiananmen Square that demanded freedom of the press, political reform, and dialogue with the government. Inspired by China's May Fourth Movement (1919 — Topic 7.5). After seven weeks of protests in 400+ cities, the CCP declared martial law and violently suppressed the movement on June 4, 1989.

> [!warning]
> Do not confuse the Tiananmen Square protests (1989) with the Tiananmen Gate, which is a common confusion point. The 1989 massacre took place in the plaza (square) in front of Tiananmen Gate — the same symbolic location where Mao proclaimed the People's Republic in 1949.
