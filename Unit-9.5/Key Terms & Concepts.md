# Key Terms & Concepts

**AP Themes:** GOV, CDI, SIO | **AMSCO pp. 667–678**

---

**Nelson Mandela**
South African anti-apartheid leader who spent 27 years in prison (1964–1990); became the first democratically elected president of post-apartheid South Africa (1994–1999). Worldwide symbol of peaceful resistance to racial oppression. South Africa's democratic transition was a landmark event of the late Cold War era.

**Negritude**
A cultural and literary movement originating in French-speaking Africa and the Caribbean in the 1930s–50s that celebrated African heritage, identity, and pride in "blackness" as a direct challenge to French colonial assimilation policies. Key figures include Léopold Sédar Senghor (Senegal) and Aimé Césaire (Martinique). On the AP exam, it represents intellectual resistance to colonialism and connects to broader decolonization and [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]] movements.

**Leopold Senghor**
Léopold Sédar Senghor (1906–2001), Senegalese poet, philosopher, and politician who co-founded the Negritude movement and became the first president of independent Senegal (1960). He advocated for African cultural pride while also seeking cooperation with France. Exemplifies intellectual anti-colonial resistance and the transition from colony to independent nation-state.

**Pope Francis**
Jorge Mario Bergoglio (b. 1936), elected pope in 2013 as the first Latin American and Jesuit pope. He reversed the Vatican's earlier opposition to liberation theology and emphasized the Church's responsibility to address poverty and social inequality. Represents the Catholic Church's evolving response to calls for social reform in the Global South.

**[[Concept Notes/Liberation Theology\|Liberation Theology]]**
A Catholic theological movement originating in Latin America in the 1960s–70s that interpreted scripture through the lens of poverty and social justice, calling the Church to take an active role in ending oppression. Priests like Oscar Romero became martyrs defending the poor against authoritarian regimes. Connects religion, political resistance, and calls for reform in post-colonial societies.

**W. E. B. DuBois**
W. E. B. Du Bois (1868–1963), African American scholar, civil rights leader, and co-founder of the NAACP who pioneered the concept of the "color line" and [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]]. He advocated for full political rights for Black Americans and for solidarity among peoples of African descent worldwide. Connects U.S. civil rights reform with global decolonization and [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|human rights]] movements.

**Desmond Tutu**
Anglican Archbishop of Cape Town (1986–1996) and Nobel Peace Prize laureate (1984) who became the most prominent religious voice against apartheid in South Africa. He chaired the Truth and Reconciliation Commission after apartheid ended. His quote "I am not interested in picking up crumbs of compassion — I want the full menu of rights" is key AP primary source evidence.

**F. W. de Klerk**
South Africa's last apartheid-era president (1989–1994) who negotiated the end of apartheid by releasing Nelson Mandela (1990), legalizing the ANC, and overseeing the transition to multiracial democracy. He shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Mandela in 1993. Exemplifies peaceful political transition and reform from within a repressive state.

**Wangari Maathai**
Kenyan environmental and political activist (1940–2011) who founded the [[Concept Notes/Green Belt Movement\|Green Belt Movement]] in 1977, mobilizing women to plant trees to combat deforestation and soil erosion caused by colonial-era land misuse. She won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004 — the first African woman to do so. Connects environmental activism, postcolonial reform, women's empowerment, and the Global South.

**Earth Day**
Annual observance on April 22, launched in the United States in 1970 in response to industrial pollution, that galvanized the modern environmental movement. Now observed in about 174 countries. Illustrates growing global environmental awareness and citizen activism as a response to industrialization's ecological consequences.

**Greenpeace**
International non-governmental environmental organization founded in 1971 that grew to operate in over 55 countries, using direct action (confronting whaling ships, blockading nuclear test sites) alongside lobbying and public education. Exemplifies transnational NGO activism and grassroots responses to environmental degradation in the late 20th century.

**Green Belt Movement**
An environmental and social movement founded in Kenya by Wangari Maathai (1977) that organized communities — especially women — to plant trees, conserve soil, and protect wilderness from colonial-era deforestation. It became a model for linking environmental activism with women's empowerment and grassroots development in Africa.

**[[Concept Notes/Universal Declaration of Human Rights\|Universal Declaration of Human Rights]]**
UN document adopted in December 1948, drafted under Eleanor Roosevelt's leadership, articulating fundamental rights for all humans without distinction of race, sex, religion, or political opinion. Foundation of modern international [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|human rights]] law; used by activists worldwide to challenge discriminatory governments.

**United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund (UNICEF)**
UN agency established in 1946 to provide humanitarian and developmental aid to children worldwide, including food, healthcare, education, and emergency relief. Represents the UN's role as an international institution addressing humanitarian reform and protecting vulnerable populations across borders.

**[[Concept Notes/International Court of Justice\|International Court of Justice]]**
The principal judicial organ of the [[Concept Notes/United Nations\|United Nations]], established in 1945 at The Hague, Netherlands, that settles legal disputes between states. Also called the World Court; has 15 judges, each from a different country. Exemplifies the post-WWII international institutional framework designed to resolve conflicts peacefully.

**refugees**
People who have fled their home countries due to war, persecution, or disaster and cannot safely return. The UN, through UNHCR (UN High Commissioner for Refugees), coordinates international protection and aid. Connects 20th-century conflicts — WWII, decolonization wars, Cold War proxy conflicts — to humanitarian reform and the growth of international institutions.

**Civil Rights Act**
U.S. federal law (1964) that outlawed discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in employment and public accommodations. Key legislative achievement of the civil rights movement. U.S. example of reform in response to mass protest; connects to global [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|human rights]] movements.

**Voting Rights Act**
U.S. federal law (1965) that banned discriminatory voting practices such as literacy tests that had disenfranchised Black Americans, particularly in the South. Demonstrates how sustained protest movements can achieve legal reform; pairs with the Civil Rights Act as evidence for 20th-century calls for reform and state responses.

**apartheid**
South Africa's system of racial segregation and white minority rule (1948–1994), enforced through pass laws, residential restrictions, language policies, and political exclusion of the non-white 85% majority. Ended peacefully through international pressure, ANC resistance, and negotiation — culminating in Mandela's election in 1994.

**pass laws**
Laws enforced under South Africa's apartheid regime requiring Black South Africans to carry identification passbooks and restricting their movement between designated "homelands" and white areas. Violating pass laws led to arrest. A concrete mechanism of apartheid oppression illustrating how states use legal systems to enforce racial hierarchy.

**African National Congress (ANC)**
South Africa's primary anti-apartheid political organization, founded in 1912, that led the resistance movement against white minority rule. Nelson Mandela was imprisoned from 1964 to 1990; after the ANC was legalized in 1990, it won the first multiracial elections in 1994. Key organization for AP essays on calls for reform and state responses.

**pariah state**
A nation internationally isolated and condemned for policies that violate widely accepted norms. In the AP context, specifically South Africa under apartheid, which was expelled from the UN in 1974 and faced international sanctions in the 1980s. Illustrates how international pressure — isolation, boycotts, sanctions — can force domestic political reform.

**Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)**
South African body established in 1995, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, that allowed perpetrators of apartheid-era human rights abuses to confess in exchange for amnesty rather than criminal prosecution. Prioritized national healing over retribution. Contrasts with the Nuremberg Trials as an alternative model of post-conflict justice.

**dalits**
The lowest caste in India's traditional Hindu hierarchy, historically called "untouchables," who faced severe social discrimination. India's 1949 Constitution outlawed caste discrimination; the caste reservation system was established to improve access to education and government jobs. Connects to broader global human rights and reform movements.

**caste reservation system**
India's affirmative action policy reserving a percentage of government jobs, university seats, and legislative positions for Dalits and other historically marginalized groups. Established after independence to address centuries of caste discrimination. Compare with U.S. civil rights legislation as parallel examples of governments reforming discriminatory systems.

**Tiananmen Square**
Beijing plaza where, in June 1989, a massive student-led pro-democracy movement was violently suppressed by the Chinese military — resulting in hundreds to thousands of deaths. Illustrates the CCP's willingness to use force to maintain political control even as China liberalized economically; illustrates the gap between economic and political reform.
