# Calls for Reform & Responses

**AP Themes:** GOV, CDI, SIO | **AP Skills:** Causation, CCOT | **Period 4** (1900–present)
**Essential Question:** How have calls for gender equality, racial justice, and environmental protection shaped society since 1900?
**AMSCO:** pp. 667–678

In the age of global economics and communication, human rights were elevated to global discourse for the first time, challenging long-held assumptions about race, class, gender, and religion. People sought liberation from what Nelson Mandela called the "continuing bondage" of poverty and discrimination. Protest movements worldwide demanded that states honor the rights articulated in the [[Concept Notes/Universal Declaration of Human Rights\|Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] (1948) — and governments, under sustained pressure, often eventually complied.

## An Era of Rights

The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (1948) was the foundational document of the postwar rights era, asserting basic freedoms for all people regardless of "race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion." The UN built an institutional framework around it: UNICEF (1946) provided aid to vulnerable children; the [[Concept Notes/International Court of Justice\|International Court of Justice]] settled disputes between states; and UNHCR protected **refugees** fleeing war and famine.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Universal Declaration is a landmark document for the AP exam. Know its date (1948), its author body (UN), and its significance: it made human rights an international responsibility, not just a matter of domestic law. Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting committee.

### Global Feminism

Women's rights advanced dramatically but unevenly after 1900. Key milestones include: First International Women's Day (1911), CEDAW — the "international bill of rights for women" (1979), and the Fourth International Congress of Women in Beijing (1995), where Hillary Clinton declared "women's rights are human rights." Voting rights followed a global pattern: New Zealand led in 1893, while Kuwait and Saudi Arabia did not grant women's suffrage until 2005 and 2015 respectively.

> [!warning]
> Women winning the right to vote did NOT mean universal equal political participation. In Pakistan, women gained voting rights in 1947 but cast only 10% of votes as late as 2018. The AP tests the gap between legal rights and lived reality.

### Cultural and Religious Reform Movements

The **Negritude** movement in French West Africa celebrated African cultural pride and rejected colonial assimilation. Léopold Senghor's poetry argued for African uniqueness; W. E. B. Du Bois and Langston Hughes made parallel arguments from the American context. Together these movements seeded the "black pride" movements of the 1960s.

[[Concept Notes/Liberation Theology\|Liberation Theology]] combined Catholicism with socialist economics, spreading across Latin America in the 1950s–60s. It interpreted Jesus's teachings as a mandate to end political and economic oppression. Military dictators persecuted liberation theologians; in Nicaragua, the movement helped topple a dictator. When Pope Francis — the first Latin American pope — reversed the Vatican's opposition in 2013, the movement achieved institutional legitimacy.

## Steps toward Racial Equality

In the United States, **Civil Rights Act** (1964) and **Voting Rights Act** (1965) were the legislative achievements of the civil rights movement, outlawing racial discrimination and banning discriminatory voting practices respectively.

### Apartheid and South Africa

South Africa's **apartheid** system (1948–1994) enforced racial segregation through **pass laws**, residential restrictions, language policies (Afrikaans-only instruction for Black students), and prohibition of mixed marriages — all to preserve privilege for the white 15% minority. The **African National Congress (ANC)** led resistance, with Nelson Mandela as its most powerful symbol. Mandela was imprisoned in 1964; his 27-year imprisonment galvanized a global movement including economic sanctions, divestment campaigns, and South Africa's expulsion from the UN in 1974.

> [!exam-tip]
> South Africa's anti-apartheid struggle is one of the highest-frequency AP essay topics. Know: (1) ANC as the key organization, (2) Nelson Mandela as the key figure, (3) international pressure (sanctions, divestment, UN expulsion) as a key causal factor in ending apartheid, (4) the TRC as a distinctive model of post-conflict justice.

F. W. de Klerk became president in 1989 and recognized the need for change. He released Mandela in 1990, legalized the ANC, and negotiated the end of apartheid. South Africa held its first multiracial elections in 1994, which Mandela and the ANC won. The **Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC)**, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, offered amnesty in exchange for truthful testimony — prioritizing reconciliation over retribution, unlike the Nuremberg Trials that followed WWII.

> [!tip]
> **TRC vs. Nuremberg comparison** is a standard AP essay setup. Nuremberg = criminal punishment; TRC = amnesty for truth + national healing. Both were responses to systematic state atrocities, but the TRC aimed at keeping a fragile multiracial nation together.

### Caste System Reform in India

India's 1949 Constitution outlawed discrimination against **dalits** (untouchables). The **caste reservation system** guaranteed a percentage of government jobs and university seats to historically marginalized castes — India's parallel to affirmative action. Despite these legal reforms, caste discrimination persisted into the 21st century.

## Human Rights Repression in China

China's economic liberalization under Deng Xiaoping was NOT matched by political reform. The CCP censored media, controlled education, and required NGOs to register with the state. In spring 1989, students and workers staged massive pro-democracy protests in **Tiananmen Square**. On June 4, the military attacked unarmed protesters; estimates range from hundreds to thousands killed. China still censors all mention of the event. This illustrates a pattern common across the AP exam: economic opening without political liberalization.

> [!warning]
> The AP may ask you to compare Tiananmen (1989) with the May Fourth Movement (1919 — Topic 7.5). Both were student-led protests in China demanding political reform; both were suppressed. The key difference: 1919 was nationalist/anti-imperialist in character; 1989 was explicitly pro-democracy and anti-authoritarian.

## Steps toward Environmental and Economic Reform

Environmental activism grew as a response to industrial pollution and deforestation. Key movements: **Earth Day** (1970, now 174 countries), **Greenpeace** (1971, direct action against whaling and deforestation), and the **[[Concept Notes/Green Belt Movement\|Green Belt Movement]]** founded by Wangari Maathai in Kenya (1977), which linked tree-planting to women's empowerment and postcolonial land repair. Maathai won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004 — the first African woman to do so.

The **World Fair Trade Organization** (1989) was founded to counterbalance the WTO with principles prioritizing fair wages, no child labor, gender equity, and environmental respect.

> [!tip]
> **Wangari Maathai** is a high-value AP figure: she connects Africa + environment + women's rights + colonialism's legacy — four AP themes in one person. Her Nobel Prize also signals that environmental activism is recognized as a form of [[Concept Notes/Human Rights\|human rights]] work.
