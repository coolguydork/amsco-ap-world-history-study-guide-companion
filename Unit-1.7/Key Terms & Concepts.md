# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 1.7
**Topic 1.7 | Unit 1: The Global Tapestry | Period 1 (c. 1200–c. 1450)**
*AMSCO pp. 65–68*

## Four Types of State-Building

**Emergence of New States**
A process by which new political entities arise on territory formerly controlled by a collapsed or weakened empire. The Mamluk Empire and Seljuk Empire both emerged from former Abbasid territory; the Delhi Sultanate arose in territory formerly under Gupta influence. Understanding this type helps explain why so many new states after c. 1200 still resembled their predecessors in religion and administration. (AMSCO p. 68)

**Revival of Former Empires**
A state-building process in which new leadership rebuilds or continues a previous empire, retaining its core structures while adding innovations. The Song Dynasty revived and transformed Han-era governance through a robust civil-service system; the Mali Empire built on the Ghana Kingdom's trade networks; the Holy Roman Empire invoked Roman imperial legitimacy. (AMSCO p. 68)

**Synthesis of Different Traditions**
A state adapts foreign political, religious, or cultural models to local conditions. Japan synthesized Chinese Confucian court culture with indigenous Japanese feudal structures. The Delhi Sultanate blended Islamic political authority with Hindu administrative practices. [[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]] itself was a synthesis of Confucian ethics with Buddhist and Daoist philosophical elements. (AMSCO p. 68)

**Expansion in Scope**
An existing state extends its power through conquest, trade control, or other means. The Aztec Empire created a tributary network across Mesoamerica; the Inca Empire expanded into South America using the mit'a labor system; East African and Southeast Asian city-states extended influence through Indian Ocean commerce. (AMSCO p. 68)

## Governance

**Centralized State**
A state in which political authority is concentrated in a central government with a bureaucracy that enforces laws across a large territory. The Song Dynasty is the strongest example in this period — its civil-service examination system produced trained administrators across China. Contrast with decentralized feudal Japan and medieval Europe. (AMSCO p. 66)

**Decentralized State**
A state in which power is distributed among local lords or regional rulers rather than held by a central authority. Japan in this period became increasingly feudal and decentralized — the shoguns and daimyo held real power while the emperor's authority was ceremonial. Medieval Europe before c. 1200 shared this structure. See [[Concept Notes/Feudalism|Feudalism]]. (AMSCO p. 66)

**Civil Service Bureaucracy**
A system of government administration staffed by trained officials, typically selected through examination on classical texts. The Song Dynasty operated the most extensive civil-service system in the world, using Confucian examinations to select officials. This allowed the Song to administer a large territory effectively and set a model that later Chinese dynasties followed. (AMSCO p. 66)

**Tributary Empire**
An empire that demands payment of tribute — goods, labor, or people — from conquered or subordinate peoples rather than directly administering their territories. The Aztec Empire operated primarily as a tributary system. See [[Concept Notes/Tribute System|Tribute System]]. (AMSCO p. 66)

**Mit'a System**
A labor tribute system used by the Inca Empire in which subjects owed a set period of labor service to the state. The mit'a funded massive public works — roads, temples, storehouses — that integrated the Inca realm. This was distinct from the Aztec tributary model: the Inca extracted labor rather than primarily goods. (AMSCO p. 66)

## Religion and Legitimacy

**Political Legitimacy**
The recognized right of a ruler to govern. In this period, religion was the primary source of legitimacy across most of the world: Islam for the Mali Empire and Delhi Sultanate, Confucianism for the Song Dynasty, Hinduism and [[Concept Notes/Buddhism\|Buddhism]] for South and Southeast Asian rulers, Christianity for European monarchs. The key AP insight is that this cross-regional pattern of religion-as-legitimacy is itself a **similarity** for comparison essays. (AMSCO pp. 65–66)

**Diffusion of Religion**
The spread of belief systems through trade, migration, conquest, and missionary activity. Islam spread across Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia via trade routes and military conquest. Buddhism spread through East and Southeast Asia. Christianity spread through European expansion and Catholic missionary orders. Religious diffusion and state-building were closely linked — new rulers often adopted or promoted the religion of powerful trading partners or conquering armies. (AMSCO p. 67)

**Foot Binding**
A Chinese practice in which girls' feet were tightly bound to prevent growth, producing small feet considered a beauty ideal among elites. The practice became more widespread during the Song Dynasty and represents a decline in women's independence in China relative to earlier periods. The AP exam uses foot binding as evidence of patriarchal social structures in East Asia. (AMSCO p. 68)

## Trade and Technology

**Champa Rice**
A fast-ripening, drought-resistant variety of rice that originated in Vietnam (the Champa region) and spread to China during the Song Dynasty. Its adoption enabled double-cropping, fueled population growth, supported urban expansion, and increased manufacturing output — all of which strengthened the Song state. (AMSCO p. 67)

**[[Concept Notes/House of Wisdom\|House of Wisdom]] (Bayt al-Hikma)**
A major center of intellectual and scientific learning in Baghdad under the Abbasid Caliphate, where scholars translated Greek, Persian, and Indian texts and made advances in mathematics, medicine, and astronomy. Paper-making technology from China helped this institution produce and circulate knowledge, boosting Islamic intellectual culture and eventually transmitting knowledge to Europe. (AMSCO p. 67)

**Manorial System**
The economic system of medieval Europe in which lords controlled large estates (manors) worked by serfs who owed labor and goods in exchange for protection. The manorial system and serfdom limited the trade-based economic growth that could fuel state-building, making European state consolidation slower and more limited than in Asia during this period. (AMSCO p. 67)

**Serfdom**
A labor system in medieval Europe (and Russia) in which peasants were legally bound to the land and could not leave without their lord's permission. Serfs owed labor, goods, and fees to their lord. Serfdom limited labor mobility and economic growth, slowing European state consolidation relative to trade-rich Asian empires. (AMSCO p. 67)

## Nomadic Peoples

**[[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica\|Pax Mongolica]] ("Mongol Peace")**
The period of relative stability and security across Eurasia created by Mongol dominance in the 13th and 14th centuries. Although established through brutal conquest, the Pax Mongolica allowed Silk Road trade to expand dramatically, facilitated direct Europe–China contacts, and accelerated the transfer of technologies including paper, gunpowder, and the printing press. See [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]]. (AMSCO p. 67)

**Pastoral Nomads**
Peoples who move seasonally with their herds across steppe grasslands, without permanent agricultural settlements. The Mongols and Turkish peoples originated as pastoral nomads from Central Asia. Their military advantages — mobility, horses, composite bows — allowed them to conquer settled agricultural civilizations. By c. 1450, nomadic influence over trade and politics was diminishing as settled merchant networks matured. (AMSCO pp. 67–68)
