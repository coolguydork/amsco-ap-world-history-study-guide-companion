# Comparison in the Period c. 1200–c. 1450 — Overview
**Topic 1.7 | Unit 1: The Global Tapestry | Period 1 (c. 1200–c. 1450)**
*AMSCO pp. 65–68*

Topic 1.7 is the capstone of Unit 1. Its entire purpose is to train your AP **Comparison** skill. Rather than introducing new civilizations, it asks: *how did state-building processes across regions resemble one another, and where did they diverge?* Almost every AP exam LEQ or DBQ on this period will reward students who can answer that question with precision.

## The Big Picture

Between c. 1200 and c. 1450, states in core areas of civilization grew larger and more centralized while smaller states declined. The broad trend was toward greater centralization — but the **mechanism** varied by region. Three forces dominated state-building across the globe:

- **Military conquest** — especially the Mongols in Asia and Eastern Europe; Turkish groups in the Mediterranean and South Asia
- **Religion** — Islam in Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia; Confucianism in East Asia; Christianity in Europe; Hinduism and [[Concept Notes/Buddhism\|Buddhism]] in South and Southeast Asia
- **Trade** — sustained by the Indian Ocean, [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads\|Silk Roads]], and trans-Saharan networks

## Religion as a Tool of State Power

The single most important cross-regional similarity was the use of religion to legitimize authority. Rulers from West Africa to Japan used shared belief systems to unite diverse populations and justify political control.

In the Islamic world, shared belief and the common language of Arabic provided the basis for ruling authority from West Africa to Southeast Asia. The Mali Empire and [[Unit-1.3/South & Southeast Asia Overview|Delhi Sultanate]] both drew on Islam as a source of political legitimacy. In China, the Song Dynasty relied on [[Concept Notes/Neo-Confucianism|Neo-Confucianism]] to staff a powerful civil-service bureaucracy — the most extensive in the world at the time. Neo-Confucianism also spread to Korea and Japan, helping rulers in those East Asian regions consolidate power. In South and Southeast Asia, rulers used Hinduism and Buddhism similarly. (AMSCO p. 66)

Europe was a partial exception. The Catholic Church competed with emerging monarchies rather than consistently supporting them, especially as states in France and the Holy Roman Empire grew stronger after 1200. (AMSCO p. 66)

## Four Types of State-Building, c. 1200–c. 1450

AMSCO provides a taxonomy that is an excellent framework for any comparison essay:

| Process | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergence of New States** | States arise on land once controlled by another empire | Mamluk Empire, Seljuk Empire, Delhi Sultanate |
| **Revival of Former Empires** | New leadership rebuilds a previous empire with innovations | Song Dynasty, Mali Empire, Holy Roman Empire |
| **Synthesis of Different Traditions** | A state adapts foreign ideas to local conditions | Japan (Chinese + Japanese), Delhi Sultanate (Islamic + Hindu), Neo-Confucianism |
| **Expansion in Scope** | An existing state expands through conquest, trade, or other means | Aztecs, Incas, East African city-states, Southeast Asian city-states |

(AMSCO p. 68)

## State-Building Through Trade

Trade networks amplified state power across regions. Champa rice, originating in Vietnam, spread to China and fueled Song population growth and urban expansion. China's manufacturing output — porcelain, silk, steel, iron — was the largest in the world, and the Song Dynasty was the strongest Chinese state since the Han, roughly a millennium earlier.

Paper-making technology traveled from China across Eurasia, boosting literacy and intellectual culture in the Islamic world (the [[Concept Notes/House of Wisdom\|House of Wisdom]] in Baghdad) and in Europe. The [[Unit-1.5/Africa Overview|Mali Empire]] built upon the trans-Saharan gold and salt trade to become larger and more centrally administered than its predecessor, the Ghana Kingdom. In South and Southeast Asia, the Chola Kingdom and Vijayanagar Empire used Indian Ocean commerce to build strong states. (AMSCO pp. 66–67)

## The Impact of Nomadic Peoples

[[Concept Notes/Mongols|The Mongols]] were the defining military force of the era. From the Central Asian steppes, they built the largest land empire in world history. Paradoxically, their brutal conquests created the [[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica\|Pax Mongolica]] — a zone of political stability that dramatically expanded Silk Road trade and enabled direct Europe–China contact for the first time since the classical period.

Turkish peoples played a parallel role. The Seljuk and Ottoman Turks built empires across the eastern Mediterranean, Persia, and South Asia. Unlike the unified Mongol campaigns, different Turkish groups built separate empires. By the late 15th century, nomadic influence over politics and trade was diminishing as organized merchant networks replaced pastoral intermediaries. (AMSCO pp. 67–68)

## Social Organization: Patriarchy Across Cultures

Most cultures remained patriarchal, but details varied. Religion sometimes opened limited spaces for women: convent life in Christian Europe, and Jainist/Buddhist religious communities in South Asia, offered women opportunities for learning and leadership. In China, by contrast, women lost independence as foot binding became more widespread during the Song Dynasty. (AMSCO p. 68)

## Key Contrasts to Know for the Exam

- **China vs. Japan**: China became more centralized under the Song; Japan became more [[Concept Notes/Feudalism|decentralized and feudal]] — see [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|East Asia Overview]]
- **Islam vs. Christianity in state-building**: Islam provided consistent legitimacy across a vast area; Christianity in Europe was often a rival to state power rather than a support
- **Mongols vs. Turkish groups**: Unified conquest (Mongols) vs. separate empire-building (Turks)
- **Americas vs. Eurasia**: Aztec tributary empire and Inca mit'a system vs. Eurasian trade-based states; most of the Americas lacked centralized states — see [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|The Americas Overview]]
- **West Africa vs. Europe**: Mali built on trade wealth; Europe was held back by the manorial system and serfdom until c. 1450

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 1.7 is almost always tested through the **Comparison** historical reasoning skill. When writing an LEQ or DBQ, use the Four Types of State-Building table as your organizational framework. You must identify **both** similarities and differences across at least two regions with specific evidence.

> [!tip]
> Memorize one strong example of each of the four state-building types and you can answer almost any Unit 1 comparison prompt: Mamluk/Seljuk (new states), Song/Mali (revival), Japan/Delhi Sultanate (synthesis), Aztec/Inca (expansion in scope).
