# Unit 1.7: Comparative Timeline, c. 1200–1450

#apwh #unit-1 #period-1

This timeline places six regions side by side to reveal simultaneous developments across an era defined by Mongol disruption, commercial expansion, and the consolidation of major world religions. Reading across the rows, notice how events in one region often had ripple effects in others — the Mongol invasions that destroyed Baghdad in 1258 also redirected trade routes and pushed migrants into new regions, reshaping economies and cultures far from the battlefield.

---

| Date | Region | Event | Significance | Continuity vs. Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| c. 1200 | East Asia | Song Dynasty at commercial peak; paper money and urban growth flourish | Most advanced commercial economy in the world; Hangzhou may be the largest city on earth | **Continuity:** Song commercial patterns built on Tang foundations; **Change:** paper money and credit instruments represent new financial innovation |
| 1206 | South Asia | Delhi Sultanate established by Qutb ud-Din Aybak | First major Islamic sultanate in India; Islam enters South Asia via conquest | **Change:** shift from Hindu/Buddhist political dominance in northern India to Turkic-Islamic rule |
| 1206 | Central Asia | Temujin proclaimed Genghis Khan; Mongol conquests begin | Initiates the most disruptive and connective force of the 13th century | **Change:** begins the transformation of Eurasian political and trade geography |
| 1235 | Africa | Mali Empire founded under Sundiata Keita after Battle of Kirina | Replaces Ghana as dominant West African empire; controls trans-Saharan gold trade | **Continuity:** West African gold trade continues; **Change:** new ruling dynasty and expansion of territory |
| 1258 | Dar al-Islam | Mongols sack Baghdad; Abbasid Caliph executed | Ends the Abbasid Caliphate; Baghdad's intellectual and commercial supremacy shattered | **Change:** major rupture in Islamic political unity; caliphate never restored in this era |
| 1260 | Dar al-Islam / Africa | Mamluk Sultanate defeats Mongols at Battle of Ain Jalut (Egypt) | First major Mongol military defeat; Egypt becomes center of Sunni Islamic power | **Change:** shifts Islamic political center from Iraq to Egypt; Mamluks become protectors of the Hajj |
| c. 1270s | East Asia | Marco Polo travels to Yuan (Mongol) China | Documents the wealth and sophistication of East Asia for European audiences; Pax Mongolica enables travel | **Continuity:** Silk Road trade continues; **Change:** Mongol rule integrates China into a wider Eurasian exchange network |
| 1279 | East Asia | Mongols (Yuan Dynasty) complete conquest of Song China | China incorporated into Mongol Empire; commercial networks disrupted then partially restored | **Change:** end of Song Dynasty; foreign rule over China for first time; but commercial economy largely preserved |
| c. 1300 | Americas | Aztec (Mexica) people settle in the Valley of Mexico; Tenochtitlan founded c. 1325 | Beginning of the civilization that will dominate Mesoamerica by 1428 | **Change:** new state-building process in Mesoamerica following decline of earlier city-states |
| 1312–1337 | Africa | Mansa Musa's reign; hajj to Mecca in 1324–25 | Demonstrates Mali's wealth and Islamic connections to the wider world; spreads knowledge of West Africa | **Continuity:** trans-Saharan trade continues; **Change:** Mali's international prestige dramatically elevated |
| c. 1336 | South / SE Asia | Vijayanagara Empire founded in southern India | Last major Hindu empire resists Islamic expansion in the south | **Continuity:** Hindu political and cultural traditions persist in India despite Islamic north |
| c. 1350–1368 | East Asia | Black Death spreads across Mongol trade routes; weakens Yuan Dynasty; Ming Dynasty restores Chinese rule (1368) | Plague kills up to 1/3 of population in affected regions; accelerates end of Mongol power | **Change:** massive demographic disruption; Ming restoration of Chinese rule and Confucian order |
| c. 1400 | SE Asia | Spread of Islam in Southeast Asia accelerates via Malacca Sultanate | Malacca becomes the key node of Indian Ocean trade; Islam spreads to Malay Peninsula and Indonesian islands | **Change:** conversion of Southeast Asian coastal elites from Hinduism-Buddhism to Islam |
| c. 1438 | Americas | Pachacuti begins Inca expansion; Tawantinsuyu empire rapidly expands | Largest pre-Columbian empire in the Americas created through conquest and mit'a labor | **Change:** unprecedented scale of state power in South America |
| c. 1440s | Europe | Johannes Gutenberg develops movable-type printing press in Europe | Revolutionizes information spread; enables Reformation and Scientific Revolution | **Change:** transforms communication; but manuscript culture had dominated for centuries before |

---

## Key Patterns to Notice

- **The 1258 turning point:** The Mongol destruction of Baghdad is the single most disruptive event of the period for the Islamic world. Note how Islamic civilization **continued** despite this political shock — through the Mamluks, Mali, Delhi Sultanate, and Southeast Asian conversions.
- **Simultaneous state-building:** While the Mongols were disrupting Eurasian empires, the Americas were independently building their most complex states (Aztec, Inca) — a reminder that world history is not just "what happened to Eurasia."
- **The long 14th century:** The Black Death (1340s–1380s), the fall of the Yuan Dynasty (1368), and the decline of the Mongol world-system all cluster in the mid-to-late 14th century, marking the end of the Pax Mongolica and the transition to a new era.

> [!exam-tip]
> On the AP exam, CCOT questions will ask you to identify **what changed** and **what stayed the same** across this period. A reliable answer for continuity: long-distance trade networks persisted despite political disruptions. A reliable answer for change: the Mongol invasions fundamentally altered political structures across Eurasia.
