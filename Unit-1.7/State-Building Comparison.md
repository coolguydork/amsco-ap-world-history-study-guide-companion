# Topic 1.7 — Comparison in the Period c. 1200–c. 1450

> [!summary] Essential Question
> In what ways was the process of **state-building** in various parts of the world between c. 1200 and c. 1450 **similar and different**?

## The Big Comparative Pattern

Between c. 1200 and c. 1450, states in core areas of civilization grew **larger and more centralized** while smaller states declined. This was a global trend — but the **process** varied dramatically by region.

Three major engines drove state-building worldwide:
1. **Military force** of nomadic peoples (especially the Mongols and Turkic groups)
2. **Religion** as a source of legitimacy and unity (Islam, Confucianism, Hinduism, Christianity)
3. **Trade** as an engine of wealth and connection

---

## Four Types of State-Building, c. 1200–c. 1450

| Process | Description | Examples |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Emergence of New States** | States arise in a land previously lacking centralized power | Mamluk Empire; Seljuk Empire; Delhi Sultanate |
| **Revival of Former Empires** | New leadership continues or rebuilds a previous empire with innovations | Song Dynasty (based on Han); Mali (based on Ghana); Holy Roman Empire (based on Rome) |
| **Synthesis of Different Traditions** | A state adapts foreign ideas to local conditions | Japan (Chinese + Japanese); Delhi Sultanate (Islamic + Hindu); Neo-Confucianism |
| **Expansion in Scope** | An existing state expands via conquest, trade, or religious conversion | Aztecs in Mesoamerica; Incas in South America; East African city-states; Southeast Asian kingdoms |

> [!exam-tip]
> Memorize this table. Almost every AP comparison essay or LEQ about this period can be structured using these four types. Ask yourself: which type of state-building best describes each region? Then explain how religion, trade, or military power drove it.

## Region-by-Region State-Building Summary

### China (Song Dynasty) — Revival + Religion (Confucianism)
- Revived Tang-era institutions; expanded the civil service exam system
- Neo-Confucianism legitimized the bureaucracy and the emperor's authority
- The Song was the most commercially sophisticated state on earth
- Weakness: bureaucracy became too expensive; fell to the Mongols in 1279

### Islamic World (Abbasid → fragmented) — Emergence + Religion
- Abbasid Caliphate fragmented; replaced by Mamluk, Seljuk, Delhi Sultanate states (all Turkic in origin)
- Islam (shariah, Arabic, shared universities) provided cultural continuity across political fragmentation
- By 16th century, three great Islamic empires emerge: Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal

### South and Southeast Asia — Synthesis + Trade
- Northern India: Delhi Sultanate = Islamic governance over Hindu-Buddhist population → synthesis of traditions
- Southern India: Chola Dynasty and Vijayanagara Empire = stable Hindu kingdoms using Indian Ocean trade
- Southeast Asia: Srivijaya, Majapahit, Khmer = states built on trade control or agricultural irrigation

### Americas — Expansion + Religion
- Aztec: tributary empire built through military conquest; religion (theocracy + human sacrifice) legitimized power
- Inca: mit'a system supported expansion and integration; ancestor veneration and sun worship legitimized rulers
- Both expanded in scope by conquering neighbors — but through very different extraction systems

### Africa — Expansion + Trade
- Ghana → Mali: expansion in scope driven by gold-salt trade; Islam added to legitimacy (Mansa Musa)
- Zimbabwe: trade-based state built on Indian Ocean gold routes
- Ethiopia: religious state (Christian island) using architectural expression of power

### Europe — Emergence of Stronger States + Church
- Feudalism slowly gave way to more centralized monarchies in England and France
- The Roman Catholic Church provided Europe's only pan-continental institution — both supporting and competing with state-building
- Renaissance and printing press accelerated state-building after 1400

### Japan — Synthesis → Decentralization
- Japan adopted Chinese Neo-Confucianism but did NOT centralize
- Instead, Japan became *more* feudal and decentralized — the shogunate was weaker than China's centralized model

> [!exam-tip]
> Japan is the great exception to the "states grew more centralized" trend. AP essays sometimes specifically ask about an exception. Japan's shogunate period (post-1192) is your answer.

## The Role of Religion in State-Building

Across virtually every region, religion was a key tool of state legitimacy:

| Region | Religion | How It Supported the State |
|--------|----------|---------------------------|
| China | Neo-Confucianism | Bureaucracy legitimized through Confucian exams; filial piety = loyalty to ruler |
| Islamic world | Islam | Caliph/sultan as God's representative; shariah as law; Arabic as unifying language |
| South Asia | Hinduism + Islam (mixed) | Hindu kings claimed divine authority; Delhi Sultans used Islam for legitimacy among Muslims |
| Southeast Asia | Hinduism → Buddhism | Khmer rulers legitimized by divine Hindu-Buddhist identity; Angkor Wat as expression of royal-divine power |
| Americas | Polytheism | Aztec emperors as divine representatives; Inca rulers as sons of Inti (sun god); ancestor veneration motivating conquest |
| Africa (West) | Islam | Sundiata and Mansa Musa used Islamic credentials to build trade relationships and legitimacy |
| Africa (East) | Christianity | Ethiopian rulers expressed divine authority through rock churches; independent Christian identity |
| Europe | Christianity (Roman Catholic) | Church supported feudal hierarchy; Crusades as religiously motivated military campaigns; popes vs. kings tensions |

## The Role of Trade in State-Building

Trade drove state formation wherever geography and resources permitted:

- **Song China**: commercialization, Grand Canal, maritime technology → most urbanized economy in the world
- **Mali / Ghana**: gold-salt trans-Saharan trade → centralized states with iron-armed militaries
- **Zimbabwe**: Indian Ocean gold trade → stone-walled capital city
- **Srivijaya / Majapahit**: control of sea routes (Strait of Malacca) → tribute-based maritime empires
- **Abbasid Caliphate**: Baghdad at the center of Eurasian trade networks → wealth → golden age of learning
- **Europe**: Very limited internal trade; contact with Islamic world slowly began stimulating growth after 1000 CE

---

## The Impact of Nomadic Peoples

**[[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]]** were the most transformative force of this period:
- Built the largest land empire in history (Central Europe to Pacific Ocean)
- Initial conquest brought devastating destruction
- Once established, **Pax Mongolica** allowed trade to flow across Eurasia → new ideas and technologies spread rapidly
- Facilitated first direct contacts between Europe and China since classical period (Marco Polo)

**Turkic peoples** (Seljuks, Mamluks, Ottoman predecessors) also built major empires — but separately, not as a unified campaign. Different Turkish groups established the Seljuk Empire, Mamluk Sultanate, and the precursors of the Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal empires.

By 1450, the **age of nomadic dominance** was ending. Organized merchant companies and gunpowder weapons were beginning to shift power from mobile nomads to settled, well-armed states.

## Patriarchy and Gender: A Cross-Regional Pattern

Social organization in most cultures remained patriarchal — but with important variations:

| Region | Status of Women |
|--------|----------------|
| China | Patriarchal; foot binding intensified, restricting mobility |
| Islamic world | Legally better off than in China or Europe (inheritance, divorce); urbanization brought new restrictions (veil, harem) |
| Vietnam | More independent than Chinese women; resisted foot binding |
| South Asia | Caste limited mobility; Islam did not dramatically change status |
| Southeast Asia | More independence than in South Asia; maintained even after Islam arrived |
| Japan | Limited rights; samurai-class women had some autonomy |
| Africa (Sub-Saharan) | Some matrilineal societies (Mississippian); women's roles in agriculture and griottes |
| Europe | Very limited rights; bourgeoisie growth and convent life offered some exceptions |
| Americas | Aztec women's weaving = economic importance; Mississippian matrilineal |

> [!exam-tip]
> A comparison of women's status across two societies is a classic AP exam prompt. Your strongest contrasts: China (foot binding, declining status) vs. Islamic world (legal rights, but urbanization restricts freedom) vs. Southeast Asia (most independence). Vietnam is a good surprise answer.
