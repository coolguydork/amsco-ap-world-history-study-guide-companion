# People, States & Movements — Topic 1.7
**Topic 1.7 | Unit 1: The Global Tapestry | Period 1 (c. 1200–c. 1450)**
*AMSCO pp. 65–68*

Topic 1.7 draws together states and figures from all six Unit 1 regions for comparative analysis. Use this note alongside the region-specific notes in Units 1.1–1.6.

## States and Empires by State-Building Type

### Emergence of New States

**Mamluk Empire** (Egypt and the Levant, c. 1250–1517)
A sultanate founded by military slaves (mamluks) of Turkic origin who took power from the Abbasid Caliphate's successor states in Egypt. The Mamluks famously stopped the Mongol advance into Africa and the Middle East at the Battle of Ain Jalut (1260). Their state emerged from former Abbasid territory and is the clearest example of the "Emergence of New States" type. (AMSCO p. 68)

**Seljuk Empire** (Persia and Anatolia, c. 1037–1194)
A Turkic empire that arose in former Abbasid territory in Persia and extended into Anatolia (modern Turkey). The Seljuks converted to Sunni Islam and became defenders of the Abbasid Caliphate in name while exercising real power. Their expansion into Anatolia triggered the First Crusade. They represent Turkish state-building as a parallel to Mongol expansion. (AMSCO p. 68)

**Delhi Sultanate** (northern India, 1206–1526)
An Islamic sultanate established by Turkic invaders in former territory of the Gupta Empire's successors. The Delhi Sultanate exemplifies *both* "Emergence of New States" (built on territory formerly controlled by others) *and* "Synthesis of Different Traditions" (blended Islamic political authority with Hindu administrative structures and local customs). See [[Unit-1.3/South & Southeast Asia Overview|South & Southeast Asia Overview]]. (AMSCO pp. 24, 65)

### Revival of Former Empires

**Song Dynasty** (China, 960–1279)
Revived and transformed the Han Dynasty's model of centralized governance with an extensive civil-service examination system. The Song was the most technologically and economically advanced state in the world during this period, producing the largest manufacturing output globally. It represents "Revival of Former Empires" because it consciously modeled itself on Han-era governance while innovating significantly. See [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|East Asia Overview]]. (AMSCO pp. 3, 65)

**Mali Empire** (West Africa, c. 1235–1600)
Built on the trade networks and territory of the earlier Ghana Kingdom. Under rulers like Sundiata and especially Mansa Musa, Mali controlled the trans-Saharan gold and salt trade and became larger and more centrally administered than Ghana. Its rulers used Islam to legitimize authority and connect Mali to the broader Islamic world. See [[Unit-1.5/Africa Overview|Africa Overview]]. (AMSCO pp. 65, 106)

**Holy Roman Empire** (central Europe, 962–1806)
A loose confederation of German-speaking kingdoms and principalities that claimed the legacy of the Roman Empire. Despite its name, the Holy Roman Empire was neither wholly holy, Roman, nor an empire in the centralized sense — it was highly decentralized, with emperors competing for power against powerful nobles and the Catholic Church. It exemplifies "Revival of Former Empires" through its deliberate invocation of Roman legitimacy. See [[Unit-1.6/Medieval Europe Overview|Medieval Europe Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 68)

### Synthesis of Different Traditions

**Japan** (feudal period, c. 1185–1600)
Unlike most states in this period, Japan became *more* decentralized and feudal rather than more centralized. The Kamakura Shogunate (1185–1333) established a feudal system in which the shogun (military dictator) held real power while the emperor remained a figurehead. Japan synthesized Chinese Confucian court culture and writing with indigenous Japanese political and social structures — the clearest example of the "Synthesis" type. See [[Unit-1.1/East Asia Overview|East Asia Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 66)

### Expansion in Scope

**Aztec Empire (Triple Alliance)** (Mesoamerica, c. 1428–1521)
A tributary empire centered on Tenochtitlan in central Mexico. The Aztecs demanded tribute — goods, labor, and sacrificial victims — from conquered peoples rather than integrating them administratively. The empire relied on military strength and the threat of force to maintain its tributaries. See [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|The Americas Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 66)

**Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu)** (South America, c. 1438–1533)
The largest empire in pre-Columbian America, stretching along the Andes from present-day Ecuador to Chile. The Inca used the mit'a labor system to build an extensive road network, storehouses, and administrative infrastructure. Unlike the Aztecs' tributary model, Inca state power was more directly administrative. See [[Unit-1.4/The Americas Overview|The Americas Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 66)

## Other Major States

**Byzantine Empire** (Eastern Mediterranean, 330–1453)
The eastern continuation of the Roman Empire, centered on Constantinople. During this period the Byzantines were in decline, losing territory to the Seljuk Turks and Mongols. The Byzantine Empire served as a conduit for classical Greek learning to reach the Islamic world and, eventually, Renaissance Europe. Its eventual fall to the Ottoman Turks in 1453 marks the symbolic end of the medieval period.

**Vijayanagar Empire and Chola Kingdom** (South India)
Both South Indian states used Indian Ocean trade to build strong maritime-connected polities. They used Hinduism to legitimize authority, in contrast to the Islamic Delhi Sultanate in the north. The coexistence of these states illustrates the Synthesis type and Hindu–Islamic tension on the subcontinent. See [[Unit-1.3/South & Southeast Asia Overview|South & Southeast Asia Overview]]. (AMSCO p. 65)

**Ottoman Turks** (Anatolia and southeastern Europe, c. 1299–1922)
A Turkish group that built an empire distinct from the Seljuks. The Ottomans would eventually conquer Constantinople in 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire, and expand into southeastern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa — making them a bridge between the periods covered in Units 1 and 2. (AMSCO p. 68)

## Key Individuals

**Ibn Sina (Avicenna)** (980–1037)
Persian philosopher and physician whose works — including the *Canon of Medicine* — synthesized Greek, Persian, and Islamic medical and philosophical knowledge. Quoted at the opening of Topic 1.7: *"The world is divided into men who have wit and no religion and men who have religion and no wit."* His career exemplifies the Islamic world's role as a center of intellectual synthesis and cross-cultural exchange. (AMSCO p. 65)

**Mansa Musa** (r. c. 1312–1337)
The most famous ruler of the Mali Empire, celebrated for his lavish pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–1325 during which he distributed so much gold in Cairo and along the route that he reportedly caused inflation across North Africa and the Middle East. Mansa Musa's pilgrimage demonstrated Mali's enormous wealth, its Islamic identity, and its connections to the broader Islamic world. He is a primary AP exam figure for Unit 1 Africa. (AMSCO pp. 72, 106)

**Ibn Battuta** (1304–c. 1368/1369)
A Moroccan scholar and traveler who journeyed across the Islamic world, reaching as far as Mali, India, China, and Southeast Asia. His travel account, the *Rihla*, is one of the most important primary sources for this period. His description of Mali emphasizes justice, peace, and Islamic piety — a useful DBQ source for arguments about Mali's achievements. (AMSCO pp. 73, 116)

**Leo Africanus** (c. 1489–c. 1554)
A traveler and writer born in Granada, al-Andalus, later captured by pirates and converted to Christianity in Rome. His *Description of Africa* (c. 1526) describes Timbuktu's thriving intellectual culture: *"Here are many doctors, judges, priests, and other learned men that are well maintained at the king's costs. Various manuscripts and written books are brought here . . . and sold for more money than other merchandise."* He is a DBQ source for Sub-Saharan African achievements. (AMSCO p. 72)

**Ubaydallah al-Bakri** (c. 1014–1094)
A scholar from al-Andalus (Córdoba, Spain) who wrote about West Africa based on interviews with travelers — he never visited Africa himself. His 1068 description of the city of Ghana is one of the oldest written sources on the region. His point of view (Muslim, outside observer, relying on secondhand accounts) is important for AP DBQ sourcing analysis. (AMSCO p. 73)

## Movements and Forces

**The Mongols**
See [[Concept Notes/Mongols|Mongols]]. The dominant military force of the 13th–14th centuries, whose conquests paradoxically created the Pax Mongolica and facilitated cross-Eurasian trade and exchange. (AMSCO pp. 67–68)

**Turkish Peoples (Seljuks, Ottomans, and others)**
Multiple distinct Turkish groups from Central Asia who built separate empires across the eastern Mediterranean, Persia, and South Asia. Unlike the Mongols, Turkish rulers typically converted to Islam and integrated into existing Islamic political structures rather than destroying them. (AMSCO pp. 67–68)

**Islamic Missionary Activity**
The spread of Islam through trade networks, Sufi missionaries, and military conquest. In Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, Islam spread primarily through trade and peaceful conversion; in South Asia, it spread significantly through military conquest. See [[Concept Notes/Sufism|Sufism]] for the role of Sufi orders in conversion. (AMSCO p. 67)
