# Key Terms — Unit 5.9: Society and the Industrial Age

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

1. **Urbanization** — The process by which an increasing proportion of a population lives in cities; driven by industrialization's concentration of factories and jobs in urban areas. (Manchester, Birmingham, Chicago, Pittsburgh, and Essen grew from small towns to industrial metropolises within a generation; urbanization was the most visible social consequence of industrialization.)

2. **Middle class (bourgeoisie in the sociological sense)** — The new social class of managers, professionals, clerks, teachers, shopkeepers, and small businesspeople that emerged between the industrial bourgeoisie (factory owners) and the proletariat (wage laborers). (Defined itself by education, domestic respectability, and consumption rather than by birth or productive property; politically supported liberalism and reform.)

3. **"Separate spheres" ideology** — The Victorian middle-class belief that men should occupy the "public sphere" (work, politics, commerce) while women should occupy the "private sphere" (home, family, moral nurture). (A powerful middle-class ideal that had limited application to working-class reality; provided the ideological backdrop against which feminist movements organized.)

4. **Cult of domesticity** — The American version of "separate spheres"; celebrated the middle-class home as a refuge of moral purity from the competitive commercial world; the "true woman" was pious, pure, submissive, and domestic. (Provided the ideological framework within which women's suffrage activists had to argue their case — many early suffragists argued *from* domestic virtues rather than against them.)

5. **Child labor** — The employment of children in factories, mines, and textile mills under often brutal conditions; pervasive in early industrialization before legislative restriction. (British Factory Acts progressively restricted child labor from the 1830s; the 1842 Mines Act banned children under 10 from underground work; effective restriction of most child industrial labor came gradually through the 19th century.)

6. **Factory Act (Britain, 1833)** — Legislation limiting children's factory working hours (9–13-year-olds to 8 hours/day; 14–17-year-olds to 12 hours/day) and creating the first factory inspectors. (The first significant government regulation of factory conditions; illustrates both the scale of the problem and the beginning of the state's regulatory response.)

7. **Cholera** — A deadly bacterial disease transmitted through contaminated water; epidemic in industrial cities in 1832, 1848, and 1854; killed indiscriminately across class lines when water supplies were contaminated. (The 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in London, analyzed by Dr. John Snow, became the founding episode of modern epidemiology — Snow traced the outbreak to a contaminated water pump, providing early evidence for germ theory.)

8. **Germ theory** — The scientific discovery (Pasteur, Koch) that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases; replaced miasma theory (disease caused by "bad air"). (Germ theory enabled rational public health policy — clean water, sewage systems, food safety — transforming urban death rates and enabling the population growth that continued into the 20th century.)

9. **John Snow (1813–1858)** — British physician who traced the 1854 London cholera epidemic to a contaminated water pump on Broad Street — a founding act of modern epidemiology and a key step toward germ theory. (His method — mapping cases to their geographic source — established the evidence-based approach to public health.)

10. **Edwin Chadwick (1800–1890)** — British public health reformer who authored the *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain* (1842), documenting the connection between poverty, poor sanitation, and disease. (His report drove the Public Health Act of 1848, requiring local governments to provide clean water and sewage; pioneered the idea that government had a responsibility for public health.)

11. **Tuberculosis (TB)** — A bacterial respiratory infection ("white plague" or "consumption") that was the leading cause of death in 19th-century industrial cities, spread by overcrowding, poor ventilation, and malnutrition. (TB killed one in four Europeans in the early 19th century; its prevalence illustrates how industrial urbanization created disease environments that required both medical and social remedies.)

12. **Working-class family** — In industrializing societies, the working-class family pattern differed significantly from the middle-class "separate spheres" ideal: women and children worked alongside men, family income was pooled for survival, and the home was not a refuge but often an additional workplace (piecework, domestic service). (Illustrates the gap between ideological prescriptions and working-class reality; also explains why working-class women were central to labor organizing.)

13. **Consumer culture** — The culture of purchasing and displaying manufactured goods as markers of social status; emerged with the middle class in the 19th century, driven by rising incomes and the availability of mass-produced goods. (Department stores (Bon Marché, Paris; Macy's, New York) were its institutional expression; consumer culture was both a product of industrialization and a driver of continued demand for manufactured goods.)

14. **Feminism (first wave)** — The political movement for women's legal and political equality, including the right to vote (suffrage), own property, access education, and enter professions; emerged in the 19th century in response to industrialization's gender contradictions. (Mary Wollstonecraft's earlier arguments were vindicated by the obvious contradictions of separate spheres ideology; organized suffrage movements emerged from the 1840s onward.)

> [!exam-tip]
> "Separate spheres" ideology is a high-yield AP topic because it allows you to: (1) analyze gender change in industrializing societies, (2) connect to early feminism, (3) compare middle-class and working-class experiences, and (4) evaluate the gap between ideological prescription and social reality. Always note that "separate spheres" was a *middle-class ideal* that did not describe most working women's lives.
