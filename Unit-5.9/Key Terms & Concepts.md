# Key Terms & Concepts

**Industrialization**
The transformation from agrarian/artisan economies to factory-based, machine-powered manufacturing. Industrialization reshaped class structures, family life, gender roles, and urban environments across the 19th century. The AP exam emphasizes its *unequal* social effects: it created new prosperity for the middle class while exposing the working class to dangerous, low-paid factory labor and overcrowded urban slums. Compare the social effects here with the technological drivers in [[Unit-5.3/Industrial Revolution Begins Overview|Industrial Revolution Begins Overview]] and [[Unit-5.4/Industrialization Spreads Overview|Industrialization Spreads Overview]].

**mass production**
Manufacturing of standardized goods in large quantities using machinery and the division of labor. Mass production lowered costs and expanded consumer access — it made goods cheaper and more abundant than ever before. But for workers, it had a darker side: the division of labor made each worker interchangeable and easily replaceable, depressing wages and reducing the skill and satisfaction of work compared to earlier artisan craft. The AP exam links mass production to both consumer society *and* working-class hardship.

**tenement**
Overcrowded, poorly constructed apartment buildings in urban industrial districts, often owned by the same factory owners who employed the residents. Tenements were dark, cramped, and lacked adequate sanitation or ventilation. They were the physical address of the working class in industrial cities and became a focus of social reform movements and public health legislation by the late 19th century.

**slums**
Urban neighborhoods where low-income industrial workers were concentrated. Slums grew rapidly as cities expanded without planning. They were characterized by overcrowding, pollution, disease (especially cholera and typhoid), and poverty. Open sewers and polluted water supplies spread illness quickly. Slum conditions eventually provoked public health acts, sanitation reform, and welfare-state legislation — a key example of industrialization driving government expansion.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam pairs *tenements* and *slums* as evidence for how industrialization worsened living conditions for the working class. Use them together when arguing causation: industrialization → urbanization → tenements/slums → disease → reform.

**working class**
Industrial wage laborers (proletariat) who performed factory and mining work. The working class emerged as a distinct social class when the factory system replaced artisan craft. Unlike artisans who owned their tools and made complete products, factory workers sold their labor hour by hour, owned no capital, and could be replaced. Long hours (14 hours/day, 6 days/week), dangerous machinery, and low wages defined working-class life. The working class is the social context for the ideological responses examined in [[Unit-5.8/Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview|Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview]].

**white-collar**
Workers in office, managerial, or professional occupations who used literacy and education rather than manual labor. White-collar workers were part of the new middle class created by industrialization — factory managers, small business owners, clerks, and professionals. Distinct from blue-collar factory workers, they symbolized upward mobility and respectability in industrial society. Their growth is important evidence on the AP exam that industrialization did *not* simply create a two-class society of rich and poor.

> [!tip]
> The new class hierarchy: working class (blue-collar, wage labor) → new middle class (white-collar, managers/professionals) → industrial capitalists (owners). This three-tier structure replaced the old landed aristocracy at the top and is essential for AP comparison with pre-industrial social hierarchies in [[Unit-4.7/Changing Social Hierarchies Overview|Changing Social Hierarchies Overview]].
