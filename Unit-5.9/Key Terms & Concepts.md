# Topic 5.9 — Key Terms & Concepts

> [!tip]
> These terms appear frequently in AP exam stimulus questions and answer choices. Learn not just the definition but the **significance** — why did this matter? Who did it affect? What changed or stayed the same because of it?

---

## Urban Life & Housing

### Tenement
A shoddily constructed apartment building, typically in urban slums, where working-class families were crowded into tight, poorly ventilated living quarters. Tenements were often owned by the same factory owners who employed the residents. They were associated with fire hazards, open sewers, contaminated water, and rapid spread of disease.

> [!exam-tip]
> Tenements are primary evidence that the working class *did not equally benefit* from industrialization. When an AP stimulus shows urban poverty images or texts, tenements are the key contextual term.

### Slum
An area of a city characterized by overcrowding, poverty, poor sanitation, and deteriorating housing. Slums formed rapidly during the early decades of industrialization, when urban populations grew faster than city governments could plan or regulate housing. London's East End, Manchester's mill districts, and similar areas became notorious slums by the mid-19th century.

### Urbanization
The process by which people move from rural areas into cities, typically to seek wage labor in factories. Industrialization drove unprecedented urbanization across Britain and later Europe: Manchester grew from around 25,000 people in 1772 to over 300,000 by 1850. This rapid growth outpaced sanitation, housing, and policing infrastructure.

### Public Health Acts
Legislation passed in Britain from the 1840s onward that required municipalities to improve sanitation — building sewage systems, supplying cleaner water, removing rubbish, and enforcing building safety standards. These acts were a direct response to cholera epidemics and other public health disasters caused by overcrowded, polluted urban environments.

## Class Structure

### Working Class
The new industrial social class made up of factory laborers, coal miners, and other wage workers who owned no means of production. The working class sold their labor for wages — typically low, with long hours and dangerous conditions. The factory system's reliance on interchangeable parts and division of labor meant workers required fewer skills than artisans of earlier generations, making them easily replaceable and holding wages down through competition.

> [!exam-tip]
> The **working class** is not the same as the **peasantry** or **serfs** of earlier eras — this is a new industrial creation. Workers were *free* but often materially worse off than the rural poor they had replaced, at least in the early decades. This distinction matters on CCOT essays.

### White-Collar Worker
A middle-class worker who performed administrative, managerial, or professional work — office managers, accountants, bank employees, insurance agents, small business owners, and professionals like doctors and lawyers. White-collar work expanded dramatically with industrialization, as factories, banks, and trading companies all required administrators. Most white-collar workers were literate and identified as middle class.

### Middle Class (Bourgeoisie)
The expanding social group between the working class and the aristocracy. The industrial middle class included factory managers, professionals, small business owners, and office workers. They enjoyed rising access to consumer goods, education, and cultural life — theaters, concert halls, and eventually sports facilities. Advertising and consumer culture increasingly targeted middle-class households.

### Industrialists / Captains of Industry
The owners of factories and large corporations who accumulated wealth through industrial production rather than land ownership. By the late 19th century, these industrial capitalists had largely displaced the landed aristocracy as the most powerful social group. Their wealth came from investments and dividends, not from feudal or agricultural holdings.

> [!tip]
> The rise of the **industrialist** class is a key example of **change** in class structure: a new economic elite based on *capital* rather than *land* or *birth*. Connect this to Enlightenment ideas about merit vs. inherited status.

### Landed Aristocracy
The traditional European elite who derived their wealth, status, and political power from land ownership — and who were increasingly overshadowed by industrial capitalists in the 19th century. The decline of the aristocracy's dominance is a major continuity-and-change theme in Period 3.

## Work & Family Life

### Division of Labor
The factory practice of breaking production into specialized, repetitive tasks performed by different workers on an assembly line, rather than having a single skilled artisan craft a complete product. The division of labor increased production efficiency but reduced the skill required for each individual task — and reduced workers' sense of craft and control over their work.

### Interchangeable Parts
The manufacturing innovation of producing standardized, identical components that could replace each other — pioneered in the American System of manufacturing. Combined with the division of labor, interchangeable parts transformed manufacturing from skilled craft to unskilled assembly, making workers more easily replaceable.

### Factory Work vs. Farm Work
Before industrialization, families worked together on farms or in cottage industries, setting their own schedules according to seasonal rhythms. Factory work severed this pattern: workers had to leave home for 14-hour days, six days a week, answering to the factory whistle rather than natural cycles. The psychological and social disruption — what AMSCO calls a "culture shock" — was enormous for former farming families.

## Gender & Family

### Cult of Domesticity
An ideology that emerged by the late 1800s, promoted through advertising and pamphlets, that idealized the middle-class female homemaker. According to this ideology, a woman's proper role was to manage the household, raise children, and create a domestic refuge from the harsh public world of commerce and industry. Women were encouraged to be "pious, submissive, pure, and domestic." The cult of domesticity defined housewifery as a **status symbol** — a sign that a husband could support his family without his wife's labor.

> [!warning]
> The cult of domesticity did **not** reflect the reality of working-class women's lives. Working-class women had to manage the household *and* work full-time in factories or domestic service — often for wages half what men earned. The cult was largely a **middle-class** fantasy.

### Feminism (Early Industrial Era)
The movement advocating for women's legal, political, and social equality. Industrialization contributed to feminism in two contradictory ways: the cult of domesticity constrained middle-class women, while working-class women's economic participation in factories gave women more visible roles in public life. The [[Concept Notes/Seneca Falls Convention|Seneca Falls Convention]] (1848) — where 300 people met in upstate New York to call for women's equality — was the major early landmark of organized Western feminism in this era.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Seneca Falls Convention (1848) is a recurring piece of evidence for AP essays on **continuity and change in gender roles** and on **Enlightenment ideas applied to social reform**. The *Declaration of Sentiments* produced there directly echoed the language of the Declaration of Independence.

## Environment

### Fossil Fuels
Coal, petroleum, and natural gas — the carbon-based energy sources that powered the Industrial Revolution. Coal was the dominant fuel of the 19th century, producing more energy than wood but generating enormous quantities of toxic smoke. Industrial towns became choked with **smog** (smoke combined with fog), causing respiratory disease. Burning fossil fuels marked a permanent environmental break from the pre-industrial world.

### Environmental Pollution
The Industrial Revolution dramatically worsened air and water quality. Coal-burning factories produced toxic air pollution and smog. Factories dumped industrial waste directly into streams, rivers, and lakes. [[Concept Notes/Fossil Fuels|Fossil fuel]] combustion and chemical byproducts contaminated urban water supplies, spreading cholera, typhoid, and other waterborne diseases. The Thames River in London — the city's primary source of drinking water — was severely polluted by sewage and industrial discharge throughout much of the 19th century.

### Cholera
A deadly waterborne disease caused by the bacterium *Vibrio cholerae*, spread through contaminated water. Cholera epidemics devastated 19th-century industrial cities, killing tens of thousands. London experienced major cholera outbreaks in 1832, 1848, 1854, and 1866. The disease's spread through filthy water supplies was a direct consequence of urbanization without adequate sanitation — and a major driver of public health reform.

## Industrial Legacy

### Mass Production
The large-scale manufacturing of goods using machines, standardized parts, and the division of labor. Mass production made goods cheaper, more abundant, and more widely accessible than ever before in human history. It was both the great achievement and the great disruption of the Industrial Revolution.

### Global Inequalities
Industrialization created a widening gap between industrialized nations and the rest of the world. Industrial states needed vast quantities of raw materials — cotton, rubber, coal, metals — that they extracted from colonized or economically dominated regions: Latin America, Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia. By exploiting overseas resources at artificially low prices, they **undercut early industrialization** in Egypt, India, China, and other regions, locking them into roles as raw material suppliers rather than industrial producers. This set the stage for the new imperialism of the late 19th century.
