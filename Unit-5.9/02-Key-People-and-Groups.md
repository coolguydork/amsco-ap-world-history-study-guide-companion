# Key People and Groups — Unit 5.9: Society and the Industrial Age

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

---

## The Urban Working Class
**Region:** Industrial Britain, Europe, USA | **Period:** 1760s–1900s

- The largest social group created by industrialization; included factory workers, miners, construction workers, domestic servants, and street vendors.
- Lived in overcrowded tenements with no running water or sewage; worked 12–16 hour days for wages insufficient to cover food, rent, and clothing simultaneously.
- Their conditions are documented in government reports (Chadwick's Sanitary Report, 1842; factory commission reports), literature (Dickens, Zola), and Engels's *Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845).

---

## The New Middle Class
**Region:** Industrial nations | **Period:** 19th century

- Managers, professionals (doctors, lawyers, engineers, teachers), clerks, and shopkeepers who occupied the space between factory owners and factory workers.
- Defined themselves by education, respectability, domestic comfort, and consumer behavior — not by birth or productive property.
- The political constituency for liberalism, reform movements, and eventually the welfare state; also the primary audience for literature, newspapers, and the emerging consumer culture.

---

## Edwin Chadwick (1800–1890)
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** 1830s–1850s

- Authored the *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population* (1842), the foundational document of the British public health movement; documented the direct link between poverty, poor sanitation, and high mortality.
- His report drove the Public Health Act of 1848, requiring local governments to provide clean water supplies and sewage systems.
- Chadwick's career illustrates how government-commissioned research could drive policy reform — the bureaucratic model of evidence-based governance.

---

## John Snow (1813–1858)
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** 1850s

- Traced the 1854 Broad Street cholera epidemic to a contaminated water pump by mapping cases geographically — establishing the method of epidemiology before germ theory was confirmed.
- His work demonstrated that cholera was waterborne (not airborne "miasma"), providing the evidential basis for water supply reform.
- A landmark figure in the history of medicine and public health; his "shoe-leather epidemiology" remains a model of applied scientific investigation.

---

## Louis Pasteur (1822–1895)
**Region:** France | **Period:** 1850s–1890s

- Developed germ theory — the demonstration that microorganisms cause disease — through experiments disproving spontaneous generation and identifying specific pathogens.
- Invented pasteurization and developed vaccines against anthrax and rabies.
- His work transformed medicine from treating symptoms to targeting specific microbial causes — enabling modern public health interventions (water treatment, food safety, vaccination).

---

## Charles Dickens (1812–1870)
**Region:** Britain | **Period:** Mid-19th century

- Novelist who depicted industrial poverty, child labor, and urban squalor in works including *Oliver Twist* (1838), *Hard Times* (1854), and *Bleak House* (1852–53).
- His novels reached middle-class audiences who had little direct experience of industrial working-class conditions; they created political pressure for reform by making poverty emotionally visible and morally urgent.
- Illustrates the role of literature and culture in driving social reform — the cultural dimension of reactions to industrialization.

---

## Elizabeth Cady Stanton (1815–1902) and Susan B. Anthony (1820–1906)
**Region:** United States | **Period:** Mid-to-late 19th century

- American suffragists who organized the Seneca Falls Convention (1848), the first women's rights convention in the United States, which issued the *Declaration of Sentiments* modeled on the Declaration of Independence.
- Led the Women's suffrage movement in the USA for decades; combined rights to vote with demands for property rights, divorce reform, and educational equality.
- Their movement illustrates how industrialization's "separate spheres" ideology generated organized feminist resistance — the contradiction between Enlightenment ideals and women's actual subordination became politically explosive.

---

## Japanese Women Factory Workers (Meiji Era)
**Region:** Japan | **Period:** 1870s–1900s

- Young rural women recruited to work in Japan's textile mills (especially silk reeling and cotton spinning) were the primary labor force of Japan's earliest factories.
- Sent earnings home to rural families; often lived in company dormitories under strict discipline; had very limited rights or mobility.
- Illustrate how industrialization used women's labor across cultures while maintaining their subordinate social position; also illustrate that the "separate spheres" ideology was not universal — it was a specifically Western middle-class construction.
