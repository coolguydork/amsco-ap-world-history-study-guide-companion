# Timeline — Unit 5.9: Society and the Industrial Age

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

> **Big Picture:** Industrialization created the modern urban social order — with its class structure, gender ideologies, public health challenges, and consumer culture — over roughly a century (1760s–1900s). By 1900, the social consequences of industrialization were being actively managed through municipal reform, labor legislation, medical science, and feminist organizing — but the basic structures of inequality persisted.

---

| Date | Event | Region | Significance |
|------|-------|--------|--------------|
| 1760s–1800s | Rural-to-urban migration accelerates in Britain | Britain | Working-class city formation begins; Manchester, Birmingham explode in population |
| 1802 | Health and Morals of Apprentices Act (Britain) — first child labor law | Britain | Limits hours for apprenticed children; largely ineffective; illustrates early awareness of problem |
| 1820s | "Separate spheres" ideology solidified in middle-class culture | Britain/Europe | Domestic women / breadwinning men become cultural ideal; shapes reform discourse |
| 1830s | Cholera pandemic reaches Europe and North America | Global | **Change:** First epidemic that makes cross-class nature of urban pollution catastrophically visible |
| 1833 | Factory Act (Britain) — children under 9 banned from most factories | Britain | **Change:** State restricts child labor; factory inspectors created |
| 1838 | Dickens publishes *Oliver Twist* | Britain | Literary depiction of industrial poverty; mobilizes middle-class reform sympathy |
| 1842 | Chadwick's *Sanitary Report* | Britain | Documents link between sanitation and disease; drives public health legislation |
| 1842 | Mines Act (Britain) — bans women and boys under 10 underground | Britain | Response to shocking documentation of conditions; parliamentary reform driven by public horror |
| 1845 | Engels publishes *Condition of the Working Class in England* | Britain | Systematic sociological documentation of Manchester working-class conditions |
| 1847 | Ten Hours Act (Britain) — limits daily factory hours for women and adolescents | Britain | **Change:** Working day capped; major reform victory for labor movement |
| 1848 | Public Health Act (Britain) — requires local water and sewage systems | Britain | Municipal government responsibility for public health established |
| 1848 | Seneca Falls Convention; *Declaration of Sentiments* (USA) | USA | **Change:** First organized women's rights convention; demands voting rights, property rights, education |
| 1850s | Manchester population reaches 316,000 (from 25,000 in 1772) | Britain | Urbanization's speed illustrated; infrastructure cannot keep pace |
| 1854 | Broad Street cholera epidemic; John Snow maps cases to water pump | Britain | **Change:** Epidemiology founded; evidence for germ theory accumulates; water supply reform driven |
| 1858 | "Great Stink" (London); Thames becomes unbearable sewer | Britain | Municipal crisis forces London's investment in Bazalgette sewer system |
| 1860s | Pasteur develops germ theory of disease | France | **Change:** Scientific basis for public health interventions established |
| 1866 | Elizabeth Garrett Anderson becomes first British woman to qualify as physician | Britain | Women begin entering professions despite "separate spheres" restrictions |
| 1869 | Mill publishes *The Subjection of Women* | Britain | Philosophical argument for women's equality; Enlightenment logic applied to gender |
| 1875 | London sewer system completed (Bazalgette) | Britain | **Change:** Clean water supply; death rates from waterborne disease fall dramatically |
| 1880s | Koch identifies bacteria causing TB and cholera | Germany | Germ theory on firm experimental basis; specific interventions become possible |
| 1888 | Matchgirls' Strike (London) — women and girls win union recognition | Britain | **Change:** Unskilled female workers successfully organize; expands labor movement beyond skilled crafts |
| 1890s | Chicago population: 1.1 million; New York: 3.4 million | USA | American industrial cities match or exceed British counterparts |
| 1890 | Ellis Island opens; processes ~12 million immigrants (1892–1954) | USA | Industrial immigration at peak; working-class ethnic diversity established |

---

> [!tip] Continuity vs. Change
> **Continuity:** Extreme gender inequality, child poverty, and class hierarchy persisted throughout this period; the changes were in the *legal* and *institutional* frameworks addressing them, not in their elimination.
> **Change:** By 1900, the expectation that government had responsibility for public health, child protection, and workers' basic conditions was established — a fundamental shift from the laissez-faire ideology that had prevailed in 1800.
