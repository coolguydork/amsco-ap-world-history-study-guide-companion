# Society & the Industrial Age Overview

**Unit 5 · Topic 5.9 | AP Period 3 | Printed pp. 343–350**
**AP Themes:** SIO · ENV · CDI · MIG
**AP Skill:** Causation

**Essential Question:** How did industrialization cause change in existing social hierarchies and standards of living?

## What This Topic Is About

[[Unit-5.3/Industrial Revolution Begins Overview|Industrial Revolution Begins Overview]] and [[Unit-5.4/Industrialization Spreads Overview|Industrialization Spreads Overview]] explain *how* factories emerged and spread. Topic 5.9 asks what that transformation did to *people* — their family structures, class positions, gender roles, living conditions, and natural environment. The AP exam tests this heavily because it links economic change (ECN) to social change (SIO) and environmental degradation (ENV).

## New Class Hierarchy

[[Concept Notes/Industrialization\|Industrialization]] shattered the old landed-gentry order and built a new three-tier class structure in its place.

| Class | Who they were | AP significance |
|---|---|---|
| Working class | Factory and mine laborers | Low wages, replaceable, dangerous work |
| New middle class | Managers, professionals, small-business owners | "White-collar" — literate, educated |
| Industrial capitalists | Factory and corporation owners | Displaced landed aristocracy as power brokers |

The **working class** sold their labor hour by hour. Unlike earlier artisans who made complete products, factory workers performed one narrow task — the division of labor made them interchangeable and kept wages down. The **new middle class** (white-collar workers) became the defining social layer of industrial society: literate, upwardly mobile, and invested in respectability.

## Urban Conditions and Reform

Cities grew faster than governments could plan for them. The results were grim. Working families were packed into **tenements** — poorly built, overcrowded apartment blocks often owned by the same factory owners who employed the residents. These buildings sat in **slums** where open sewers, polluted water, and coal-smoke air created cholera, typhoid, and respiratory disease. Fire and crime followed.

Over time, urban reformers and governments responded: police and fire departments were created; public health acts established sanitation systems; building codes reduced hazards. The process was slow and uneven, but industrial cities eventually became more livable — at least for the growing middle class.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam often asks about the *relationship* between industrialization and reform: environmental disasters and public health crises directly drove government intervention and welfare-state legislation.

## Effects on Women

Industrialization affected women differently by class — a favorite AP comparison prompt.

**Working-class women** took jobs in textile factories and domestic service because their families needed the income. Factory owners paid women roughly half of men's wages. Britain eventually banned women from coal mining (1840s).

**Middle-class women** did *not* work for wages — not working was itself a status symbol proving a husband could support the family alone. The "cult of domesticity" (reinforced by advertising and conduct pamphlets by the 1880s) idealized the female homemaker as pious, pure, submissive, and domestic.

Yet industrialization also **spurred feminism**. Women's absence from the workplace created organizing space; the Seneca Falls Convention (1848) in the U.S. became an early landmark of the women's rights movement.

> [!tip]
> Compare to [[Unit-4.7/Changing Social Hierarchies Overview|Topic 4.7]]: [[Unit-4.7/Changing Social Hierarchies Overview|Changing Social Hierarchies Overview]] — industrialization created new hierarchies rather than simply reshuffling old ones.

## Effects on Children

Early industrialization was brutal for children. Children as young as five worked in textile mills; their small bodies were useful for climbing into machinery. Coal mines were even more hazardous — oppressive heat, heavy loads, lung-damaging dust, and constant risk of collapse. Reform movements eventually won child-labor legislation, but this took most of the 19th century.

## Effects on the Environment

Industrial production ran on coal, which meant toxic air (smog = smoke + fog), respiratory illness, and acid deposits. Rivers and lakes absorbed factory waste, spreading cholera and typhoid. The environmental damage of early industrialization set patterns that worsened through the 19th century and into the 20th — connecting directly to **Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World** [[Unit-6.6/Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World Overview|Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World Overview]] as polluted urban environments pushed and pulled migration flows.

## Industrial Revolution's Legacy

**Mass production** made goods cheaper and more abundant, expanding consumer access. Factory growth pulled millions from rural areas to cities — and from less-industrialized countries to industrial ones. But industrialization also: concentrated poverty in urban slums, deepened global inequality (industrialized states extracted raw materials from colonized regions, undercutting industrialization in Egypt, China, and India), and created a new, contested relationship between workers and owners that fueled socialism and labor reform (see [[Unit-5.8/Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview|Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview]]).
