# People, States & Movements

## Key Figures

**Jane Addams (1860–1935)**
American social reformer and co-founder of Hull House (1889) in Chicago, one of the first settlement houses in the United States. Hull House provided social services to the urban poor — immigrants, women, children — in the heart of an industrial slum neighborhood. Addams' work is a direct AP example of how industrialization's social costs (overcrowding, poverty, child labor) generated organized reform responses. She later won the Nobel Peace Prize (1931). On the exam: use Addams as evidence for the emergence of a reform movement rooted in urban industrial conditions.

**Florence Nightingale (1820–1910)**
British nurse who revolutionized hospital care during the Crimean War (1853–1856) by improving sanitation and reducing mortality rates from disease. Nightingale professionalized nursing as a field and pioneered the use of statistical data in public health advocacy. Her career illustrates two AP-relevant patterns: (1) industrialization created new professions that women could enter (even if the "cult of domesticity" limited middle-class women in domestic life), and (2) the connection between industrial-era disease environments and the emergence of modern public health as a discipline.

**Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797)**
British writer and early feminist who argued in *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) that Enlightenment principles of reason and equality applied to women as much as to men. Wollstonecraft preceded the industrial era but her ideas became the intellectual foundation for women's rights movements that emerged *because of* industrialization — as the cult of domesticity confined middle-class women, feminist reformers invoked her arguments. The Seneca Falls Convention (1848) explicitly echoed her Enlightenment framework.

> [!exam-tip]
> These three figures together illustrate that industrialization's social effects were not passive — they generated organized responses from reformers and activists. Jane Addams = urban poverty reform; Florence Nightingale = public health; Mary Wollstonecraft (+ Seneca Falls) = women's rights. All three are AP-testable.

## Movements

**The Women's Rights Movement / Feminism**
[[Concept Notes/Industrialization\|Industrialization]] paradoxically both *constrained* and *enabled* feminism. The cult of domesticity confined middle-class women to the home, yet industrialization also drew working-class women into the paid workforce and created new organizing opportunities. When men left communities for factory work, women remained and developed civic networks. The Seneca Falls Convention (1848, New York) — attended by ~300 people and producing the Declaration of Sentiments — was a landmark of organized feminist activism, directly linked to the Enlightenment values of equality and individual rights (see [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment Overview]]). By the late 19th century, suffrage movements were active across Europe and the Americas.

**Urban Reform and the Public Health Movement**
Slum conditions — cholera outbreaks, polluted water, fire risk — forced governments to act. Britain's Public Health Acts (1848, 1875) established sewage systems, clean water infrastructure, and building standards. Cities created police and fire departments. These reforms represent a major expansion of government's role in social welfare, foreshadowing 20th-century welfare states. On the exam, urban reform is the *institutional* response to the social and environmental costs of industrialization.

**Child Labor Reform**
Child labor was widespread in early industrialization — children as young as five worked in textile mills and coal mines. Reform movements, driven by journalists, religious groups, and social reformers, eventually won legislation restricting child labor: Britain's Factory Acts (1833, 1844) limited working hours for children and required some schooling. This is another AP example of industrialization generating organized reform responses.

> [!tip]
> For LEQ/DBQ: the reform movements in Topic 5.9 are excellent evidence for the argument that industrialization had contradictory effects — creating new wealth *and* new social crises that demanded state intervention.
