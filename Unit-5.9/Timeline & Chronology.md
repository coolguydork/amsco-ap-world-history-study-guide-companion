# Timeline & Chronology

| Date | Event | AP Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1792 | Mary Wollstonecraft publishes *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* | Applies Enlightenment equality to women; intellectual foundation for 19th-century feminism |
| c. 1800–1850 | Rapid, unplanned urbanization in Britain and Western Europe | Working-class migration to cities; tenements, slums, disease outbreaks (cholera) |
| c. 1800s | Child labor widespread in early factories and mines | Children as young as 5 in textile mills; coal mine dangers; eventually drove reform legislation |
| 1833 | British Factory Act | Limits working hours for children; requires some schooling; early example of industrial reform legislation |
| c. 1840s | Britain bans women from coal mining | Response to documented exploitation; example of state regulation of industrial labor |
| 1848 | British Public Health Act | Establishes sanitation infrastructure; government response to cholera epidemics in industrial slums |
| 1848 | Seneca Falls Convention (New York) | ~300 attendees; Declaration of Sentiments calls for women's equality; landmark of organized feminism |
| c. 1850s–1900s | "Cult of domesticity" ideology peaks | Advertising and conduct literature idealize female homemaker; reinforces gender division of labor |
| c. 1860s–1900 | Women's suffrage movements emerge in Europe and Americas | Industrial age creates conditions for organized women's rights activism |
| 1875 | British Public Health Act (expanded) | Comprehensive sanitation reform; model for urban public health worldwide |
| 1889 | Jane Addams co-founds Hull House, Chicago | Settlement house model; direct response to industrial urban poverty |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam rarely asks for exact dates on 5.9 content — it tests concepts and causation. However, knowing the *sequence* matters: unplanned urbanization → disease/slums → public health crises → reform legislation. The Seneca Falls Convention (1848) is the one specific date most likely to appear.

> [!tip]
> Notice the pattern: nearly every reform (Factory Acts, Public Health Acts, child labor laws) follows roughly 30–50 years of industrial damage. This lag between industrialization and reform is itself an AP argument: industrialization outpaced the capacity of governments and societies to respond.
