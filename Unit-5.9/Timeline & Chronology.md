# Topic 5.9 — Timeline & Chronology

> [!tip]
> Chronology matters on the AP exam — especially for CCOT and causation essays. Use this timeline to track how industrial social change accelerated over the 19th century and how reform followed (with a lag) behind the disruption.

---

## Chronological Overview: Industrial Society, c. 1769–1900

### 1769 — Watt's Steam Engine Patented

[[Concept Notes/James Watt|James Watt]]'s improved steam engine design becomes the technological foundation of factory production. As steam engines power ever-larger factories in urban centers, populations begin streaming into industrial cities — triggering all the social dynamics Topic 5.9 describes.

*Causation:* Steam power → factory concentration → urbanization → slums, class formation, environmental pollution.

---

### c. 1780s–1840s — Early Industrialization and Peak Urban Squalor

The first decades of large-scale industrialization produce the worst social conditions. Cities grow faster than governments can manage. Tenements and slums proliferate. Open sewers run alongside homes. Children as young as five work in textile mills and coal mines. Working families crowded into factory-owned housing near the factories where they labor 14-hour days, six days a week.

*Key text:* Charles Dickens, *Oliver Twist* (1839) — published at the peak of this period.

---

### 1833 — Factory Act (Britain)

Britain's first significant child labor legislation. Limited children under nine from working in textile mills; restricted hours for children under thirteen; required two hours of daily schooling for child workers. Passed partly in response to the **Sadler Report** (1832), which documented horrific factory conditions through parliamentary testimony.

*Significance:* First major legislative response to child labor — evidence that industrial social problems could produce government reform, though enforcement was initially weak.

---

### 1840s — Women Banned from Coal Mining (Britain)

The **Mines Act of 1842** prohibited women and children under ten from working in underground coal mines. Women had previously worked alongside men hauling coal in extremely dangerous conditions. The ban reflected both humanitarian concern and the **cult of domesticity** ideology — that women belonged in the home, not underground.

> [!warning]
> The Mines Act did not help working-class women economically — it simply removed one avenue of employment. Many women were left with fewer options, not more freedom. Don't assume reform = improved lives automatically.

---

### 1845 — Engels, *The Condition of the Working Class in England*

Friedrich Engels publishes his detailed account of Manchester's industrial slums — documenting tenement housing, pollution, disease, and child labor. This work provides the empirical foundation for Marxist critique and remains a landmark primary source for understanding industrial-era urban conditions.

---

### 1848 — Seneca Falls Convention (United States)

About 300 people gather in Seneca Falls, New York, to call for women's legal and political equality. Elizabeth Cady Stanton drafts the *Declaration of Sentiments*, which echoes the Declaration of Independence: "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal." The Convention marks the beginning of organized feminist political advocacy in the United States.

*Connection:* The Seneca Falls movement applies [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment]] natural rights arguments to gender — the same logic used in the American and French Revolutions, now applied to women's exclusion from political life.

---

### 1848 — *The Communist Manifesto* Published

[[Concept Notes/Karl Marx|Karl Marx]] and Engels publish *The Communist Manifesto*, responding directly to the social conditions described in Topic 5.9. "A specter is haunting Europe — the specter of communism." Marx's critique grew directly from observation of the industrial working class's misery — the proletariat created by the very factory system and urbanization that produced the slums and child labor of this topic.

---

### 1848–1858 — Peak of London's "Great Stink"

London's Thames River becomes critically polluted with sewage and industrial waste. The city's drinking water — drawn from the same river — spreads cholera epidemics. The **Great Stink** of 1858 (when the smell became literally unbearable in Parliament) finally forced the construction of a modern sewage system, completed in the 1860s.

*Significance:* The Great Stink is a landmark case of **environmental crisis driving government action** — an early public health win from the industrial era.

---

### 1840s–1870s — Public Health Acts (Britain)

A series of acts requiring municipalities to improve sanitation: build sewage systems, supply cleaner water, remove rubbish, enforce building standards. These reforms reduced cholera mortality significantly by the 1870s. They represent **top-down government response** to industrial social problems, paralleling the bottom-up pressure from labor unions and reformers.

---

### Late 1800s — Advertising and Consumer Culture

Mass production makes goods cheaper and more widely available. A consumer culture emerges targeting the growing middle class. Advertising promotes the **cult of domesticity** — encouraging women to purchase household products. The image of the ideal housewife is constructed and sold. This is both a product of industrialization (mass-produced goods need mass markets) and a mechanism of social control (defining women's proper sphere).

---

### c. 1800–1900 — Global Inequality Deepens

Throughout the 19th century, industrial states' demand for raw materials accelerates the exploitation of colonized regions. Cotton from South Asia and the Caribbean; rubber from Africa; metals from Latin America. Cheap industrial imports from Britain undercut Egypt's, India's, and China's early industrialization attempts. By 1900, the gap between industrialized and non-industrialized economies is enormous — setting the stage for the imperialism studied in Unit 6.
