# Practice & Review

## Multiple-Choice Practice

*Source: Bismarck speech, 1881 (AMSCO p. 348)*

**Questions 1–3** refer to Otto von Bismarck's 1881 speech advocating a workers' insurance bill in Germany:

> "The invalid workman is saved from starvation by the measure we now advocate… our first object in bringing forward this bill is to ensure kindlier treatment to this class of the poor; and next year I will do my best to give Deputy Richter full satisfaction as to the extent of the provision proposed to be made by the state for the better usage of the unemployed."

---

**1.** Which statement provides the best context for understanding the passage?

A. Germany lagged behind Britain in enacting reforms to improve the lives of industrial workers and the unemployed.
B. The problems of urban industrial workers and the unemployed were much worse in Germany than in Britain.
C. The United States led the way in enacting reforms to improve the lives of industrial workers and the unemployed.
D. Industrialization caused greater economic insecurity in the lives of many urban workers.

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> Bismarck is responding to the economic insecurity of industrial workers — this is the overarching context for *any* 19th-century welfare reform. Option A is wrong: Germany and Britain both enacted reforms; Germany's social insurance was actually *earlier* than comparable British legislation. Options B and C are comparative claims not supported by the passage. D is the broadly correct contextual statement.

---

**2.** Bismarck made the proposal in the passage because he believed that:

A. the end of capitalism would create an era of equality and justice for industrial workers
B. if the government did not address worker problems associated with industrialization, socialists and other radicals might incite a revolt
C. he had to appeal to socialists and other radicals in order to get them to join his government
D. the government needed to step in to reverse the environmental effects created by industrialization

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> Bismarck's social insurance legislation was explicitly designed to *undercut* the appeal of socialist movements by demonstrating that the state could address workers' needs without revolution. He was a conservative who used social reform tactically. Option A inverts his view (he wanted to *prevent* the end of capitalism). Option C is wrong — he opposed socialist participation in government. Option D is about environmental policy, which the passage doesn't address.

---

**3.** Which reform was instituted by another government in response to the problems of industrialization?

A. Britain expanded voting by reducing property-owning qualifications.
B. The U.S. government encouraged the growth of labor unions.
C. Russia adopted a form of utopian socialism.
D. The United States allowed a women's rights movement to emerge.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Britain's Reform Acts (1832, 1867) expanded suffrage in response to political pressure from the new industrial middle and working classes — a direct governmental response to the social changes of industrialization. Option B is incorrect: the U.S. government historically *opposed* labor unions through most of the 19th century. Option C is incorrect: Russia did not adopt utopian socialism as state policy. Option D: women's suffrage movements existed but governments did not actively "allow" them — this phrasing is inaccurate.

## Short-Answer Practice

### SAQ 1 — Five Points Neighborhood

*Source: Tyler Anbinder, *Five Points*, 2001 (AMSCO p. 349)*

> "Five Points was the most notorious neighborhood in nineteenth-century America… overlapping waves of Irish, Italian, and Chinese immigrants flooded this district… densely populated enclave was once renowned for jam-packed, filthy tenements, garbage-covered streets, prostitution, gambling, violence, drunkenness, and abject poverty."

**A.** Identify ONE way Anbinder's description relates to the development of a consumer society in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Contextualization
> The Five Points neighborhood illustrates the underside of consumer society's development: industrialization and mass production generated wealth and expanded consumer goods access for the middle class, but the same processes created urban poverty and overcrowded slums for low-income workers and immigrants. The dense, impoverished Five Points neighborhood was home to workers whose labor in nearby factories and workshops produced the goods that filled middle-class homes — the consumer society's prosperity and its slums were two sides of the same industrial coin.

**B.** Explain ONE way in which the global nature of trade influenced the population of urban areas in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Global trade drew migrant workers into industrial cities from across the world. As industrializing economies expanded production to meet global demand, they required large labor forces that could not be supplied locally. Economic disruptions in agrarian regions — such as the Irish Famine (1845–1852), caused partly by the collapse of subsistence agriculture in the face of market competition — pushed workers toward industrial cities in Britain and North America. Five Points' Irish, Italian, and Chinese immigrant populations directly reflected global economic flows: poverty and disruption in sending regions combined with demand for labor in industrial receiving regions to create ethnically diverse urban slums.

**C.** Explain ONE historical situation in the period 1750–1900, other than the one in the passage, in which industrial capitalism affected economic or societal structures.

> [!tip] Model Response — Evidence Beyond the Passage
> In Britain, the factory system's division of labor transformed artisan workers into an industrial working class, fundamentally altering economic and social structures. Before industrialization, skilled artisans — weavers, smiths, potters — owned their tools and made complete products, earning relatively high wages and maintaining social status. The factory system made individual skills redundant: standardized tasks required minimal training, wages fell, and workers became interchangeable. This destruction of artisan culture and creation of a propertyless working class generated new forms of political consciousness — labor unions, Chartism, and eventually socialist movements — demonstrating how industrial capitalism restructured both economic relations and social organization.

### SAQ 2 — Mass Production

**A.** Identify ONE point that supports the claim that mass production benefited consumers.

> [!tip] Model Response — Evidence
> Mass production lowered the cost of manufactured goods by using machinery and division of labor to produce standardized products in large quantities. Items that had previously been handcrafted and expensive — clothing, furniture, tools — became affordable to the growing middle class and even to some working-class families. The expansion of consumer access to manufactured goods is a direct benefit of mass production for consumers.

**B.** Identify ONE point that supports the claim that mass production was difficult for workers.

> [!tip] Model Response — Evidence
> Mass production's division of labor reduced each worker's task to a single, repetitive operation, eliminating the skill and craft knowledge that had defined artisan work. This made workers easily replaceable, suppressed wages through competition, and created physically and psychologically exhausting conditions — long hours performing the same motion repeatedly, operating dangerous machinery, and lacking any connection to the finished product. For workers, mass production meant deskilling, low pay, and loss of occupational autonomy.

**C.** Explain ONE way wealthy and poor people might have been *differently* affected by mass production.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Wealthy consumers benefited from mass production through access to a wider variety of cheaper goods — household items, clothing, and eventually luxury goods became more abundant and affordable. For wealthy factory owners, mass production generated enormous profits and capital accumulation. For poor workers, however, mass production's low wages, dangerous conditions, and deskilling outweighed any consumer benefit. A poor factory worker might earn just enough to buy the cheapest mass-produced goods while bearing the full physical cost of producing them, whereas a wealthy consumer enjoyed the products without experiencing the labor. This divergence illustrates how industrialization produced unequal outcomes across the class hierarchy simultaneously.

## Quick Review: AP Big Ideas for 5.9

The core AP argument for this topic is that **industrialization produced deeply unequal social outcomes**. It created the new middle class *and* the working class simultaneously. It gave middle-class women the "cult of domesticity" while sending working-class women into factories. It made goods cheaper and cities more dynamic while filling those cities with disease-ridden slums. For every AP prompt about industrialization's effects, you should acknowledge this fundamental tension between progress and exploitation — that is the sophisticated, nuanced position the AP exam rewards.
