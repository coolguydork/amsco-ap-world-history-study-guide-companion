# Unresolved Tensions After World War I

**AMSCO pp. 493–502 | AP Period 4 | Themes: GOV, CDI, SIO | Skill: Causation**

**Essential Question:** How did the peace settlement after WWI create unresolved tensions that contributed to anti-colonialism and WWII?

## The Core Argument

World War I raised two contradictory expectations. Colonial peoples — who had contributed troops, labor, and resources to the Allied war effort — hoped Wilson's principle of [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]] would earn them independence. Instead, the Paris Peace Conference extended European and Japanese imperial control through the [[Concept Notes/Mandate System\|mandate system]], dashing those hopes and supercharging anti-colonial [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|nationalism]] across Asia, Africa, and the Middle East. The unresolved tensions of 1919 became the kindling for both World War II and the post-1945 decolonization wave.

## Effects of WWI on Colonial Territories

The war showed that European powers were not invincible. Colonial soldiers fought in European trenches and returned home politicized. The **Big Three** at Paris (Wilson, Lloyd George, Clemenceau) applied self-determination only to white European populations. Colonized peoples in Asia and Africa were assigned to the **mandate system** — effectively a renamed form of colonialism dressed up in League of Nations language.

The pattern was consistent across regions: Arab soldiers who fought for the Allies were denied self-rule; African veterans returned to colonies that were unchanged or expanded; Chinese laborers who supported the Allies watched their territorial interests at Shandong handed to Japan. Each betrayal fueled a new nationalist movement.

## The Middle East: Mandate System & Balfour Declaration

Former Ottoman territories — Palestine, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon, Iraq — became League of Nations mandates under British and French control. The Arabs who had revolted against the Ottomans in expectation of independence were furious. This produced **Pan-Arabism**, an ideology demanding Arab unity and independence across North Africa and the Middle East.

Simultaneously, the 1917 **Balfour Declaration** promised a Jewish homeland in Palestine. **Zionists** (supporters of a Jewish homeland) immigrated in growing numbers to British-controlled Palestine, setting the stage for Arab-Jewish conflict that continues to this day.

> [!exam-tip]
> The mandate system is a favorite AP comparison point: it looks like Wilsonian internationalism but functions like old-fashioned colonialism. On DBQs and LEQs, use it as evidence that WWI's peace settlement *intensified* rather than reduced imperial control.

## Anti-Colonialism in South Asia

**[[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]]** transformed the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]] from an elite petition organization into a mass movement. His **satyagraha** ("devotion-to-truth") philosophy of nonviolent civil disobedience challenged British rule through boycotts, strikes, and willful arrest. Key events:

- **1919 Amritsar Massacre** — British troops killed ~379 peaceful protesters; radicalized moderate Indians and discredited British claims of benevolent rule
- **1930 Salt March** — Gandhi led thousands 240 miles to the sea to make salt illegally, defying Britain's salt monopoly; became a global symbol of nonviolent resistance

The independence movement was not unified on how a free India would be organized. **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** ultimately proposed a separate Muslim state — **Pakistan** — fearing Hindu domination in a unified India. **Jawaharlal Nehru** opposed partition. These tensions, unresolved in the interwar period, exploded at partition in 1947.

> [!warning]
> Do not confuse Gandhi's *South African* experience (where he developed satyagraha in the 1890s–1910s) with his India campaigns of the 1920s–40s. The AP may ask you to trace the continuity of the method across both contexts.

## Nationalism in East Asia

**Korea** was a Japanese colony since 1910. The **March First Movement** (1919) — involving up to 2 million Koreans — demonstrated mass nationalist sentiment; Japan crushed it but could not extinguish Korean nationalism.

**China's May Fourth Movement** (1919) exploded when the Paris Peace Conference gave Germany's Shandong concessions to Japan rather than returning them to China. Chinese intellectuals and workers rejected Western liberalism and turned toward Soviet Marxism. Two competing forces emerged: the **Chinese Communist Party (CCP)**, led eventually by **Mao Zedong**, and the **Kuomintang** (Nationalist Party), led by **Chiang Kai-shek** after Sun Yat-sen's death. Their civil war, interrupted by Japanese invasion, defined Chinese politics until 1949.

The **Long March** (1934–35) — a 6,000-mile retreat of the CCP from Nationalist attack — became the founding myth of Chinese communism. Only ~10,000 of 80,000+ survived, but Mao's prestige among peasants grew enormously.

Japan's response to the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]] was imperial expansion. Japan invaded Manchuria (1931) and set up **Manchukuo** as a puppet state. As Japan seized more territory, it framed its empire as the **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere** — claiming to liberate Asia from Western colonialism while in fact creating a Japanese imperial system.

## Anti-Colonialism in Africa

African movements were slower to develop in the interwar period but were gathering force. European-educated leaders like **Jomo Kenyatta** (Kenya) and **Léopold Sédar Senghor** (Senegal) studied in London and Paris, developed pan-African and Négritude ideas about African cultural identity, and returned home to lead independence movements after WWII. Labor strikes in French West Africa in the 1930s–40s showed that resistance was not only intellectual but economic.

> [!tip]
> The AP loves comparing anti-colonial strategies: Gandhi's nonviolent mass mobilization vs. African intellectuals' cultural assertion vs. Chinese communists' peasant revolution. All three are responses to the same broken promise of self-determination after WWI.
