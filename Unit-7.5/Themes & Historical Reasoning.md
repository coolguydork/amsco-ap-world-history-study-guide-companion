# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**AMSCO pp. 493–502 | AP Skill: Causation | Themes: GOV, CDI, SIO**

## AP Skill Focus: Causation

Topic 7.5 is explicitly a **causation** topic. The AP wants you to explain *why* anti-colonial movements intensified after WWI and *what they caused* in turn.

**Causes of intensified anti-colonialism (1919–1940s):**

The most immediate cause was the betrayal at Paris: colonial peoples who fought for the Allies expected [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]] but received the mandate system instead. Wilson's Fourteen Points had raised expectations that the peace conference deliberately dashed. This betrayal did not create anti-colonialism from nothing — nationalist movements had existed for decades — but it radicalized them and gave them a clear grievance narrative.

A second cause was the experience of WWI itself. Colonial soldiers fighting in European trenches learned that their rulers were not invincible, saw European civilization tear itself apart, and returned home with military skills and political awareness. French West African veterans, for example, assisted in colonial administration, creating a new class of politically engaged intermediaries.

A third cause was the spread of ideological alternatives. Wilson's liberalism having failed, many colonial intellectuals turned to Marxism (Mao, Ho Chi Minh) or developed their own ideologies (Pan-Arabism, Négritude). The Soviet Union actively courted anti-colonial movements, providing both ideology and organizational models.

**Effects of post-WWI unresolved tensions:**

In the short term: intensified nationalist movements across Asia and Africa, labor strikes in West Africa, the Chinese Civil War, Korean and Chinese nationalist uprisings. In the medium term: Japanese imperial expansion (Manchukuo, 1932) driven partly by its own nationalist grievances at Paris; the conditions for WWII in Asia. In the long term: post-WWII decolonization — the independence movements of the 1940s–70s grew directly from interwar organizing.

> [!exam-tip]
> On a Causation DBQ or LEQ, the AP wants you to distinguish *why nationalism intensified after WWI specifically* (not just that it existed). The answer is the combination of: (1) raised then dashed expectations, (2) radicalized returning soldiers, and (3) ideological alternatives to Western liberalism.

## GOV: Governance & Resistance

The mandate system represents a key continuity in imperial governance: despite Wilson's rhetoric, the major powers preserved and expanded colonial control. The **League of Nations** — supposed guarantor of international order — became a vehicle for legitimizing imperial expansion. Japan exploited this by framing its annexation of Manchukuo as a "mandate"-style arrangement.

The forms of anti-colonial resistance reveal how nationalists worked *within and against* colonial structures. Gandhi's [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]] had originally been a British-sponsored body for elite Indian political expression; Gandhi transformed it into a tool for mass mobilization against British rule. Similarly, African intellectuals educated in European universities used European concepts (liberalism, Marxism, romantic nationalism) against European empires.

> [!warning]
> Don't oversimplify Gandhi as purely anti-Western. He was an English-trained lawyer who used British legal concepts to challenge British law. His genius was turning the empire's own values (rule of law, Christian morality, Enlightenment liberalism) against its practice.

## CDI: Cultural & Demographic Identity

This topic is rich in CDI content. The key movements — satyagraha, Négritude, Pan-Arabism — are all fundamentally about *identity*: who are we, and why should we govern ourselves?

**Négritude** is the clearest CDI case: Senghor and Césaire argued that African cultural identity had intrinsic value and that colonialism's dehumanization of African peoples required a cultural as well as political response. Reclaiming African identity was itself a form of resistance.

**Pan-Arabism** similarly rooted political claims in cultural identity: the shared Arabic language, Islamic heritage (though Pan-Arabism was not exclusively religious), and historical experience of the Ottoman and now British/French empires. The Balfour Declaration's promise of a Jewish homeland in Palestine struck Pan-Arab nationalists as a colonialist imposition of a foreign cultural identity into Arab lands.

**Hindu-Muslim identity** in India: the partition debate shows how religious identity — which had coexisted in complex ways for centuries — was sharpened by the political necessity of defining who would rule an independent state. Jinnah's "two-nation theory" reframed religious community as political nationality.

## SIO: Social Interactions & Organization

The interwar anti-colonial movements involved new forms of mass organization. Gandhi's achievement was not just philosophical but organizational: he mobilized millions of ordinary Indians — peasants, urban workers, merchants — into coordinated political action. The boycott of British goods was both a protest and an economic strategy.

Labor organizing in French West Africa (railway strikes of 1925, general strikes of 1946) shows that resistance was not only elite and intellectual. Working-class Africans used the tools of industrial labor organizing — the same tools European workers used — to resist colonial economic exploitation.

## Think as a Historian: Purpose & Audience

The AP uses Ho Chi Minh's 1930 speech (announcing the Indo-Chinese Communist Party) as a primary source for this topic. Key points for evaluation:

**Purpose:** Mobilize workers, peasants, and colonial subjects against French imperialism. Ho explicitly frames WWI's aftermath as a disaster *for the colonized* and a recovery opportunity *for French imperialism* at the colonized's expense.

**Audience:** Workers, peasants, soldiers, youth — explicitly the laboring classes, not elites. This reflects the Marxist-Leninist political strategy of building a proletarian/peasant coalition.

**Limitations:** As a revolutionary rallying speech, Ho's account is explicitly partisan. He frames all French economic activity in Vietnam as pure exploitation and predicts WWII with certainty (which turned out to be accurate, but the prediction serves a rhetorical purpose). He does not acknowledge any benefit Vietnam received from French rule.

**Overcoming limitations:** Compare with French colonial administrative records; compare with Vietnamese non-communist nationalist accounts; look at economic data on wages and living standards in French Indochina to evaluate his claim of "starvation wages."

> [!tip]
> Purpose & Audience is a core AP historical thinking skill. For any primary source: ask (1) Why did the author write this? (2) Who was it for? (3) How do purpose and audience shape what is included, excluded, or exaggerated?
