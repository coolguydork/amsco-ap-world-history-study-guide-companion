# People, States & Movements

**AMSCO pp. 493–502**

## Key Leaders

**Mohandas Gandhi (1869–1948)**
The central figure of Indian independence. Gandhi developed his philosophy of *satyagraha* (nonviolent resistance) while fighting racial discrimination in South Africa (1893–1914), then returned to India to lead the independence movement. As *Mahatma* ("great soul"), he directed the Indian National Congress toward mass civil disobedience: the 1919 Non-Cooperation Movement, the 1930 Salt March, and the 1942 Quit India Movement. He consistently opposed partition, believing Hindu-Muslim unity was possible — a position that put him at odds with Jinnah. He was assassinated in 1948 by a Hindu nationalist who thought he was too accommodating of Muslims.

> [!tip]
> AP Causation angle: Gandhi's strategy works on two levels — direct pressure on Britain and international publicity that made continued colonialism politically embarrassing. Both were necessary.

**Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1876–1948)**
Muslim leader of the All-India Muslim League who initially supported Hindu-Muslim unity within the Indian National Congress. After years of unsuccessful attempts at compromise, he concluded that a Hindu-majority independent India would marginalize Muslims politically. His "two-nation theory" — that Hindus and Muslims were distinct nations requiring separate states — led to the creation of Pakistan (1947). He became Pakistan's first Governor-General. His story illustrates how the independence movement fractured along religious lines.

**Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964)**
Indian nationalist and Gandhi's political heir; first Prime Minister of independent India (1947–1964). Nehru advocated a secular, democratic, socialist India and pursued non-alignment during the Cold War (refusing to join either the U.S. or Soviet bloc). His opposition to Jinnah's partition plan was sincere but ultimately unsuccessful.

**Mao Zedong (1893–1976)**
Son of a prosperous peasant who became the leader of the Chinese Communist Party. Mao's key theoretical innovation was adapting Marxism to China: instead of an urban proletarian revolution (Lenin's model), Mao believed China's massive peasant population could be the revolutionary base. He led the Long March, rebuilt the CCP in the late 1930s–40s, defeated Chiang Kai-shek in the Civil War, and founded the People's Republic of China in 1949.

**Chiang Kai-shek (1887–1975)**
Leader of the Chinese Nationalist Party (Kuomintang) after Sun Yat-sen's death in 1925. Conservative and anti-communist, Chiang launched the 1927 attack on the CCP that began the Chinese Civil War. During the 1930s, his Nationalist government ruled most of China, but his failure to address corruption and poverty undermined popular support. Defeated by Mao's forces in 1949, he retreated to Taiwan, where the Republic of China government continues.

**Jomo Kenyatta (c. 1897–1978)**
Kenyan nationalist who studied at the London School of Economics; became politically radicalized in Europe where he experienced pan-Africanism and recognized the racial discrimination structuring colonialism. Returned to Kenya to lead the independence movement and became Kenya's first president (1964). His trajectory — education in Europe → political radicalization → return to lead independence — was the standard path for interwar African nationalist leaders.

**Léopold Sédar Senghor (1906–2001)**
Senegalese poet, intellectual, and politician who studied in Paris; co-founder with Aimé Césaire of the *Négritude* literary and intellectual movement, which asserted the value and dignity of African cultural identity against colonial racism. First president of Senegal (1960–80). Exemplifies how African anti-colonialism operated through cultural and intellectual assertion as well as political organizing.

> [!tip]
> Compare Kenyatta and Senghor with Gandhi: all three were educated in the colonial metropole, returned home radicalized, and used that knowledge against the colonizers — but their methods differed (mass nonviolent action vs. cultural/intellectual assertion vs. political organizing).

## States & Contested Territories

**India (British India)**
The jewel of the British Empire — a vast colony providing raw materials, markets, and soldiers. The interwar period saw the Indian National Congress evolve from an elite lobbying body to a mass movement under Gandhi. Independence came in 1947 with partition into India and Pakistan, accompanied by massive communal violence and the displacement of millions.

**China**
Not formally colonized, but dominated by foreign "spheres of influence." The May Fourth Movement (1919) triggered Chinese nationalism's turn toward Marxism after Western liberalism failed China at Paris. The struggle between the CCP and Kuomintang defined Chinese politics from 1927 to 1949. Japan's invasion of Manchuria (1931) and full-scale war from 1937 interrupted but did not end the Civil War.

**Korea**
Japanese colony since 1910. The March First Movement (1919) demonstrated that ~12% of the Korean population was willing to protest openly. Japan suppressed it harshly but the movement became a foundational event in Korean national consciousness.

**Mandate Territories (Middle East)**
Palestine, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon, Iraq — former Ottoman territories administered by Britain and France as League of Nations mandates. Arabs saw these as a betrayal of WWI promises; the simultaneous Balfour Declaration (Jewish homeland in Palestine) added another layer of conflict. These territories' borders, many drawn arbitrarily by colonial powers, still shape Middle Eastern conflict today.

## Movements

**[[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]]**
Founded 1885; became the primary vehicle of Indian independence under [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Gandhi's]] leadership in the 1920s–30s. Represented a broad Hindu nationalist base but also included Muslim members before Jinnah's departure.

**Chinese Communist Party vs. Kuomintang**
The central political struggle of interwar China. The CCP mobilized peasants; the KMT governed cities and was backed by business interests. Both fought Japanese imperialism (1937–45) as uneasy allies before resuming the Civil War, which the CCP won in 1949.

**Pan-Arabism**
The ideology that Arab peoples across North Africa and the Middle East form one nation and should be politically unified. Arose from the betrayal of Arab nationalists at Paris; remained a powerful but ultimately frustrated force throughout the 20th century.

**Négritude**
Intellectual and literary movement founded by African and Caribbean intellectuals (Senghor, Aimé Césaire) in Paris during the 1930s; asserted the value and beauty of African culture, history, and identity against colonial racism's claim of African inferiority. A cultural form of anti-colonial resistance.

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the distinction between the types of anti-colonial resistance for AP comparisons: (1) mass nonviolent civil disobedience (Gandhi), (2) armed/political nationalism (Kuomintang, early CCP), (3) intellectual/cultural assertion (Négritude, Kenyatta), (4) labor organizing (West African strikes). The AP may ask you to categorize or compare these.
