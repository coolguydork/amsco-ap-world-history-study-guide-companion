# Key Terms & Concepts

**AMSCO pp. 493–502**

## Government & Policy

**decolonization**
Post-WWII process by which European colonial empires granted independence to their territories; created 100+ new nations between 1945 and 1975. The interwar nationalist movements in this topic are the direct *cause* of decolonization — the promise was broken after WWI, fulfilled after WWII.

**mandate system**
Post-WWI arrangement giving Britain and France control over former Ottoman and German territories as "mandates" of the League of Nations; disguised colonialism. Article 22 of the League charter claimed colonized peoples needed "tutelage" from more "advanced" nations — racist paternalism used to justify imperial expansion. Cameroon, Palestine, Syria, Iraq, and others became mandates.

**Balfour Declaration**
1917 British statement promising to support a Jewish homeland in Palestine; conflicted with earlier British promises of Arab [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]]. Set the stage for decades of Israeli-Palestinian conflict. A key AP example of how WWI's peace settlement created lasting regional tensions.

**civil disobedience**
Non-violent refusal to obey unjust laws; used by Gandhi in India and later by MLK in the U.S. Civil Rights Movement; based on Thoreau's philosophy. In the AP context, focus on Gandhi's use: willingly accepting arrest to expose the moral bankruptcy of British colonial law.

**Pan-Arabism**
Ideology calling for the political unification of Arab peoples across North Africa and the Middle East based on shared language, culture, and identity; emerged as a response to Western imperialism and the mandate system after WWI. Became a powerful force in 20th-century Middle Eastern politics.

**Zionists**
Those who supported the creation of a Jewish homeland in Palestine; the movement emerged in the late 19th century in response to European antisemitism. The Balfour Declaration gave Zionism British backing; Jewish immigration to Palestine accelerated in the interwar period, intensifying Arab-Jewish tensions.

## Movements & Events

**[[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]]**
Political organization founded (1885) by educated Indians to seek greater autonomy from British rule; became the main vehicle of Indian independence. Under [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Gandhi's]] leadership in the 1920s–30s, it transformed into a mass movement.

**satyagraha (devotion-to-truth) movement**
Gandhi's philosophy and practice of nonviolent resistance to unjust laws; participants willingly accepted arrest and punishment to expose the injustice of British colonial rule. The approach influenced civil rights movements worldwide and is a key AP example of nonviolent anti-colonial resistance.

**Salt March**
1930 act of civil disobedience in which Gandhi led thousands of Indians 240 miles to the Arabian Sea to make salt illegally, defying the British salt monopoly. Galvanized the Indian independence movement internationally and demonstrated the power of nonviolent protest.

**March First Movement**
1919 series of Korean protests against Japanese colonial rule, involving up to 2 million Koreans; brutally suppressed by Japan with thousands killed. Demonstrated the strength of Korean nationalism and paralleled other post-WWI independence movements inspired by Wilson's Fourteen Points.

**May Fourth Movement**
1919 Chinese intellectual and student protest movement triggered by the Treaty of Versailles awarding Germany's Shandong concessions to Japan. Symbolized China's nationalism and rejection of Western imperialism; many leaders joined the Chinese Communist Party, reshaping China's political trajectory.

**Chinese Communist Party (CCP)**
Founded in 1921, the CCP grew under Mao Zedong's leadership by mobilizing China's peasantry. Survived the Long March and the Civil War against the Nationalists to establish the People's Republic of China in 1949. Represents the Marxist-Leninist alternative in the Chinese political struggle.

**Kuomintang**
Chinese Nationalist Party founded by Sun Yat-sen; advocated for Chinese independence, modernization, and national unity. Allied briefly with the CCP against warlords and Japan; led by Chiang Kai-shek after 1925; defeated by Communists in 1949 and retreated to Taiwan.

**Long March**
1934–35 retreat of the Chinese Communist Party's Red Army — approximately 6,000 miles through treacherous terrain to escape Nationalist forces; only ~10,000 of 80,000+ who began survived. Became a founding myth of the CCP, demonstrating ideological commitment and building Mao's legendary status.

## Countries & Places

**Palestine**
Former Ottoman territory placed under British mandate after WWI; promised to both Arabs (for wartime support) and Jewish Zionists (Balfour Declaration). Became a flashpoint for conflict as Jewish immigration increased in the 1920s–40s; the 1948 partition created Israel and the ongoing Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

**Pakistan**
Muslim-majority state carved out of British India at partition (1947), proposed by Jinnah to protect Muslim political interests; composed of separate western and eastern regions (the eastern becoming Bangladesh in 1971). Represents the resolution of Hindu-Muslim tensions through partition rather than unified independence.

**Amritsar**
Site of the 1919 massacre in Punjab, India, where British colonial troops fired into an unarmed crowd of ~10,000 protesters, killing ~379 and wounding 1,200. Radicalized the Indian independence movement and discredited British claims of benevolent imperialism; a watershed moment in Indian nationalism.

**Manchukuo**
Japanese puppet state established in Manchuria (northeastern China) in 1932 after Japan's invasion; installed the last Qing emperor Puyi as a figurehead. Its creation demonstrated Japanese imperial expansion and triggered League of Nations condemnation, which Japan ignored by withdrawing from the League.

**Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere**
Japan's propaganda term for its wartime empire across East and Southeast Asia (1930s–1945), framed as Asian liberation from Western colonialism. In reality a Japanese imperial system exploiting conquered territories for resources and labor; a key AP example of imperial ideology masking conquest.

## People & Titles

**Big Three**
The three main Allied leaders at the Paris Peace Conference (1919): U.S. President Woodrow Wilson, British PM David Lloyd George, and French PM Georges Clemenceau. They shaped the Treaty of Versailles and rejected self-determination for colonial peoples in Asia and Africa, fueling anti-colonial nationalism.

**Mahatma**
Sanskrit title meaning "great soul," given to Mohandas Gandhi by the Indian people; signifies his moral authority and spiritual leadership of the independence movement. Gandhi's use of nonviolent civil disobedience under this identity made him one of the most influential anti-colonial leaders in history.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP essays frequently ask you to explain the causes of anti-colonial nationalism after WWI. The strongest answer links *three factors*: (1) Wilson's broken promise of self-determination, (2) colonial soldiers' radicalization from WWI service, and (3) the mandate system's naked hypocrisy. Then trace specific movements (Gandhi, May Fourth, March First) as effects.

> [!tip]
> Mao vs. Lenin: Lenin's Marxism said revolution required an urban proletariat. Mao adapted this for China, arguing that *peasants* — the vast majority of China's population — could be the revolutionary class. This is the key conceptual distinction for AP Comparative essays on communist revolutions.
