# Decolonization after 1900

**AP Period 4** | **Unit 8: Cold War and Decolonization** | AMSCO pp. 579–588
**AP Themes:** GOV, CDI, SIO | **Skills:** Causation, Comparison

**Essential Question:** How did nationalist movements achieve independence, and what challenges did newly independent states face?

**Learning Objective:** Explain the causes and effects of decolonization after 1900.

---

## The Big Picture

Decolonization was one of the defining global transformations of the 20th century. Within roughly three decades after World War II, dozens of new nations emerged across Africa, Asia, and the Middle East as European empires collapsed under the combined pressure of nationalist movements, Cold War politics, economic exhaustion, and the ideological contradictions of colonial powers who preached liberty while denying it.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves to ask about the **causes** of decolonization (WWII weakened empires + nationalist ideology) and the **contrast** between negotiated vs. violent paths to independence. Know at least two specific examples of each.

The paths to independence varied dramatically. India achieved independence through mass civil disobedience, though partition into India and Pakistan triggered catastrophic communal violence. Ghana negotiated independence peacefully, becoming a model for sub-Saharan Africa. Algeria fought a brutal eight-year war against France. Vietnam's independence struggle became entangled with the Cold War, drawing in US military intervention. Egypt used the Suez Crisis to assert sovereignty against former colonial masters. These cases illustrate a central AP theme: **the form of independence shaped the nature of the new state**.

Most new states faced a common set of post-independence challenges: ethnic and religious divisions that colonial borders ignored, weak economies oriented toward colonial extraction rather than national development, and political instability that often led to military coups and one-party states. Cold War superpowers compounded these problems by backing authoritarian allies and fueling proxy conflicts.

## Why Decolonization Happened When It Did

**WWII as a turning point:** The war discredited European moral authority (colonial powers fighting fascism while denying freedom to colonies), physically exhausted European economies, and produced the UN and its [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]] principles. Japanese victories over European colonial forces (Singapore, Indochina) destroyed the myth of white invincibility.

**Ideological pressure:** The US and USSR both, for different reasons, opposed European colonialism publicly. The UN provided a diplomatic forum for independence movements. [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Charter\|Atlantic Charter]] language about self-determination was weaponized by colonial subjects.

**Nationalist movements:** Educated elites trained in European universities returned home with liberal, socialist, and nationalist ideas and organized mass movements. Gandhi's non-violent model, Nkrumah's pan-African vision, and Ho Chi Minh's communist nationalism all represented distinct but equally effective ideological frameworks.

> [!warning]
> Don't say decolonization was simply "given" by colonial powers. In most cases, colonial powers resisted — the British negotiated only after imperial overstretch, the French fought bloody wars in Indochina and Algeria, and Portugal held on until 1975. Independence was won, not granted.

## Common Post-Independence Patterns

**Military coups and one-party states:** Ghana (1966), Egypt (1952), Algeria (1965), Nigeria (multiple. These were not random — they reflected the difficulty of building democratic institutions amid ethnic divisions and economic underdevelopment, and Cold War superpowers often supported friendly military governments.

**Ethnic and religious conflict:** Colonial borders deliberately cut across ethnic lines to prevent unity. After independence, suppressed tensions exploded: Nigeria's Biafran War, India-Pakistan partition violence, Algeria's Islamist civil war. The map of post-colonial states was a colonial inheritance, not an ethnic or cultural reality.

**Economic dependency:** Newly independent states often remained economically tied to former colonizers through trade, debt, and infrastructure designed for extraction. This neo-colonial relationship is a key AP theme connecting decolonization to economic systems.

> Connect decolonization to earlier topics: the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]] traces back to the 19th century; [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]] builds on earlier African resistance covered in [[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview\|State Expansion Overview]]. Decolonization didn't begin in 1945 — it was the culmination of decades of organizing.
