# Themes & Historical Reasoning

*AMSCO pp. 579–588*

---

## AP Themes in This Topic

**GOV (Governance):** The collapse of colonial governance and the construction of new states is the central story. Key governance questions: why did so many new states become authoritarian? How did colonial administrative structures (or their absence) shape post-independence governance?

**CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions):** [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]], Pan-Arabism, and the Non-Aligned Movement all represent new transnational identities built in opposition to colonial cultural hierarchies. Religion (Islam, Hinduism) became a force in shaping new states — India-Pakistan partition, Algerian Civil War, Nigerian sectarianism.

**SIO (Social Interactions and Organization):** Independence movements mobilized mass participation across class lines. Post-independence, the challenge was turning colonial subjects — defined by ethnic, religious, or tribal identity — into citizens of new national states.

## Causation: Why Did Decolonization Happen?

**Causes of decolonization** operate at multiple levels — AP prompts often ask you to identify and explain them:

*Long-term causes:*
- European Enlightenment ideals of liberty and self-determination were weaponized by colonial subjects trained in European universities
- [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] as a political ideology spread globally through education and print culture
- Rising educated elite in colonies who organized mass movements

*Medium-term causes:*
- WWI: colonial troops fought for European empires; Wilson's Fourteen Points promised self-determination; independence movements intensified
- Interwar period: organized nationalist parties (Congress, Muslim League, ANC) developed mass followings

*Immediate causes:*
- WWII devastated European economies and military capacity
- Japanese victories destroyed the myth of white invincibility (Singapore fell 1942)
- [[Concept Notes/Atlantic Charter\|Atlantic Charter]] (1941) — Churchill and Roosevelt proclaimed the right of all peoples to choose their government; colonial subjects took this literally
- UN Charter (1945) — enshrined self-determination as a principle; gave independence movements a diplomatic forum
- Cold War — both US and USSR, for different reasons, publicly opposed European colonialism

**Effects of decolonization** are equally testable:
- 50+ new states created, mostly in Africa and Asia
- Ethnic and religious conflicts (partition violence, Biafra, Nigeria)
- Military coups and one-party states across Africa and Asia
- Cold War proxy wars in newly independent states (Vietnam, Angola, Congo)
- Economic dependency on former colonial powers (neo-colonialism)
- New international organizations: OAU, Non-Aligned Movement

> [!exam-tip]
> LEQ prompts on decolonization often ask you to evaluate the **relative significance of causes**. A strong answer will argue *which* cause was most important and *why*, not just list causes. A common argument: WWII was the single most important cause because it simultaneously discredited colonial moral authority, exhausted European economies, and produced institutional frameworks (UN) that empowered independence movements.

## Comparison: Paths to Independence

The Think As a Historian section (AMSCO p. 588) explicitly asks students to compare decolonization in two countries where **religion** played a prominent role. Here are the key comparisons:

**Negotiated vs. Armed Independence:**

| | Negotiated | Armed |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | India, Ghana, French West Africa, Nigeria | Algeria, Vietnam, Angola |
| Colonial power's response | Accepted independence (often after exhaustion) | Military resistance; brutal suppression |
| Outcome | Relatively smoother transition | Prolonged war; deeper post-independence trauma |
| Pattern | British colonies more likely to negotiate | French colonies more likely to fight |

> [!tip]
> The British-French contrast is worth memorizing: Britain generally negotiated independence (India, Ghana, Nigeria); France fought (Indochina, Algeria). Portugal fought longest of all (Angola, Mozambique — until 1975). Why? France and Portugal linked colonial empire more closely to national identity and prestige.

**Religion's Role in Decolonization:**

*India/Pakistan:* Religious identity (Hindu vs. Muslim) determined the shape of independence. The Muslim League's demand for Pakistan showed that religious community could override shared anti-colonial struggle. Partition produced mass violence — the dark underside of religious nationalism.

*Algeria:* The FLN initially presented a secular nationalist movement, but religion surfaced later. The Algerian Civil War (1991–2002) pitted the secular military-backed government against Islamist groups — the deferred conflict between secular nationalism and religious identity.

*Egypt:* Nasser blended Islam and socialism in his domestic policy but governed as a secular authoritarian. His Pan-Arabism used cultural/linguistic rather than strictly religious identity, though both overlapped.

*Nigeria:* Post-independence ethnic and religious tensions (Christian south vs. Muslim north) drove the Biafran Civil War and continue to shape Nigerian politics.

## Continuity and Change Over Time

**What changed:** European colonial rule ended; new sovereign states emerged; global political map transformed; self-determination became the norm; racial hierarchies embedded in colonialism officially rejected.

**What continued:** Economic dependency on former colonizers (neo-colonialism); ethnic and religious conflicts suppressed under colonial rule resurfaced; authoritarian governance — just under new management; Cold War superpowers replaced European empires as external forces shaping internal politics.

> [!warning]
> Don't frame decolonization as a clean break. The AP rewards students who can identify **continuities**: colonial borders remained; economic structures remained extractive; authoritarian governance continued under new flags. Nkrumah's Ghana or Nasser's Egypt were not dramatically more free for ordinary people than colonial rule had been.

## Historical Perspectives: Historiography

**Nationalist perspective:** Independence movements were heroic mass struggles for freedom against illegitimate colonial oppression. Leaders like Gandhi, Nkrumah, and Ho Chi Minh are celebrated as liberators. Independence itself is the triumph.

**Post-colonial critique:** Decolonization was incomplete. Economic neo-colonialism replaced political colonialism. New elites — often educated in colonial institutions — replicated colonial power structures while adopting nationalist rhetoric. Franz Fanon (*The Wretched of the Earth*, 1961) argued that true decolonization required revolutionary transformation of economic and psychological structures, not just flag independence.

**Cold War lens:** Decolonization cannot be separated from superpower rivalry. The US and USSR both saw newly independent states as battlegrounds for influence. Cold War intervention (CIA in Congo, Soviet support for Vietnam, Cuban troops in Angola) prolonged conflicts and distorted political outcomes.

**Comparative view:** Decolonization produced radically different outcomes depending on colonial power, pre-colonial political structures, Cold War positioning, and natural resources. Ghana's relative stability vs. Congo's collapse; India's democracy vs. Pakistan's repeated military coups — these variations demand explanation, not a single narrative.
