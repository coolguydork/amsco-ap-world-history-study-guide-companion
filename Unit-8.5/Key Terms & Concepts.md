# Key Terms & Concepts

*13 terms — all resolved. AMSCO pp. 579–588.*

---

**Kwame Nkrumah**
First president of Ghana (1960–1966); led Ghana to independence from Britain in 1957, the first sub-Saharan African nation to achieve independence. Advocated [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]] and founded the Organization of African Unity (OAU). Overclaimed dictatorial powers in 1966 and was overthrown in a military coup — a cautionary example of post-independence authoritarian drift.

**Charles de Gaulle**
Leader of the Free French during WWII and President of France (1959–1969). Granted Algeria independence after the Algerian War (1954–1962) through a referendum, bypassing the French National Assembly. Represents France's painful acceptance that empire was no longer sustainable. Also withdrew France from [[Concept Notes/NATO\|NATO]]'s integrated command in 1966, asserting French national independence from both superpowers.

**Ho Chi Minh**
Vietnamese communist leader who led resistance against French colonialism and then against US intervention during the Vietnam War. Founded the Democratic Republic of Vietnam (North Vietnam) and inspired revolutionary movements across Southeast Asia. His nationalism and communism were inseparable — he saw liberation from colonialism and liberation from capitalism as the same struggle. Died in 1969, six years before North Vietnam's final victory.

**Gamal Abdel Nasser**
President of Egypt (1954–1970); nationalized the Suez Canal in 1956, triggering a major international crisis and humiliating Britain and France when the US and USSR both forced their withdrawal. Champion of Pan-Arabism and the Non-Aligned Movement. His land reform, nationalization programs, and canal takeover made him the defining symbol of post-colonial assertiveness in the Arab world.

**one-party state**
Political system in which a single party holds exclusive power, banning or suppressing all opposition. Common outcome in newly decolonized African and Asian states, often justified as necessary for national unity and rapid development. Ghana under Nkrumah, Algeria under the FLN, and Vietnam under the Communist Party all followed this pattern. Frequently led to authoritarianism despite decolonization rhetoric of freedom and [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]].

> [!warning]
> Students sometimes assume decolonization automatically meant democracy. It didn't. Most new states became one-party states or military dictatorships within a few years of independence. The AP asks why — emphasize ethnic division, Cold War pressure, and lack of democratic institutions under colonialism.

**Algerian War for Independence**
1954–1962 armed conflict between Algerian nationalists (FLN — National Liberation Front) and France. One of the most brutal decolonization conflicts: France sent 500,000 troops, and hundreds of thousands of Algerians died; French historian Pierre Vidal-Naquet documented "hundreds of thousands of instances of torture" by French forces. Ended when de Gaulle accepted that colonial rule was untenable and held a referendum on independence. The war caused sharp divisions inside France itself.

**Algerian Civil War**
1991–2002 internal conflict in Algeria between the military-backed government and Islamist groups (Islamic Salvation Front/GIA). Erupted after the government cancelled elections the Islamists were winning. An estimated 100,000–200,000 died. Demonstrates that post-colonial authoritarian states faced new internal challenges — particularly from religious movements that rejected secular nationalist governments inherited from the independence era.

**Suez Crisis**
1956 international crisis triggered when Egypt's Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal. Britain, France, and Israel invaded Egypt; the US and Soviet Union, for different reasons, opposed the invasion and used the UN to force withdrawal. A landmark event demonstrating the decline of European imperial power — Britain and France were humiliated by their own Cold War ally. Egypt emerged with sovereignty over the canal, and Nasser became a hero of anti-colonial nationalism.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Suez Crisis is a perfect example of **Cold War dynamics reshaping decolonization**. The US opposed Britain and France to avoid pushing Arab states toward the USSR — not out of anti-colonial principle. This tension between US Cold War interests and its stated support for self-determination is a recurring AP theme.

**Biafran Civil War**
1967–1970 Nigerian civil war in which the Igbo-dominated southeastern region declared independence as Biafra. Nigeria's federal forces defeated Biafra; an estimated 1–3 million died, many from famine and starvation. Demonstrates the violence of post-colonial ethnic conflicts in Africa — the root cause was that colonial-era borders enclosed incompatible ethnic and religious groups (Christian Igbo, Islamic Hausa-Fulani) without any mechanism for power-sharing.

**Quiet Revolution**
1960s political and social transformation in Quebec, Canada: secularization, modernization, and the rise of Quebec nationalism under the Liberal Party. Fueled demands for independence from Canada; separatist groups turned to terrorism (bombings beginning in 1963) and later to referenda (1980, 1995 — both narrowly failed). Demonstrates that independence movements were not limited to the Global South — identity-based resistance to perceived cultural domination occurred even in wealthy Western nations.

**Muslim League**
Political organization founded in 1906 in British India; advocated for a separate Muslim state. Led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, it successfully lobbied for the partition of British India into India (Hindu majority) and Pakistan (Muslim majority) in 1947. Partition triggered massive communal violence — an estimated 200,000–2 million killed and 10–20 million displaced in one of history's largest refugee crises. Demonstrates that independence movements were often divided by internal religious and cultural tensions.

**Organization of African Unity (OAU)**
Pan-African organization founded in 1963 by Nkrumah and other African leaders; promoted African unity, anti-colonialism, and non-interference in member states' internal affairs. Frequently criticized for protecting authoritarian governments behind the non-interference principle. Replaced by the African Union (AU) in 2002, which adopted a more interventionist stance on human rights. Connects to [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]].

**Viet Cong**
South Vietnamese communist guerrillas (officially the National Liberation Front) who fought against the US-backed South Vietnamese government during the Vietnam War. Used guerrilla tactics and the Ho Chi Minh Trail supply network. Integral to North Vietnam's strategy for reunifying the country. Their ability to withstand vastly superior US firepower reflects how nationalist motivation — fighting for liberation — can offset material disadvantage.
