# People, States & Movements

*AMSCO pp. 579–588*

## Key Leaders

**[[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]]** (1869–1948)
Led the Indian independence movement through non-violent resistance (satyagraha) and mass civil disobedience. Leader of the Indian National Congress from the 1920s. His tactics — boycotts, hunger strikes, the Salt March — built a mass movement that Britain ultimately could not suppress without undermining its own moral standing. Opposed partition but was unable to prevent it. Assassinated by a Hindu nationalist in January 1948, five months after independence. Gandhi's methods influenced the American civil rights movement and anti-apartheid struggle, making him one of the most globally consequential figures of the 20th century.

> [!exam-tip]
> Gandhi is the canonical example of **non-violent resistance** as a decolonization strategy. Contrast him with Ho Chi Minh (armed struggle) for comparison questions. Both succeeded, but through opposite means.

**Jawaharlal Nehru** (1889–1964)
First Prime Minister of independent India (1947–1964). Represented secular, democratic socialism — a deliberate choice to keep India unified across religious lines. Nehru was a leading figure in the Non-Aligned Movement, arguing India should not be forced to choose sides in the Cold War. His domestic policies included industrialization and state planning (Five-Year Plans) modeled loosely on Soviet development without Soviet authoritarianism.

**Muhammad Ali Jinnah** (1876–1948)
Leader of the Muslim League and the founder of Pakistan. Argued that Muslims could not trust a Hindu-majority independent India to protect their rights and pushed for a separate Muslim homeland. His vision produced Pakistan (including East Pakistan, later Bangladesh). Died in September 1948, just over a year after Pakistan's founding, leaving the new state without its founding leader.

**Kwame Nkrumah** (1909–1972)
See [[Unit-8.5/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. Led Ghana to independence in 1957; founded the OAU. Represents both the promise of African nationalism and its tendency toward authoritarian drift. Built on nationalist traditions he absorbed studying in the United States and Britain — national narratives, founding myths, flags, currency, monuments. Overthrown 1966.

**Ho Chi Minh** (1890–1969)
See [[Unit-8.5/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. Led Vietnam's independence movement against France (1945–1954) and the war against US-backed South Vietnam. His combination of nationalism and communism made him difficult for the US to counter — Vietnamese fighters saw themselves as fighting for liberation, not ideology. The Viet Cong's guerrilla tactics frustrated US military superiority.

**Gamal Abdel Nasser** (1918–1970)
See [[Unit-8.5/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. Overthrew Egypt's king in 1952, became president in 1954. Nationalized the Suez Canal in 1956, triggering an international crisis that ended in Egypt's favor when the US and USSR forced a British-French-Israeli withdrawal. Nasser's defiance made him a hero of anti-colonial nationalism across the Arab world. Promoted Pan-Arabism — the idea that Arab peoples shared cultural and political identity that should override national borders.

**Ahmed Ben Bella** (1918–2012)
Led the FLN (National Liberation Front) during the Algerian War for Independence and became Algeria's first president (1963–1965). Overthrown in a military coup by his former ally Houari Boumédiène in 1965 — the first of several coups that would mark Algeria's post-independence instability.

**Patrice Lumumba** (1925–1961)
First Prime Minister of the independent Democratic Republic of Congo (1960). His assassination in January 1961, with alleged CIA and Belgian involvement, became a symbol of how Cold War powers undermined African [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]] by eliminating leaders they deemed too independent or too left-leaning. Not in AMSCO 8.5 directly, but frequently appears in AP exam sources on decolonization.

**Charles de Gaulle** (1890–1970)
See [[Unit-8.5/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. His willingness to accept Algerian independence despite fierce opposition from French settlers and military hardliners — and his use of a direct referendum to bypass the National Assembly — showed both political courage and pragmatic recognition that empire was unsustainable.

**Kofi Annan** (1938–2018)
Ghanaian diplomat who became UN Secretary-General (1997–2006). Cited in AMSCO as a point of Ghanaian national pride — the contrast between Ghana's functioning democratic transfer of power (1992 constitution) and Algeria's continued instability illustrates divergent post-independence trajectories.

## Key States & Entities

**India / Pakistan (1947)**
Partition created two independent states simultaneously: secular democratic India and the Islamic Republic of Pakistan (West + East Pakistan). The partition was accompanied by catastrophic communal violence as Hindus and Muslims fled in opposite directions — one of history's largest refugee crises. The India-Pakistan rivalry over Kashmir has persisted to the present.

**Ghana (1957)**
First sub-Saharan African country to achieve independence in the 20th century. Gold Coast + British Togoland merged to form Ghana, named after the historic West African empire. Its independence inspired other African nationalist movements. By 1960 ("Year of Africa"), 17 African nations gained independence.

**Algeria (1962)**
France considered Algeria part of metropolitan France, not merely a colony, because of the large settler population (pieds-noirs). This made it far harder to negotiate independence compared to sub-Saharan colonies, resulting in a brutal 8-year war. Post-independence Algeria became a single-party socialist state under the FLN that lasted decades.

**Nigeria (1960)**
Most populous country in Africa. Colonial borders enclosed over 250 ethnic and linguistic groups. The Biafran Civil War (1967–1970) tested whether the colonial-era federal structure could hold. Nigeria survived, but only after enormous casualties. The Niger Delta oil wealth remained a flashpoint for regional grievances.

## Key Movements

**Indian National Congress**
See [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]]. The organizational engine of Indian independence, dating to 1885. Under Gandhi, it shifted to mass civil disobedience.

**Muslim League**
See [[Unit-8.5/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]]. The rival to the Congress, advocating a separate Muslim state.

**FLN (Front de Libération Nationale / National Liberation Front)**
Led the Algerian War for Independence and became the ruling party of independent Algeria. Used guerrilla warfare against French forces. Continued as the dominant political force in Algeria for decades after independence, maintaining a single-party socialist state.

**Viet Cong / National Liberation Front**
See [[Unit-8.5/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]]. South Vietnamese communist guerrillas aligned with Ho Chi Minh's North Vietnam.

**Pan-Africanism**
See [[Concept Notes/Pan-Africanism\|Pan-Africanism]]. A movement promoting African cultural unity and political solidarity, championed by Nkrumah. In the 20th century, it meant rejecting colonial interference and building continental institutions like the OAU.

**Pan-Arabism**
The idea that Arab peoples share a common identity that transcends national borders, championed by Nasser. Sought to unify Arab states politically and culturally. The United Arab Republic (Egypt + Syria, 1958–1961) was Pan-Arabism's most ambitious experiment; it collapsed due to Syrian resentment of Egyptian dominance.

**Non-Aligned Movement**
Coalition of newly independent states (led by Nehru, Nasser, and Yugoslavia's Tito) that refused to align formally with either the US or Soviet bloc during the Cold War. Not the same as neutrality — Non-Aligned states actively played superpowers against each other to maximize aid and autonomy. A key expression of post-colonial sovereignty.

> [!tip]
> The Non-Aligned Movement, Pan-Africanism, and Pan-Arabism all represent newly independent states **asserting collective identity** beyond colonial boundaries. All three reflect CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions) and GOV themes for AP purposes.
