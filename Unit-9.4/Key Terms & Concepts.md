# Key Terms & Concepts

**Unit 9.4 — Economics in the Global Age | AMSCO pp. 657–666**

## Economy: World Trade

**economic liberalization**
The reduction of government intervention in the economy — lowering tariffs, privatizing state industries, deregulating markets — to encourage freer trade and foreign investment. Adopted widely from the 1970s onward under neoliberal influence. Central AP argument: did liberalization benefit developing nations or entrench inequality? Chile and China are the two key case studies.

**free market**
An economic system driven by supply and demand with minimal government regulation or intervention. Championed by Reagan and Thatcher in the 1980s; the ideological core of neoliberal globalization. Contrast on AP with command economies (Soviet Union) and mixed welfare states (post-WWII Europe).

**protective tariff**
A tax on imported goods designed to make them more expensive than domestically produced goods, shielding local industries from foreign competition. World average tariffs were ~40% before GATT; their systematic reduction to under 5% by the 1990s is the structural foundation of modern globalization.

**General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)**
A multilateral trade treaty (1947) that progressively reduced tariffs and trade barriers through successive negotiating rounds — global average tariffs fell from ~40% to under 5% by the 1990s. Replaced by the WTO in 1995. Key AP evidence for post-WWII economic liberalization driven by U.S. hegemony.

**World Trade Organization (WTO)**
International organization (est. 1995, replacing GATT) that sets and enforces rules for global trade, resolves trade disputes between member states, and continues to negotiate tariff reductions. Governs over 90% of international trade. A major target of anti-globalization protests for favoring wealthy nations and MNCs over developing countries.

**NAFTA**
The North American Free Trade Agreement (1994), eliminating most tariffs among the U.S., Canada, and Mexico. Accelerated **maquiladora** growth in Mexico and is the AP's go-to example of regional free-trade blocs. Critics: exported U.S. jobs, exploited Mexican workers. Supporters: raised living standards in Mexico, lowered consumer prices.

**maquiladora**
Foreign-owned assembly factories in Mexico (especially near the U.S. border) that import duty-free materials, manufacture goods using low-wage Mexican labor, and export the finished product. A direct result of NAFTA. Illustrates globalization's double-edged nature: foreign investment and jobs vs. labor exploitation and weak environmental enforcement.

**Mercosur**
A South American regional trade bloc founded in 1991 by Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay to promote free trade and economic integration. AP example of regional economic cooperation, comparable to the EU and ASEAN as alternatives to or complements of global institutions like the WTO.

**Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN)**
Regional intergovernmental organization founded in 1967 (Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, Thailand — later expanded) to promote political stability, economic cooperation, and trade in Southeast Asia. AP example of regional integration; connects to Vietnam and Bangladesh as manufacturing hubs.

## Economy: Globalization

**multinational corporation**
A business incorporated in one country but producing or selling goods and services in multiple others. The joint-stock companies of the Commercial Revolution ([[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]], Dutch East India Company — [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview\|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]]) were the earliest MNCs. Today's MNCs employ knowledge workers in wealthy countries and low-wage manufacturers abroad, selling to global markets. Key AP continuity.

**knowledge economy**
A post-industrial economy driven by the production, distribution, and application of knowledge and information rather than physical goods. Characteristic of late-20th-century developed societies. Finland, Silicon Valley, and India's IT sector are the defining examples. Contrast with manufacturing economies (Bangladesh, Vietnam, Mexico).

**Asian Tigers**
Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan — four economies that achieved rapid export-led industrialization from the 1960s–1990s by combining state investment in education and infrastructure with integration into global markets. Modeled partly on Japan. The Asian Tigers' success lifted hundreds of millions from poverty and is AP evidence that globalization can benefit developing nations.

**Microsoft**
U.S.-based multinational technology corporation (founded 1975) central to the knowledge economy through software development. Along with Google, invested in India's tech sector. AP example of a Western knowledge-economy MNC driving globalization through outsourcing and global investment.

**Google**
U.S.-based multinational technology corporation (founded 1998) central to the knowledge economy through internet search, digital advertising, and mobile technology. AP example of a Western MNC investing in India's IT sector; also notable for the Saathis program in India, which brought internet access to rural women — a microcosm of globalization's potential to empower and its corporate profit motive.

**Mahindra & Mahindra**
An India-based multinational conglomerate (founded 1945) producing vehicles, farm equipment, military vehicles, and energy products with global operations (South Korea, China, Australia, U.S., South Africa). AP example of a non-Western MNC that became a major global player through India's economic liberalization. Contrasted with Nestlé as a relatively ethical, socially responsible MNC.

**Nestlé**
Swiss-based multinational food corporation (world's largest by revenue) that exemplifies MNC controversy — sourcing cocoa from farms using child labor, treating water as a commodity — alongside corporate social responsibility programs. Contrasted with Mahindra & Mahindra. Standard AP example in arguments about globalization's uneven costs and benefits for developing-world communities.

## Government: Neoliberal Leaders

**Margaret Thatcher**
British Prime Minister (1979–1990) who implemented Thatcherism: privatization of state industries, deregulation, dismantling of labor unions, and welfare-state cuts. Alongside Reagan, a defining architect of the neoliberal turn that reshaped the global economy in the 1980s. Her policies increased GDP but also increased income inequality.

**Deng Xiaoping**
Chinese paramount leader (effectively from 1978) who launched the Four Modernizations: replacing communes with peasant-leased plots, reopening the Shanghai stock market, creating Special Economic Zones, and allowing limited private enterprise — while maintaining Communist Party political control. "Let some people get rich first." His reforms fueled explosive growth but culminated in the Tiananmen Square crackdown (1989).

**Augusto Pinochet**
Chilean military dictator (1973–1990) who seized power in a U.S.-backed coup against the elected socialist government of Salvador Allende. Implemented radical free-market reforms via the "Chicago Boys" — privatization, deregulation, austerity. An early and influential (if brutal) test case for neoliberal economic policy in Latin America. Indicted for human rights abuses; died before conviction (2006).

## Society: China

**Tiananmen Square**
The central plaza in Beijing where, in June 1989, a massive student-led pro-democracy movement was violently suppressed by the Chinese military using guns and tanks, killing hundreds to thousands. Illustrates the defining tension of Deng Xiaoping's China: economic liberalization without political liberalization. The Communist Party accepted free markets; it would not accept free speech.
