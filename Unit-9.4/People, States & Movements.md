# People, States & Movements

**Unit 9.4 — Economics in the Global Age | AMSCO pp. 657–666**

---

## Key Leaders

**Ronald Reagan** (U.S. President, 1981–1989)
Championed free-market capitalism: tax cuts, deregulation, welfare cuts, anti-union policies (famously fired striking air traffic controllers in 1981). "Reaganomics" became the template for neoliberal policy globally. Reagan's push for economic liberalization was part of the same ideological package as his Cold War confrontationalism — both aimed at weakening state-centric economic models.

> [!tip]
> Reagan's economic policies and his Cold War strategy are two sides of the same coin on the AP exam. Both aimed at demonstrating capitalism's superiority over communism.

**Margaret Thatcher** (British PM, 1979–1990)
"The Iron Lady" implemented Thatcherism — privatizing British Telecom, British Gas, and British Steel; breaking the coal miners' union (1984–85); cutting the welfare state. Her policies paralleled Reagan's and gave neoliberalism a transatlantic legitimacy. Increased inequality alongside GDP growth.

**Deng Xiaoping** (China's paramount leader, 1978–1992)
Successor to Mao Zedong's revolutionary generation, Deng pragmatically dismantled key Maoist economic structures without abandoning Communist Party rule. "It doesn't matter if the cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice." Opened China to foreign direct investment through Special Economic Zones (Shenzhen being the most famous). His reforms connected China to global supply chains, eventually making China the world's manufacturing hub. The Tiananmen crackdown (1989) shows the hard limit of his liberalism — economic freedom yes, political freedom never.

**Augusto Pinochet** (Chilean dictator, 1973–1990)
The AP's clearest example of economic liberalization by authoritarian means. Overthrew the democratically elected socialist Salvador Allende with CIA support. Brought in the "Chicago Boys" (economists trained under Milton Friedman) to implement free-market reforms. The economy eventually stabilized but poverty went unaddressed under pure neoliberalism; only later democratic governments achieved genuine poverty reduction by combining free trade with social programs. Pinochet was indicted for murder, kidnapping, and torture; he died in 2006 before conviction.

**Milton Friedman** (American economist, University of Chicago)
The intellectual architect of the neoliberal program. His arguments for monetarism, free markets, and minimal government intervention influenced Thatcher, Reagan, Pinochet's Chicago Boys, and the IMF's structural adjustment programs. Not a political figure himself, but his ideas shaped the global economic order more than almost any other individual.

## Key States & Economic Zones

**Chile**
The first major test case of neoliberal economic reform (1973–). Under Pinochet, Chile privatized state industries and deregulated markets. After Pinochet's ouster, subsequent democratic governments combined free trade with targeted social spending, reducing poverty substantially. By the 2000s, Chile had one of the highest per capita incomes in Latin America. AP lesson: free markets alone don't reduce poverty; state investment in social programs is also necessary.

**China**
Under Deng Xiaoping, China transitioned from a command economy toward "socialism with Chinese characteristics" — a hybrid of state ownership and market mechanisms. Special Economic Zones attracted massive foreign direct investment, and China's cheap labor became the backbone of global manufacturing supply chains. China's growth lifted hundreds of millions from poverty. The Tiananmen crackdown (1989) prevented the political liberalization that some expected to accompany economic reform.

**Japan**
Post-WWII Japan deployed quasi-mercantilist policies: export subsidies, protective tariffs, rigorous education, and coordinated government-industry partnerships. It became the world's second-largest economy by the 1980s, then diversified into a knowledge economy (banking, finance, IT). Japan's success inspired the Asian Tigers model.

**Asian Tigers: Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan**
Each followed variants of Japan's export-led development model. Key shared features: strong government direction of the economy, high investment in education, export-oriented manufacturing, and integration into global trade networks. Their success is the AP's strongest argument that globalization, when well-managed, can produce rapid development. By the 2000s, all four had become high-income economies.

**India**
Opened its markets in the early 1990s after decades of state-directed economic planning (the "License Raj"). With its educated, English-speaking workforce, India became a global software and IT services hub. Investments from Microsoft, Google, and other MNCs helped grow India's middle class to an estimated 350 million by 2014 — the largest in the world. India exemplifies how a developing nation can leverage globalization's knowledge economy rather than being reduced to low-wage manufacturing.

**Finland**
Illustrates how a small nation can pivot from manufacturing to a knowledge economy. After the Soviet collapse destroyed a major market for Finnish goods (1990s), Finland invested in education, R&D, and technology infrastructure. It became a global leader in mobile phones (Nokia) and software, outsourcing hardware production to lower-wage countries while retaining high-value design and innovation work domestically.

**Vietnam and Bangladesh**
Both became major low-wage manufacturing hubs (garments, electronics) as MNCs shifted production from China to even cheaper labor markets. Clothing accounts for ~80% of Bangladesh's exports. Workers in both countries have staged strikes protesting low wages and dangerous conditions. These states represent the newest tier of global manufacturing — benefiting from foreign investment while struggling with exploitation.

**Mexico (maquiladora zones)**
NAFTA (1994) transformed northern Mexico's border regions into dense maquiladora manufacturing zones — foreign-owned factories producing goods for U.S. and Canadian markets using low-wage Mexican labor. Debated outcome: created millions of jobs but at low wages, weak environmental standards, and with harsh conditions (especially for young women workers). U.S. unions blamed NAFTA for exporting American manufacturing jobs.

## Key Movements & Organizations

**Neoliberalism / Washington Consensus**
The dominant economic ideology of the late 20th century: free markets, privatization, deregulation, fiscal austerity, and free trade. Promoted globally by the IMF and World Bank through "structural adjustment programs" (loans conditional on adopting free-market policies). Critics argue it entrenched inequality and undermined developing nations' ability to protect nascent industries.

**Anti-Globalization Movement**
Organized opposition to the WTO, IMF, and multinational corporations — coalesced visibly in the 1999 Seattle WTO protests. Argued that globalization's rules favored wealthy nations and corporations, suppressed labor rights and environmental standards, and widened inequality. A direct predecessor to [[Unit-9.7/Resistance to Globalization Overview|Topic 9.7]].

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the arguments on both sides for AP free-response questions. Pro-globalization: lifted millions from poverty, transferred technology, created consumer goods. Anti-globalization: exploits low-wage workers, undermines domestic industries, enriches corporations at expense of communities.
