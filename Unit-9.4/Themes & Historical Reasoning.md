# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Unit 9.4 — Economics in the Global Age | AMSCO pp. 657–666**

**AP Themes:** ECN (Economy), GOV (Governance), TEC (Technology)
**AP Skills:** Causation, Continuity and Change Over Time

---

## ECN — Economic Systems

The defining economic change of the late 20th century was the global spread of market-oriented economic systems at the expense of command economies and state-directed development models. This shift had multiple causes: the ideological influence of neoliberalism (Reagan, Thatcher, Chicago School economists), the failure of Soviet-style command economies, and the practical success of export-led growth in East Asia.

The structure of the global economy also changed fundamentally. For much of the 19th–mid 20th centuries, the global economy was organized around the export of raw materials from colonies/periphery to industrial metropoles. By the late 20th century, manufacturing itself had relocated to the global periphery (Mexico, Bangladesh, Vietnam), while wealthy nations retained high-value knowledge work and financial services. This represents both continuity (persistent inequality between wealthy and developing nations) and change (the nature of what is produced where).

The GATT → WTO transition marks the institutionalization of free trade as global law. Tariffs fell from ~40% to ~5% over 50 years — a structural change that made global supply chains economically rational for the first time.

> [!exam-tip]
> For ECN arguments: the AP loves the tension between "globalization lifted millions from poverty" (Asia Tigers, India, China) vs. "globalization exploited cheap labor and widened inequality" (maquiladoras, Bangladesh garment workers, structural adjustment in Africa). Know specific examples for BOTH sides.

## GOV — Governance and Political Structures

The neoliberal turn represents a significant shift in the role of government in economic life. Where post-WWII governments in most of the world (including capitalist ones) accepted substantial state management of economies (welfare states in Europe, import-substitution industrialization in Latin America, state-owned enterprises in developing nations), the 1980s brought a policy reversal: privatization, deregulation, and structural adjustment.

The WTO and IMF represent a new form of supranational economic governance — international institutions with the power to impose trade rules on member states or condition loans on policy changes. Critics see this as a constraint on national sovereignty; supporters see it as the enforcement mechanism of a rules-based trading order that benefits everyone.

China's case is the most striking governance story: a single-party communist state successfully harnessing market mechanisms while refusing political liberalization. The Tiananmen crackdown (1989) definitively separated economic from political freedom in China's developmental model.

Pinochet's Chile shows the extreme: free-market economic policy imposed by authoritarian repression. The lesson democracies drew: economic liberalization is more politically sustainable when accompanied by social safety nets.

## TEC — Technology and Innovation

Technology is both a cause and a product of economic globalization in this period. The digital revolution (internet, telecommunications, computerization) made global supply chains logistically manageable, enabled instantaneous financial transactions, and created the knowledge economy itself.

Finland and Silicon Valley exemplify how nations and regions that invested in education and technological infrastructure could capture the high-value end of the global economy. India's IT boom shows how digital technology allowed a developing nation to skip the manufacturing stage and compete directly in knowledge-intensive services.

The Saathis program (Google in India) illustrates a further dimension: technology companies using connectivity initiatives in developing markets to simultaneously generate goodwill, expand their user base, and create future customers — blurring the line between corporate self-interest and genuine development.

> [!tip]
> TEC-ECN connection: Technology didn't just enable globalization — it restructured which countries benefited. Nations that built knowledge economies (Finland, South Korea, Taiwan, India) captured high-value work. Nations that remained low-wage manufacturers (Bangladesh, Vietnam, early Mexico) remained dependent on foreign capital and vulnerable to exploitation.

## Historical Reasoning: Causation

**Causes of accelerated economic globalization (post-1970s):**

1. **End of Cold War** — dissolved barriers between capitalist and formerly communist/nonaligned markets
2. **Neoliberal ideology** — Reagan/Thatcher framework legitimized deregulation and privatization globally
3. **Digital revolution** — made global supply chains logistically and financially viable
4. **GATT/WTO** — reduced tariffs from ~40% to ~5%, making international trade economically rational
5. **IMF structural adjustment programs** — imposed free-market conditions on developing nations as loan requirements

**Effects of accelerated economic globalization:**

- Hundreds of millions lifted from poverty (China, India, Asian Tigers)
- Manufacturing relocated to low-wage regions (Mexico, Bangladesh, Vietnam)
- Knowledge economies emerged in wealthy nations (Finland, Silicon Valley, India's IT sector)
- Labor exploitation and environmental degradation in export manufacturing zones
- Growth of a global middle class alongside persistent inequality between and within nations
- Anti-globalization backlash and political resistance [[Unit-9.7/Resistance to Globalization Overview|Resistance to Globalization Overview]]

## Historical Reasoning: Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

**Continuity:**
- Multinational corporations have operated globally since the 16th century (East India Companies → today's MNCs). The profit motive, labor exploitation, and resource extraction are continuous.
- Free trade ideology has recurred since Adam Smith (18th century) and the Corn Laws debates (19th century).
- Wealthy, industrialized nations have consistently benefited more from trade than developing ones — a pattern from the age of imperialism through late-20th-century globalization.
- Manufacturing supply chains have always sought the cheapest available labor — from enslaved Africans to 19th-century factory workers to 21st-century garment workers in Bangladesh.

**Change:**
- Scale and speed: digital technology made globalization qualitatively different from anything before
- Tariffs fell from ~40% to ~5% globally — a structural change with no precedent
- China and India integrated as major economic powers, not just suppliers of cheap labor
- Knowledge economy emerged as distinct from industrial economy
- New supranational institutions (WTO, IMF, World Bank) codified global economic rules

## Think as a Historian: Source Analysis (AMSCO p. 666)

Two sources on free trade and globalization are presented for comparison:

**Bernie Sanders (2016, political debate):** Argues NAFTA and free trade agreements serve multinational corporations at the expense of American and Vietnamese workers. Purpose: campaign rhetoric, aimed at Democratic primary voters. Limitation: advocacy context means it presents the strongest anti-NAFTA case, not a balanced analysis.

**Dr. Timothy Hogan (2011, academic report):** Acknowledges that some U.S. workers at globalized firms benefited while "large numbers" lost jobs. Purpose: academic analysis for a state policy council. More balanced, but still focused on Arizona/U.S. perspective, not the global picture.

Key AP skill: both sources agree on the basic fact (many U.S. jobs disappeared due to globalization) but differ in purpose and framing. Sanders uses this to condemn trade agreements; Hogan uses it to analyze the knowledge economy's structure. Neither source captures the perspective of Vietnamese or Mexican workers.

> [!warning]
> When analyzing sources about globalization on the AP exam, always ask: whose perspective is missing? Sources from wealthy-nation politicians or economists rarely capture how workers in developing nations experience globalization — which may be as beneficial (new jobs, rising wages) or exploitative depending on context.
