## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: Vivienne Walt, "Google's Hopes and Dreams in India," Fortune, February 20, 2019*

The passage describes Google's Saathis program in India — recruiting ~60,000 women to train other women in rural villages to use the internet and smartphones, in partnership with Tata Trusts.

---

**Question 1.** Which of the following describes a likely benefit to Google of its efforts in India?

(A) Google is looking to India's women as a source of low-cost labor.
(B) Google is creating potential future customers by introducing technology.
(C) Google is looking to avoid U.S. taxes by establishing a presence in India.
(D) Google is looking for ways to extract natural resources from India for its finished products.

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Saathis program teaches women to use smartphones and the internet — creating a new user base for Google's services. This is Google's core business model: expand internet access, expand the pool of potential Google users and advertisers. Options A (labor source), C (tax avoidance), and D (resource extraction) don't fit Google's digital, service-based business model.

---

**Question 2.** Which best states a claim about globalization that this article could be used to support?

(A) Companies both encourage and benefit from economic growth.
(B) The pay difference among workers around the world doing similar jobs will grow larger over time.
(C) Google's relationship with India's middle class is mainly charitable.
(D) One limit to economic liberalism is that companies fail to transfer skills to workers around the world.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The Saathis program shows Google encouraging economic activity (women starting cottage industries via YouTube tutorials) while expanding its own user base and revenue potential. Option B isn't supported by the article. Option C is wrong — Google has clear profit motives. Option D is the opposite of what the article shows (skills are being transferred).

---

**Question 3.** In what way have Google and YouTube benefited the women in the Saathis program economically?

(A) It pays them a $40 per month stipend.
(B) It saves them money through online bill paying.
(C) It exposes them to ideas they might use to make money.
(D) It gives women more freedom to work outside the home.

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> The passage gives the example of Parveen Begum, who learned to decorate bangles using YouTube tutorials and now sells them to local clients. This is economic benefit through skill and idea transfer, not through a stipend (that's Tata Trusts paying Saathis, not trainees), bill paying, or working outside the home (she works from home).

## Short-Answer Questions

### SAQ 1 — Image Analysis (LBJ in Vietnamese textile mill, 1961)

*U.S. Vice President Lyndon B. Johnson visits a Vietnamese textile mill with workers in front of "Texas Cotton U.S.A." banner, 1961.*

**(A) Identify ONE reason cotton from Texas was shipped to Vietnam, as reflected in the image.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Sourcing
> Texas cotton was shipped to Vietnam because Vietnam had lower labor costs than the United States. The "Texas Cotton U.S.A." banner suggests the raw material originated in the U.S., while the manufacturing (processing, weaving, garment production) was done in Vietnam. This allowed American companies or importers to lower production costs by taking advantage of Vietnam's cheaper workforce while using American raw materials.

**(B) Explain ONE way in which globalization contributed to the development of foreign manufacturing in the late 20th century.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The reduction of tariffs through the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) made it economically rational for companies to locate manufacturing in low-wage countries and export finished goods back to wealthy markets. Before GATT, high protective tariffs (averaging ~40% globally) would have offset the savings from cheap foreign labor. As tariffs fell to under 5% by the 1990s, companies could profit by paying low wages in Vietnam or Bangladesh and selling the finished products in high-income markets without heavy import penalties.

**(C) Explain ONE way in which economic trends in Vietnam were similar to economic trends in Mexico in the late 20th century.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Both Vietnam and Mexico became major manufacturing hubs for foreign companies seeking low-wage labor. In Mexico, NAFTA (1994) enabled U.S. and Canadian companies to build maquiladoras employing low-wage Mexican workers to produce goods tariff-free for North American markets. Similarly, Vietnam attracted foreign investment in garment and electronics manufacturing due to significantly lower labor costs than China or the United States. In both cases, workers — especially women — faced low wages, poor conditions, and limited ability to organize, and mounted strikes in protest. Both countries thus exemplify globalization's pattern of manufacturing relocation to the global periphery.

### SAQ 2 — Economic Liberalization (No Source)

**(A) Identify ONE way in which governments encouraged economic liberalization in the late 20th century.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Identification
> Governments reduced protective tariffs and trade barriers through international agreements. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) and, later, the World Trade Organization (WTO) facilitated a reduction in average global tariffs from approximately 40% to under 5% over the second half of the 20th century. By removing these barriers, governments enabled companies to buy and sell across national borders more cheaply, encouraging the integration of global markets.

**(B) Explain ONE way in which revolutions in information and communications technology affected knowledge economies in the late 20th century.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The development of the internet and digital communications technology enabled knowledge economies to grow by allowing the near-instantaneous sharing of information across national borders. In Finland, for example, investment in telecommunications infrastructure and education during the 1990s enabled the country to transition from a manufacturing economy to a tech-sector economy, pioneering mobile phone technology through companies like Nokia. Knowledge work — software design, engineering, financial services — could now be performed remotely and sold globally, allowing nations with educated workforces but without significant raw materials to compete in the global economy.

**(C) Explain ONE way in which industrial production affected Asia or Latin America in the late 20th century.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Effect
> The relocation of industrial production to Asia in the late 20th century created millions of jobs in countries like Bangladesh, Vietnam, and China, contributing to significant economic growth and poverty reduction. Bangladesh, for example, built an export economy in which clothing accounts for approximately 80% of its exports, funded largely by foreign investment from companies in wealthy nations seeking cheaper manufacturing. However, this industrial growth also exposed workers to exploitative conditions — low wages, long hours, and dangerous factories — leading to labor strikes and international campaigns for improved standards. Industrial relocation thus both raised living standards in absolute terms and created new forms of labor exploitation.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

*In one to three paragraphs, explain how the global economy changed and remained the same from 1900 to the present.*

> [!tip] Model Response — CCOT
> The global economy changed dramatically after 1970 in terms of integration, speed, and the geographical distribution of production. Free trade agreements like GATT and the WTO reduced global tariffs from roughly 40% to under 5%, making international supply chains economically viable at an unprecedented scale. The digital revolution enabled near-instantaneous global financial transactions and created entirely new economic sectors — knowledge economies in Finland, Silicon Valley, and India — that had no parallel in earlier periods. The rise of China and India as major economic powers shifted the center of gravity of global production, and hundreds of millions of people were lifted from poverty in a historically short time.
>
> Yet many features of the global economy remained continuous. Multinational corporations — the organizational engine of economic globalization — trace their lineage directly to the joint-stock companies of the Commercial Revolution (the British and Dutch East India Companies of the 17th century). The fundamental dynamic of wealthy nations benefiting disproportionately from global trade, while workers in developing nations face exploitation and low wages, echoes patterns from the age of industrialization and colonialism. Mexican maquiladora workers in the 1990s faced conditions not entirely unlike those of 19th-century British factory workers or colonized peoples producing export commodities for imperial markets. The profit motive, labor exploitation, and the search for the cheapest available production have been continuous features of global capitalism from the 16th century to the present.
