# Economics in the Global Age

**Unit 9 — Globalization | AP Period 4 | AMSCO pp. 657–666**

**Essential Question:** How did the global economy change and remain the same from 1900 to the present?

**AP Themes:** ECN (Economy), GOV (Governance), TEC (Technology)
**AP Skills:** Causation, Continuity and Change Over Time

---

## The Big Picture

The late 20th century saw the most dramatic acceleration of economic globalization in human history. Three interlocking forces drove it: the collapse of the Soviet bloc (which opened formerly closed economies), the spread of **neoliberal** free-market ideology (championed by Reagan and Thatcher), and the digital revolution (which enabled instantaneous global transactions). The result was a world economy far more integrated than ever before — but also one where gains were unevenly distributed and where manufacturing migrated toward low-wage regions while knowledge work concentrated in educated, technology-rich societies.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently tests continuity here: multinational corporations are NOT new in the late 20th century. The British East India Company and [[Concept Notes/Dutch East India Company\|Dutch East India Company]] were the original MNCs — see [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] and [[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview\|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]]. Know the continuity (profit-driven global operations, exploitation of labor) and the change (scale, speed, digital infrastructure).

## The Acceleration of Free-Market Economies

**Economic liberalization** — reducing government intervention to enable freer trade and investment — spread globally from the 1970s onward. After the Cold War ended, Eastern Bloc nations entered global markets, and previously nonaligned nations like India relaxed trade restrictions in the 1990s.

The ideological architects were **Ronald Reagan** (U.S.) and **Margaret Thatcher** (UK), who championed **free markets**, privatization, deregulation, and cuts to social programs. While framed as national policy, corporations exploited the shift by moving production to countries with lower wages, taxes, and environmental standards. Critics charged that globalization enabled labor exploitation and environmental damage — a tension that runs through every AP argument about this period.

**Chile as an early test case (AMSCO p. 658):** In 1973, Augusto Pinochet seized power in a U.S.-backed coup and allowed the "Chicago Boys" — economists trained under Milton Friedman — to implement radical free-market reforms: privatization, deregulation, and austerity. The reforms failed to address poverty and required violent repression to implement. Later democratic governments tempered the approach, combining free trade with social programs to substantially reduce poverty. Chile thus illustrates both the promise and the human cost of economic liberalization.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate free-market ideology with economic success. The AP exam tests nuance: Chile reduced poverty only after combining free trade WITH government social programs. Pure neoliberalism had brutal costs.

**China's economic reforms (AMSCO p. 658):** In 1978–1981, Deng Xiaoping launched the Four Modernizations, replacing communes with peasant-leased plots, reopening the Shanghai stock market, and creating Special Economic Zones to attract foreign investment. His motto: "Let some people get rich first." The result was explosive economic growth — but political liberalization did not follow. When students staged pro-democracy protests in **Tiananmen Square** (1989), the government crushed the movement with tanks, killing hundreds to thousands. This moment crystallizes the defining tension of China's development: economic openness with political authoritarianism.

## Knowledge Economies

The late 20th century also produced a new kind of economy. A **knowledge economy** generates wealth through information, innovation, and intellectual work rather than manufacturing. Silicon Valley, Finland's tech sector, and India's software industry are all examples.

**Finland** transformed from an agrarian economy (1950s) to an industrial one (post-WWII) to a tech powerhouse (1990s) after the Soviet Union's collapse eliminated a major customer for its manufactured goods. By investing in education and establishing the Science and Technology Policy Council, Finland achieved growth through mobile phones and software.

**Japan** used quasi-mercantilist policies after WWII — export subsidies, high protective tariffs, rigorous education — to become a manufacturing powerhouse, then diversified into a knowledge economy and international banking center. It remains (as of the AMSCO writing) the world's third-largest economy.

**Asian Tigers:** Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan followed Japan's model — government-business partnerships, export-oriented production, intensive education — and achieved rapid industrialization. The success of Japan, the Asian Tigers, and China lifted hundreds of millions from poverty, making East Asia the defining success story of late-20th-century globalization.

> [!tip]
> Asian Tigers comparison: They share Japan's state-guided export model but each has distinct characteristics. Singapore = city-state entrepôt; South Korea = chaebols (conglomerates) like Samsung; Taiwan = electronics supply chains. The AP exam may ask you to compare their development model with Latin American or African economic experiences.

## Shifting Manufacturing

As knowledge economies developed in Europe, North America, and East Asia, manufacturing relocated to regions with lower labor costs.

**Vietnam and Bangladesh** became major garment and electronics exporters. Workers in both countries have mounted strikes protesting low wages and poor conditions — illustrating that the same forces that provide jobs also create new forms of labor exploitation.

**Mexico and NAFTA (AMSCO p. 660–661):** The 1994 North American Free Trade Agreement eliminated most tariffs among the U.S., Canada, and Mexico. The immediate result: growth of **maquiladoras** (foreign-owned assembly factories) employing cheap Mexican labor to produce tariff-free exports. U.S. labor unions protested the export of American jobs; Mexican workers, especially young women, faced harsh factory conditions. NAFTA is the AP's go-to example for the costs and benefits of regional free trade.

**Honduras** pursued sustainable manufacturing through business-government partnerships — an example of a developing nation attempting to attract foreign investment on better terms.

## Transnational Trade Organizations

Several institutions reduced tariffs globally and set the rules of the new economic order:

The **General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)** (1947) progressively cut tariffs from a ~40% world average to under 5% by the 1990s. The **World Trade Organization (WTO)** replaced GATT in 1995 and now governs over 90% of international trade. The WTO became highly controversial: its closed meetings, corporate-dominated board, and rules that could punish nations for refusing to buy sweatshop goods made it a lightning rod for anti-globalization movements (see Topic 9.7).

Regional trade blocs complemented the WTO: the **European Economic Community** (later EU), **Mercosur** (South America), and **ASEAN** (Southeast Asia) each promoted regional economic integration.

## Multinational Corporations

Multinational corporations (MNCs) — businesses incorporated in one country but operating globally — are the organizational engine of economic globalization. Their lineage runs from the joint-stock companies of the Commercial Revolution ([[Unit-4.5/Maritime Empires Develop Overview\|Maritime Empires Develop Overview]]) through the imperial extraction of the 19th century to today's tech giants. The [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] is the canonical precursor — profit-driven global operations, labor exploitation, tax avoidance — all continuous to the present.

**India's rise (AMSCO p. 662):** When India opened its markets in the early 1990s, its educated, English-speaking workforce attracted massive foreign investment. **Microsoft** and **Google** invested in India's IT sector. India's middle class swelled to over 350 million by 2014 — the largest of any country.

**Mahindra & Mahindra** — an India-based conglomerate producing vehicles, farm equipment, and energy — exemplifies how globalization enabled a non-Western corporation to become a major multinational.

**Nestlé** — the Swiss food giant — illustrates the controversies: sourcing cocoa from farms using child labor, treating bottled water as a commodity rather than a human right, while simultaneously investing in sustainable agriculture programs. The Nestlé/Mahindra contrast is a built-in AP comparison: responsible vs. controversial MNC behavior, Western vs. non-Western corporate power.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves MNC arguments. Structure yours: MNCs create jobs and raise living standards (pro), but exploit cheap labor, evade taxes, and undermine local industries (con). Always connect to the continuity: European joint-stock companies of the 16th–17th centuries did the same things — profit extraction through global operations — just without fiber-optic cables.

## Continuity & Change Summary

| Change | Continuity |
|--------|------------|
| Tariffs fell from ~40% to ~5% globally | Multinational corporations have existed since the 16th century |
| China and India integrated into global economy | Wealthy nations benefited more than developing ones |
| Knowledge economy replaced manufacturing in wealthy nations | Low-wage labor exploitation persisted (maquiladoras ≈ colonial labor systems) |
| Regional trade blocs (EU, ASEAN, Mercosur) proliferated | Free trade ideology recurring from Adam Smith onward |
| Digital technology enabled near-instant global transactions | Gap between rich and poor persisted or widened |
