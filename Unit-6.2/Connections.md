# Connections

**AMSCO Pages:** 375–387

## Prerequisite — What This Topic Builds On

**[[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview\|Topic 6.1 — Rationales for Imperialism]]**
Topic 6.1 explains *why* imperial expansion happened: economic demand for raw materials, Social Darwinism and racial ideology, nationalist competition, and the "civilizing mission" rationale. Topic 6.2 shows *how* those motivations were translated into actual territorial conquest and control — the specific mechanisms (state-run colonies, settler colonies, spheres of influence), the technologies that made it possible (quinine, steamships), and the specific regional patterns across Africa, Asia, and the Americas. You cannot fully explain the causes of state expansion without 6.1, and you cannot understand 6.1's ideas without 6.2's concrete examples.

## What This Topic Leads To

**Topic 6.3 — Indigenous Responses to State Expansion** ← folder not yet built
The logical consequence of 6.2's expansion is 6.3's resistance. Indigenous peoples did not passively accept imperial conquest — they resisted, accommodated, and negotiated in varied ways. The Boxer Rebellion (China), the Battle of Adwa (Ethiopia), Maori wars in New Zealand, and Afrikaner resistance in South Africa all appear in Topic 6.2 as the beginning of stories that 6.3 extends into a full analysis of patterns of resistance. When writing LEQs about causation or continuity/change, connecting 6.2's expansion to 6.3's responses strengthens the argument by showing the colonial relationship as contested and dynamic.

**Topic 6.4 — Global Economic Development** ← folder not yet built
The economic consequences of 6.2's colonial structures — raw material extraction, cash-crop monocultures, corvée and forced labor, spheres of influence — created the global economic patterns Topic 6.4 analyzes. Colonialism fundamentally restructured global trade flows, making tropical regions producers of raw materials for industrial metropoles. The underdevelopment argument (Hobson, Lenin, later dependency theory) connects directly: 6.2's mechanisms explain *how* 6.4's economic inequalities were created and maintained.

## Lateral Connections — Compare With

**[[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview\|Topic 4.4 — Maritime Empires Link Regions]]**
The 15th–17th century maritime empires (Portugal, Spain, Netherlands, Britain) established the first wave of European overseas expansion that 6.2 continues and transforms. Key comparisons for AP essays:

- *Similar:* Both driven by desire for trade profits; both used corporate intermediaries (EIC, VOC); both displaced indigenous populations in settler contexts; both imposed European goods and culture.
- *Different:* 19th-century expansion was far faster, more total, and more technologically asymmetric (industrial weapons vs. non-industrial); earlier expansion was primarily coastal and mercantile, 19th-century expansion penetrated interiors and imposed direct administration; 19th-century expansion was justified by Social Darwinism (new ideology) rather than religious conversion alone.

## Cross-Topic Thematic Connections

**Technology and Expansion:** Topic 4.1 covers early gunpowder weapons enabling empire; Topic 6.2 shows how industrialized weaponry, steamships, and quinine created a new technological gap between industrial and non-industrial societies that made the Scramble for Africa possible at a speed and scale unimaginable in earlier centuries.

**Continuity in Corporate Colonialism:** The East India Company (Topic 6.1 and 6.2) and the VOC (Topics 4.4 and 6.2) appear across multiple units as examples of corporate colonialism. The pattern of corporate rule eventually replaced by state rule (EIC → British Crown 1858; VOC → Dutch state 1800; Leopold's Congo → Belgian state 1908) is a recurring historical pattern worth noting in essays.

**Comparison with Russian Expansion:** Russia's overland expansion into Central Asia and Siberia (covered in this topic) differs structurally from Western maritime imperialism but shares the core features of resource extraction and indigenous displacement. The Great Game illustrates that imperial competition was itself a cause of expansion — powers expanded partly to block rivals.

**Japan as Both Victim and Perpetrator:** Japan first appears in 6.2 as the target of U.S. coercion (Perry's black ships, 1853) and then rapidly transitions to becoming an imperial power itself (Colonization Society, 1893; empire in East Asia by 1900s). This dual role makes Japan essential for comparison questions about responses to imperialism and the adoption of imperial strategies.

> [!exam-tip]
> For LEQ comparison prompts about "European expansion before 1750 vs. after 1750," use Topic 4.4 as your before-case and 6.2 as your after-case. The key differences are industrialization (technology), scale (interior vs. coastal), administration (direct state vs. corporate), and ideology (Social Darwinism vs. religion/mercantilism).

> [!tip]
> The three-type imperialism framework (state-run / settler / economic domination) from 6.2 is also useful for contextualizing earlier colonial patterns from Topics 4.3 and 4.4 — you can argue that earlier colonialism was predominantly economic domination with emerging settler elements, while 19th-century colonialism added the full bureaucratic state-run model.
