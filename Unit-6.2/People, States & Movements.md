# People, States & Movements

**AMSCO Pages:** 375–387

## Key People

**King Leopold II of Belgium**
Belgian king (r. 1865–1909) who is one of the AP World History exam's central examples of the brutal extremes of imperial exploitation. Unable to convince the Belgian government to fund an African colony, Leopold established the Congo Free State as his *personal* property. His agents forced Congolese workers to harvest ivory and rubber under a quota system backed by mutilation — workers who failed quotas had their hands cut off; their spouses were held hostage. As many as 8 million Congolese perished. When international journalists and activists exposed conditions in the 1900s, the Belgian Parliament stripped Leopold of the colony in 1908 and assumed direct government control. His letter to missionaries — "teach students to read and not to reason" — is a classic AP source illustrating colonial ideology.

**Cecil Rhodes**
British mining magnate and colonialist who built a personal fortune through the De Beers diamond company and used it to colonize much of southern Africa (present-day Zimbabwe was once called "Rhodesia"). Rhodes openly believed in Anglo-Saxon racial superiority and dreamed of a British-controlled corridor from Cairo to Cape Town. He is a quintessential example of the individual entrepreneur as an engine of imperial expansion — connecting personal profit, racial ideology, and state power.

**Lord Curzon**
British Viceroy of India (r. 1899–1905); a symbol of the administrative machinery of British colonial rule. The Viceroy was the Crown's representative, overseeing a vast bureaucracy that ran the Indian subcontinent after the EIC's dissolution in 1858. Curzon's tenure exemplified both the confidence of British imperialism at its height and the tensions with Indian nationalist sentiment that would intensify in the 20th century.

**Empress Dowager Cixi**
Chinese ruler (effectively 1861–1908) who encouraged the Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901) and in 1900 ordered all foreigners killed. When the Boxers were defeated by a multinational foreign force, Cixi fled Beijing; the resulting settlement imposed further foreign controls on China. Her combination of conservatism and tactical maneuvering — earlier ending the Hundred Days of Reform ([[Unit-5.8/Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview\|Topic 5.8]]), later belatedly supporting limited reforms — illustrates the [[Concept Notes/Qing Dynasty\|Qing Dynasty]]'s inability to adapt quickly enough to survive foreign pressure.

**President James Monroe**
U.S. president who in 1823 issued the Monroe Doctrine warning European powers to stay out of the Western Hemisphere. Although framed as defensive, the doctrine implicitly reserved the Americas as a U.S. sphere of influence — a claim that Theodore Roosevelt made explicit with the 1904 Roosevelt Corollary.

**President Theodore Roosevelt**
U.S. president (1901–1909) who actively promoted American imperial expansion grounded in Social Darwinism and belief in Anglo-Saxon superiority. His 1904 Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine asserted the U.S. right to intervene anywhere in Latin America if countries showed "instability." He sent troops to the Dominican Republic in 1905 and pushed the construction of the Panama Canal (completed 1914). Roosevelt embodied the U.S. transition from continental to overseas empire.

## Key States and Colonial Holdings

**Belgium — Congo Free State**
Unique in that it was owned *personally* by Leopold II rather than the Belgian state. After 1908, the Belgian government took direct control. The Congo's brutal history made it a touchstone for early 20th-century debates about the limits of imperialism.

**Britain — Global Empire**
By 1900, Britain held India (directly since 1858), Sierra Leone, Gold Coast (Ghana), Lagos/Nigeria, Cape Colony and South Africa, Egypt (1882), Australia, and New Zealand — among many others. The administration varied: some territories were Crown colonies with governors appointed from London; others were self-governing dominions (Australia, New Zealand, Canada). India was the "jewel in the crown."

**France — Second Largest Empire**
France controlled Algeria (settler colony since 1830), West African territories (Guinea, Ivory Coast, Niger), and Indochina (Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos after 1885). Unlike Britain, France pursued assimilation — attempting to make colonial subjects French citizens.

**Russia — Eurasian Land Empire**
Expanded south into Central Asia (Great Game rivalry with Britain over Afghanistan) and east into Manchuria. Sold Alaska to the United States in 1867. Russia's expansion was predominantly overland rather than maritime — distinct from Western European patterns.

**United States — Emerging Pacific Power**
After the [[Concept Notes/Spanish-American War\|Spanish-American War]] (1898), the U.S. held the Philippines, Guam, and Puerto Rico, while Cuba became a U.S. protectorate. This marked a dramatic departure from continental expansion into overseas imperialism.

**Japan — Industrial Imperial Power**
After the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration\|Meiji Restoration]] (1868), Japan rapidly industrialized and began to build its own empire. Through the Colonization Society (1893) and later military conquest, Japan sought colonies in Korea, China, and Southeast Asia — driven by lack of natural resources and the desire to match Western imperial powers.

## Key Movements and Conflicts

**Scramble for Africa (1880s–1900s)**
The rapid European colonization of Africa in the last decades of the 19th century. Formalized by the [[Concept Notes/Berlin Conference\|Berlin Conference]] (1884–85). By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent. The "Scramble" was driven by competition for raw materials, nationalist rivalries among European powers, and the new accessibility of the African interior via quinine and steamships.

**Boer Wars (1880–81; 1899–1902)**
Wars between Britain and the Afrikaners (Dutch-descended settlers in South Africa) over land and political control. The Second Boer War was the bloodier conflict; Britain used scorched-earth tactics and concentration camps. The wars ended with British control over South Africa but laid the seeds for the apartheid system that would emerge in the 20th century.

**[[Concept Notes/Taiping Rebellion\|Taiping Rebellion]] (1850–1864)**
Massive internal revolt against the Qing Dynasty in China, led by Hong Xiuquan. One of the bloodiest conflicts in human history — 20+ million dead. The rebellion catastrophically weakened the Qing government, making China more susceptible to foreign economic domination through spheres of influence.

**Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901)**
Chinese anti-imperialist uprising that attacked Christian missionaries and foreign interests. Encouraged by Empress Cixi. Defeated by a multinational force. Resulted in the Boxer Protocol (1901) imposing humiliating reparations and further restricting Chinese sovereignty. The rebellion illustrates both the depth of Chinese resentment of imperialism and the limits of that resistance given Western military superiority.

**Trail of Tears (1830s)**
U.S. government's forced relocation of Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, and other Eastern Woodlands Native American peoples to Indian Territory (Oklahoma). Thousands died from exposure, malnutrition, and disease. A defining event of American settler colonialism — directly comparable, on the AP exam, to indigenous dispossession in Australia and New Zealand.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently asks you to compare imperial agents: state-run colonialism (Leopold, Lord Curzon), corporate colonialism (EIC, VOC), and settler colonialism (Afrikaners, American westward expansion). Know the role of the individual *and* the structural forces behind each.

> [!tip]
> Japan is a high-yield comparison: Japan *was* a target of imperialism (Perry, 1853) and then *became* an imperial power (by 1895). This makes Japan useful for both sides of comparison questions about responses to imperialism and the adoption of imperial strategies.
