# State Expansion

**Essential Question:** How did European and other powers expand their states and empires in this period?

**AMSCO Pages:** 375–387 | **AP Period 3** | **AP Themes:** GOV, ECN, MIG | **AP Skills:** Causation, Comparison

## Big Picture

Between roughly 1750 and 1900, industrialized powers — primarily European nations, but also the United States, Russia, and Japan — dramatically expanded their territorial control across Africa, Asia, the Pacific, and the Americas. This expansion built on the motivations covered in [[Unit-6.1/Rationales for Imperialism Overview\|Topic 6.1]] (economic greed, Social Darwinism, nationalism, religious mission) and took three distinct structural forms that the AP exam tests directly.

The scope of change was staggering: by 1914, European powers controlled roughly 84% of the Earth's land surface. Only Abyssinia (Ethiopia) and Liberia in Africa, and Siam in Southeast Asia, remained meaningfully independent of European rule. The pace of this conquest accelerated after the 1880s, driven by industrial technology — especially [[Concept Notes/Quinine\|quinine]], which made tropical Africa survivable for Europeans, and steamships that penetrated river interiors.

## The Three Types of Imperialism

The AP exam frequently asks you to compare these three types. Know their features and outcomes cold.

| Type | Examples | Key Features | Outcomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **State-Run Colony** | British West Africa, Belgian Congo | Western institutions replace local culture; justified as "civilizing" | Exploitation of indigenous labor; loss of indigenous culture; creation of mixed elite class |
| **Settler Colony** | British South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, French Algeria | Focus on land control; settlers displace indigenous peoples | Loss of indigenous culture; genocide; spread of disease; forced dispossession |
| **Economic Domination** | British & French spheres in China; U.S. in Latin America | Raw material extraction; local government nominally intact but weakened | Social destabilization; monoculture; soil depletion; environmental damage |

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves asking you to categorize colonial situations by type. Congo Free State = state-run colony under Leopold II. Algeria = settler colony. China = economic domination via spheres of influence. Siam = escaped colonization entirely by playing powers against each other.

## Imperialism in Africa

For most of the 1800s, Europeans were confined to coastal trading posts. Two technologies changed this: [[Concept Notes/Quinine\|quinine]] (treating malaria, allowing interior penetration) and the steamship (enabling river navigation). Africa's mineral and agricultural wealth — diamonds, ivory, palm oil, rubber — then made the interior enormously attractive.

**The Scramble for Africa (1880s–1900s)** was formalized at the [[Concept Notes/Berlin Conference\|Berlin Conference]] (1884–85), hosted by Bismarck to prevent European war over African colonies. No African representatives were invited. European powers divided the continent with artificial borders that ignored existing ethnic and political boundaries — borders that became sources of violent conflict when African nations gained independence in the 20th century.

Key colonial situations to know:

- **West Africa** — Britain established Sierra Leone (1787, for freed enslaved people), the Gold Coast (1874, present-day Ghana), and Lagos (base for Nigeria). France held Algeria, Guinea, Ivory Coast, and Niger.
- **South Africa** — British took the Cape Colony from the Dutch during the Napoleonic Wars. Afrikaners (descendants of Dutch settlers) moved east, clashing with Zulus and later fighting Britain in the Boer Wars (1880–81, 1899–1902). Britain won, using concentration camps to intern civilians; nearly 15,000 of 100,000 interned black Africans died.
- **Congo** — King Leopold II personally owned the Congo Free State, extracting rubber and ivory through forced labor and terror (severing hands, holding spouses captive). Up to 8 million Congolese died. In 1908 the Belgian Parliament took over.
- **Independent Africa** — By 1900 only Abyssinia (Ethiopia, which defeated Italy at Adwa in 1895) and Liberia remained independent.

## Imperialism in South and East Asia

**South Asia:** The British [[Concept Notes/East India Company\|East India Company]] (EIC) steadily absorbed the weak Mughal Empire, eventually controlling all of South Asia from Pakistan to Ceylon (Sri Lanka). After the Sepoy (Indian) Rebellion of 1857, the British Crown dissolved the EIC and assumed direct rule.

**East Asia — China:** China kept its own government but was carved into spheres of influence by multiple powers. Internal crises weakened the [[Concept Notes/Qing Dynasty\|Qing Dynasty]] and invited foreign domination:

- **[[Concept Notes/Taiping Rebellion\|Taiping Rebellion]] (1850–1864):** 20+ million dead; one of history's deadliest conflicts; weakened Qing authority.
- **Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901):** Anti-imperialist uprising encouraged by Empress Cixi; defeated by a multinational force; further humiliated Qing legitimacy.

**East Asia — Japan:** After Commodore Perry forced open Japan in 1853, Japan industrialized rapidly (Meiji Restoration) and became an imperial power itself, eventually building an empire across Korea, parts of China, and Southeast Asia.

## Imperialism in Southeast Asia

The Dutch controlled the **Dutch East Indies** (Indonesia) after the VOC went bankrupt (1800); forced cash crop production (tea, rubber, sugar) devastated rice cultivation and Indonesian farmers. France controlled **Indochina** (Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos) after the Sino-French War (1884–85). Britain held **Malaya** — the world's largest rubber producer by 1900. **Siam** (Thailand) alone escaped colonization through skillful diplomacy and [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration\|Meiji]]-style modernizing reforms.

## Settler Colonialism in the Pacific and Americas

**Australia and New Zealand** became British settler colonies — Australia initially as a penal colony (1788), New Zealand after the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) promised Maori land rights that were then repeatedly violated.

**United States expansion** followed a parallel logic:
- The **Monroe Doctrine** (1823) warned Europe out of the Western Hemisphere while implying American dominance there.
- **Manifest Destiny** rationalized continental expansion and displacement of Native Americans (Trail of Tears, 1830s).
- The **[[Concept Notes/Spanish-American War\|Spanish-American War]]** (1898) made the U.S. a Pacific and Caribbean imperial power (Philippines, Guam, Puerto Rico, Cuba).
- The **Roosevelt Corollary** (1904) asserted the U.S. right to intervene in "unstable" Latin American nations.

**Russia** expanded east and south — into Central Asia, Alaska (sold to U.S. in 1867), and Manchuria — competing with Britain in the **Great Game** over Afghanistan.

## Technologies That Enabled Expansion

Quinine (treating malaria) and the steamship opened Africa's interior. Industrial military technology (repeating rifles, machine guns, artillery) gave European armies decisive advantages over indigenous forces. The Suez Canal (1869), built by corvée labor, shrank the sea route to Asia and made the region even more strategically valuable.

> Compare 19th-century state expansion with earlier maritime empires ([[Unit-4.4/Maritime Empires Link Regions Overview\|Topic 4.4]]): both extracted resources and used trading companies, but the scale, speed, and reach of 19th-century imperialism was vastly greater due to industrialization. The motivations in 6.1 explain *why* this happened; 6.2 covers *how* it happened and *where*; [[Unit-6.3/Indigenous Responses to State Expansion Overview|Topic 6.3]] covers indigenous responses.

> [!warning] Common Misconception
> The Berlin Conference did not colonize Africa — it formalized and *divided* colonization that was already underway. Also, China was never formally colonized; it experienced "economic domination" through spheres of influence, meaning it kept its own government while losing effective economic sovereignty.
