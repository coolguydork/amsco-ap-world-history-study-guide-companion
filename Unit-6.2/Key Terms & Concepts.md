# Key Terms & Concepts

**AMSCO Pages:** 375–387

## Rulers

**King Leopold II**
Belgian king (r. 1865–1909) who personally owned the Congo Free State as a private colony; used brutal forced labor — including severing workers' hands — to extract ivory and rubber; responsible for the deaths of up to 8 million Congolese; international outcry prompted the Belgian Parliament to take over the colony in 1908. His quote to missionaries — "teach students to read and not to reason" — is a classic AP source for analyzing imperial attitudes.

**Cixi**
Empress Dowager of China (1835–1908) who supported the Boxer Rebellion and in 1900 ordered all foreigners killed; the rebellion's defeat further humiliated the Qing Dynasty and deepened Western control. Cross-reference Topic 5.8 for her earlier role ending the Hundred Days of Reform.

## Colonial Territories

**Sierra Leone**
British colony established in West Africa in 1787 as a home for freed enslaved people from across the British Empire; along with Gambia, served as a base for British efforts to suppress the ongoing export of enslaved people from the region.

**Gold Coast**
British crown colony (1874) in West Africa, present-day Ghana; the powerful Asante Empire to the north resisted British control until 1901; a prize colonial holding due to its gold and palm oil.

**Cape Colony**
British colony at the southern tip of Africa, seized from the Dutch during the Napoleonic Wars (1806); the starting point for British-Afrikaner conflict that escalated through the 19th century into the Boer Wars.

**Congo Free State**
Personal colony of King Leopold II of Belgium in central Africa (1885–1908); site of systematic forced labor, mutilation, and mass death of indigenous Congolese; transferred to Belgian state control in 1908 after an international humanitarian outcry.

**Abyssinia**
Modern-day Ethiopia; one of only two African nations to remain independent of European colonization; its army defeated an Italian invasion at the Battle of Adwa (1895), making it a powerful symbol of African resistance to imperialism.

**Liberia**
West African nation founded by formerly enslaved people from the United States; one of only two African countries (along with Abyssinia/Ethiopia) not colonized by Europeans, though it maintained a dependent relationship with the United States.

**Ceylon**
Island south of the Indian subcontinent (modern Sri Lanka) brought under British East India Company control as part of the broader colonization of South Asia; later a British Crown colony and major producer of tea and rubber.

**Dutch East Indies**
Present-day Indonesia; controlled by the Dutch government after the VOC went bankrupt in 1800; forced to produce cash crops (tea, rubber, sugar) for export, which curtailed rice cultivation and caused severe hardship for Indonesian farmers.

**Indochina**
French colonial territory comprising Cambodia, Laos, and all of modern-day Vietnam; established after France's victory in the Sino-French War (1884–85); rubber plantations were the primary engine of French exploitation.

**Malaya**
British colony on the Malay Peninsula (modern Malaysia); became the world's greatest producer of natural rubber by the end of the 19th century; Singapore, founded 1822, served as its key trading hub and the most important seaport in Southeast Asia.

**Siam**
Modern-day Thailand; the only Southeast Asian nation to avoid European colonization by skillfully playing Britain and France against each other and implementing Meiji-style modernizing reforms including railroads and Western-style schools.

**Australia**
British settler colony established 1788 initially as a penal colony for convicts; became economically significant through wool production and gold discoveries (1851); indigenous Aboriginal populations were systematically displaced as the colony expanded.

**New Zealand**
British settler colony annexed 1839; became a separate colony in 1840 after the Treaty of Waitangi; despite treaty promises of Maori land rights, European settlers repeatedly encroached on Maori land, leading to open warfare.

## Treaties and Conferences

**[[Concept Notes/Berlin Conference\|Berlin Conference]]**
Meeting (1884–85) hosted by Bismarck at which European powers divided Africa among themselves without any African input; formalized the "Scramble for Africa" and established rules for the orderly colonization of the continent. The arbitrary borders drawn here divided unified societies and merged rival groups — causing conflicts that persisted for centuries.

**Treaty of Waitangi**
1840 treaty between the British Crown and Maori chiefs of New Zealand guaranteeing protection of Maori land rights; repeatedly violated as European settlers expanded; the gap between treaty promises and colonial reality is a key example of settler colonialism's pattern of indigenous dispossession.

## Ideas and Doctrines

**Scramble for Africa**
Rapid European colonization of Africa (1880s–1900) following the Berlin Conference; by 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. Driven by competition for resources, nationalism, and the belief that Africa's interior was now accessible with quinine and steamships.

**Monroe Doctrine**
1823 U.S. declaration (President James Monroe) that European powers should not intervene in the affairs of Western Hemisphere nations; implied American imperial ambition in the Americas and laid the groundwork for the Roosevelt Corollary (1904).

**Manifest Destiny**
19th-century American belief that the United States had a natural and inevitable right to expand across the North American continent to the Pacific Ocean; used to justify displacement of Native Americans, the Mexican-American War (1846–48), and westward settlement.

**Roosevelt Corollary**
1904 addition to the Monroe Doctrine by President Theodore Roosevelt asserting the U.S. right to intervene in Latin American nations that showed "instability"; justified multiple U.S. military interventions (e.g., Dominican Republic); extended American economic domination under the guise of maintaining order.

**Great Game**
19th-century geopolitical rivalry between the British and Russian Empires for dominance in Central Asia, especially Afghanistan; neither power succeeded in controlling Afghanistan; illustrates how imperial competition extended beyond formal colonization into zones of indirect control.

## Systems and Structures

**concentration camps**
British detention camps used during the Boer Wars (1880–1902) to intern Afrikaner and black African civilians displaced by fighting; characterized by inadequate food, medical care, and sanitation; nearly 15,000 of 100,000 interned black Africans died — an early use of the term for a system of mass civilian detention.

**penal colony**
Settlement established to house convicted criminals transported from Britain; Australia was designated a penal colony in 1788 after Britain lost its American colonies; it later grew into a free-settler colony as economic opportunities (wool, gold) emerged.

**spheres of influence**
Zones within a nominally independent country where a foreign power holds exclusive trading rights and access to natural resources; used by European powers and Japan to dominate China economically without establishing direct colonial rule. China kept its government but lost effective economic sovereignty.

**settler colony**
Colonial territory in which large numbers of settlers from the colonizing nation permanently relocate, displace or dominate indigenous populations, and take control of land; examples include British Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and French Algeria; characterized by loss of indigenous culture, genocide, disease, and forced dispossession.

**corvée laborer**
Worker forced to perform unpaid labor as a form of taxation; approximately 1.5 million Egyptians, many of them corvée laborers, were compelled to build the Suez Canal (completed 1869); thousands died — illustrates how imperial projects relied on coerced indigenous labor.

## Wars and Conflicts

**Boer Wars**
Two conflicts (1880–81 and 1899–1902) between Britain and Afrikaner (Boer) settler communities in South Africa over land and political control; British victory absorbed Afrikaner and African communities into the British Empire and displaced millions of farmers onto poor land.

**Seven Years' War**
Global conflict (1756–1763) fought between Britain, France, and their allies across multiple continents; France's defeat drove it out of India, leaving Britain as the dominant colonial power in South Asia — a turning point in the history of British imperialism.

**Taiping Rebellion**
Massive Chinese civil war (1850–1864) led by Hong Xiuquan against the Qing Dynasty; one of the deadliest conflicts in history with 20+ million deaths; weakened the Qing government and made China more vulnerable to European economic domination.

**Boxer Rebellion**
Anti-imperialist uprising in China (1899–1901) by the "Boxers" (martial arts practitioners) who attacked Chinese Christians and foreign missionaries; supported by Empress Cixi; crushed by a multinational force; further humiliated the Qing Dynasty and deepened Western control over China.

**Spanish-American War**
1898 war between the United States and Spain in which U.S. victory gave America control of Guam, Puerto Rico, Cuba, and the Philippines; marked the emergence of the United States as a global imperial power.

## Peoples

**Afrikaners**
Descendants of 17th-century Dutch settlers in South Africa (also called Boers); after Britain took the Cape Colony, many Afrikaners moved east into conflict with indigenous groups (including Zulus) and eventually with Britain in the Boer Wars; their displacement laid the groundwork for apartheid.

**Maori**
Indigenous Polynesian people of New Zealand; guaranteed land rights under the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) but repeatedly dispossessed as European settlers encroached; resisted through open warfare; their experience is a key example of settler colonialism's impact on indigenous peoples.

## Society and Migration

**Colonization Society**
Japanese organization established in 1893 by government leaders to plan and establish colonies in Mexico and Latin America; part of Japan's broader late-19th-century drive for overseas territories and rural population relief; connects to Japan's wider imperial ambitions in East Asia.

**Indian Territory**
Area in present-day Oklahoma designated by the U.S. government as a forced resettlement zone for Native American peoples displaced from the southeastern United States; the destination of the Trail of Tears (1830s).

**Trail of Tears**
Forced relocation (1830s) of thousands of Cherokee and other Eastern Woodlands Native Americans from the southeastern U.S. to Indian Territory in present-day Oklahoma; thousands died from exposure, malnutrition, disease, and exhaustion; a central example of U.S. settler colonialism and indigenous displacement.

## Environment and Technology

**quinine**
Medicine derived from cinchona bark used to treat malaria; its widespread availability in the 19th century dramatically reduced European mortality in tropical regions, enabling the rapid expansion of European colonization into the African interior and other tropical zones.

**Suez Canal**
100-mile waterway completed in 1869 connecting the Red Sea to the Mediterranean Sea; built with French management using the forced labor of up to 1.5 million Egyptians (many corvée laborers); dramatically shortened sea routes to Asia; Britain seized Egypt in 1882 to protect the canal, making it a strategic linchpin of British imperial power.
