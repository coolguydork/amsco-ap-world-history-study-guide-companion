# Topic 5.10 — People, States & Movements

> [!tip]
> Focus on **significance, causation, and comparison** — not biography. For each figure or movement, ask: What did they argue or do? What caused them to emerge? What changed or continued because of them? How do they compare to other figures or movements?

---

## Key Individuals

### Adam Smith (1723–1790)

**Who:** Scottish moral philosopher and political economist; author of *The Wealth of Nations* (1776).

**Significance:** Smith provided the foundational argument for **capitalism** and **laissez-faire** economics. He argued that individuals pursuing their own economic self-interest would be guided by an "invisible hand" toward outcomes that benefited society as a whole. Free markets, not government direction, were the engine of prosperity. His ideas gave intellectual legitimacy to the factory owners, merchants, and industrialists who were reshaping the global economy.

**AP Relevance:** Smith's capitalism is the *change* in economic systems that Topic 5.10 tracks. He represents the shift from mercantilism to capitalism — arguably the most consequential economic transition since the Agricultural Revolution. Smith is also the ideological opponent Marx is arguing against.

> [!exam-tip]
> On the AP exam, Adam Smith = **laissez-faire capitalism**. His key claim: private profit pursuit → collective prosperity. The key evidence against this claim: slums, child labor, working-class poverty — the conditions described in [[Unit-5.9/Society and the Industrial Age Overview|Topic 5.9]].

---

### Karl Marx (1818–1883)

**Who:** German philosopher, economist, and political theorist; co-author (with Friedrich Engels) of *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) and author of *Capital*.

**Significance:** Marx argued that capitalism was structurally exploitative — not a moral failing of individual capitalists, but an inevitability of a system that required the **bourgeoisie** to extract surplus value from the **proletariat** to generate profit. He called on workers to unite across national borders and seize the means of production, replacing capitalism with a classless communist society. Marx's ideas inspired the major political movements and revolutions of the 20th century.

**Causation:** Marx's ideas emerged directly from the conditions of industrial Britain and Western Europe — the slums, child labor, and brutal factory conditions documented in Topic 5.9. The *Communist Manifesto* opens: "A specter is haunting Europe — the specter of communism." It was a response to the specific injustices of industrial capitalism.

> [!tip]
> Marx is not arguing capitalists are evil people making bad choices. He is arguing that the *system* compels them to minimize wages and maximize profit — regardless of personal ethics. This structural critique is philosophically sophisticated and is what AP essays need to engage with accurately.

**Comparison:** Marx vs. Adam Smith is the defining intellectual confrontation of the industrial era. Both analyzed capitalism; they reached opposite conclusions about its justice and sustainability.

---

### Friedrich Engels (1820–1895)

**Who:** German businessman and social theorist; Marx's collaborator and financial supporter.

**Significance:** Engels co-authored *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) and authored *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) — a landmark empirical documentation of Manchester's industrial slums that provided the evidentiary foundation for Marxist critique. Engels's wealth (from his family's textile business) allowed Marx to write *Capital* without starving. The irony that Marxism's key patron was himself a capitalist is worth noting.

---

### Otto von Bismarck (1815–1898)

**Who:** Chancellor (prime minister) of Prussia and later the unified German Empire; the dominant European statesman of the second half of the 19th century.

**Significance:** Bismarck is significant in Topic 5.10 for two reasons. First, he unified Germany through a series of wars (Austria in 1866, France in 1870–71), demonstrating the role of nationalism and military power in state-building. Second, and more directly relevant here, Bismarck introduced Europe's **first comprehensive welfare state**: workers' accident compensation insurance, unemployment insurance, and old-age pensions.

**Causation:** Bismarck's social reforms were not motivated by sympathy for workers. He was a conservative who feared that socialist and radical movements would overthrow the German state if the government did not address industrial poverty. His reforms were a calculated preemptive strike: give workers basic security so they would not turn to revolution.

> [!exam-tip]
> Bismarck's welfare state is a key example of **political change** driven by fear of working-class radicalism. Know that his model spread — by 1900, most industrial nations were adopting some version of social insurance. This is a case of *reform as continuity-preservation*: the political system changed its policies to avoid revolutionary change to the system itself.

**Comparison:** Bismarck vs. laissez-faire liberals: liberals opposed state intervention in the economy; Bismarck used state intervention to stabilize capitalism. Both were conservatives in the sense of defending existing social order — but they chose opposite economic strategies.

---

### James Watt (1736–1819)

**Who:** Scottish inventor and engineer; developer of the improved steam engine (1769).

**Significance:** Watt's steam engine is the technological foundation of the Industrial Revolution's social and economic consequences. By making steam power more efficient and adaptable, Watt's engine enabled the factory system to concentrate in cities — triggering the urbanization, working-class formation, and global commodity demand that define Topics 5.9 and 5.10. Watt is not primarily a Topic 5.10 figure (he belongs to 5.3–5.5), but his engine's downstream effects are the subject of 5.10.

## Key States and Their Trajectories

### Britain — The First Industrializer

Britain industrialized first (c. 1760–1840) and established the global template. By the mid-19th century, Britain's economic dominance was built on its combination of coal, textile production, overseas trade networks, and naval power. Britain's political system responded to industrial social pressure through **reform bills** (1832, 1867, 1884) that gradually extended male suffrage and through **factory legislation** that regulated child labor and working conditions.

---

### Germany — State-Led Industrialization and the Welfare State

Germany industrialized rapidly after unification in 1871, driven by state investment in railways, heavy industry, and technical education. [[Concept Notes/Otto von Bismarck|Bismarck's]] political genius lay in combining industrial development with the world's first modern welfare state — making Germany both an economic powerhouse and a model for social policy reform.

---

### Japan — Meiji Modernization

Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] (1868) is the most dramatic case of state-sponsored industrialization outside Western Europe. The Meiji government:
- Abolished feudalism and the samurai class
- Built railways and telegraph networks
- Created a national education system
- Sent students abroad to study Western science and technology
- Adopted a Western-style constitution (1889)
- Built a navy and modern army

By the 1890s, Japan had become the only non-Western industrialized nation. This rapid transformation is a stark example of **change** driven by deliberate state policy.

> [!exam-tip]
> Japan vs. China is the most-tested comparison in this era. Japan succeeded because the Meiji government achieved consensus around total modernization. China's [[Concept Notes/Self-Strengthening Movement|Self-Strengthening Movement]] attempted to modernize technology without restructuring the political system — and failed. The difference was political will and social consensus, not resources.

---

### China — The Self-Strengthening Movement (c. 1861–1895)

Following military defeats by Western powers, some Chinese officials launched the Self-Strengthening Movement to modernize China's military and industry while preserving Confucian political and social structures ("Chinese learning as the foundation; Western learning for practical use"). The movement built modern arsenals and shipyards but was hampered by internal political resistance, lack of central coordination, and defeat in the Sino-Japanese War (1895). It represents a failed attempt at *selective* modernization.

---

### Egypt — Muhammad Ali's Reforms

Egyptian ruler Muhammad Ali (r. 1805–1848) launched ambitious state-sponsored industrialization: cotton cultivation for export, modern textile factories, a new military, and educational missions to Europe. However, British competition and political pressure from the Ottoman Empire and Britain undercut Egypt's industrialization. By mid-century, Egypt had become a cotton exporter to British mills rather than an industrial competitor — an early example of how Western industrial dominance could lock other regions into commodity-exporting roles.

## Key Movements

### Labor Union Movement

A bottom-up movement in which industrial workers organized collectively to negotiate with employers. Labor unions used strikes and collective bargaining to win concessions on wages, hours, and working conditions. The movement began in Britain, spread across Western Europe and the United States, and eventually led to the formation of **labor parties** that entered parliamentary politics.

**Causation:** Low wages, long hours, dangerous conditions, and the factory system's reduction of worker skill and autonomy created the conditions that made collective action both necessary and possible.

**Continuity vs. Change:** Workers had resisted exploitation before industrialization (peasant revolts, artisan guilds). But the labor union is a *new form* — a permanent, legally recognized institution that negotiates within the capitalist system rather than overthrowing it.

---

### Revolutions of 1848

A wave of liberal and nationalist uprisings across Europe. Driven by a combination of middle-class liberalism (demands for constitutions and press freedom) and working-class economic grievances, the revolutions shook governments from Paris to Budapest. Most were suppressed within a year, but they demonstrated the political power of the middle and working classes — and accelerated gradual reform in the decades that followed.

**Significance for CCOT:** The Revolutions of 1848 are evidence of both *change* (mass political mobilization challenging existing governments) and *continuity* (elites successfully suppressing most revolutions, maintaining existing power structures). They are best understood as a *hinge moment*: after 1848, governments could no longer ignore popular political demands entirely.
