# Continuity & Change in the Industrial Age

**AP Period 3 · Unit 5: Revolutions · Printed pp. 351–356**
**AP Themes:** ECN · GOV · SIO
**AP Skill:** Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 5.10 is the unit synthesis topic — it never appears as a standalone MCQ section but is the backbone of every LEQ and DBQ in Unit 5. The AP exam will ask you to evaluate the *extent* of change or continuity, so you must be ready to argue BOTH sides with specific evidence.

**Essential Question:** How did the Industrial Revolution demonstrate both continuity and change?

The Industrial Revolution (c. 1750–1900) produced economic, social, cultural, political, and environmental changes unseen since the Agricultural Revolution 10,000 years earlier. Yet it also preserved older hierarchies, inequalities, and power structures. The AP exam tests whether students can identify what *actually* changed versus what only appeared to change — and use evidence to argue a defensible extent claim.

---

## Economic Continuities and Changes

**What Changed:** Western Europe shifted from a mercantilist system (tightly regulated trade designed to maximize state wealth) to industrial capitalism (private companies competing for profit). [[Concept Notes/Capitalism|Capitalism]], championed by Adam Smith, replaced older guild and artisan production with factory mass production. Consumer goods became cheaper, more varied, and more widely available. A new class of wealthy industrialists — capitalists — overtook the old landowning aristocracy in wealth and influence.

Industrialization also spread unevenly beyond Western Europe. The United States, Russia, and Japan all expanded industrial output and railroad networks, with Japan and Egypt pursuing state-sponsored modernization. However, Western Europe and the United States maintained dominance of world manufacturing throughout the period.

**Share of Total World Manufacturing Output (%)**

| Region | 1750 | 1800 | 1860 | 1880 | 1900 |
|--------|------|------|------|------|------|
| Europe | 23.2 | 28.1 | 53.2 | 61.3 | 62.0 |
| United States | 0.1 | 0.8 | 7.2 | 14.7 | 23.6 |
| Japan | 3.8 | 3.5 | 2.6 | 2.4 | 2.4 |
| Rest of World | 73.0 | 67.7 | 36.6 | 20.9 | 11.0 |

*Source: Paul Kennedy, The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers, p. 149*

**What Stayed the Same:** Many regions continued their pre-industrial roles. Latin America and Africa remained suppliers of minerals and metals. South Asia, Egypt, and the Caribbean kept exporting raw cotton. Southeast Asia still supplied spices, while also becoming a source of rubber, tin, and timber. The raw-material periphery continued to serve the industrial core — a continuation of colonial economic relationships established in earlier periods.

> [!tip]
> The table is gold for LEQ evidence: the "Rest of World" share collapses from 73% → 11%, while Europe's triples. This is quantitative proof of the shift in global economic power — use it to argue *extent*.

## Social Continuities and Changes

**What Changed:** Before industrialization, Western Europe was predominantly rural and agricultural. Factories pulled workers into cities, creating two new social classes: the **industrial working class** (proletariat) and an expanded **middle class** (bourgeoisie). The working class labored for low wages in dangerous conditions; unlike agricultural laborers, they had no flexibility in scheduling and were completely dependent on factory owners for income. In response, workers formed [[Concept Notes/Labor Unions|Labor Unions]] and used strikes and collective bargaining to press for better wages, hours, and conditions (pp. 353–354).

The middle class expanded dramatically to include factory managers, bankers, insurance agents, and shipping merchants — alongside the traditional doctors, lawyers, and shopkeepers. At the top, industrial capitalists who made money from investments rather than land came to dominate social hierarchies.

**Non-Agricultural Workers as % of Workforce**

| Country | 1800 | 1850 | 1900 |
|---------|------|------|------|
| England | 68% | 78% | 84% |
| France | 41% | 57% | 69% |
| Italy | 42% | 56% | 67% |
| Poland | 44% | 53% | 58% |

**What Stayed the Same:** Despite women entering factory work in large numbers and their wages becoming essential to family survival, patriarchal structures endured. Women were consistently paid less than men for identical work and barred from high-wage positions. The right to vote was extended to working-class *men* in many industrialized countries, but women would not gain suffrage in most Western nations until the early 20th century. Rigid social orders based on economic and ethnic status also persisted.

> [!warning]
> A common mistake: students argue that industrialization "liberated" women. The better argument is that it *complicated* patriarchy — women's economic role became more visible and essential, yet legal and political subordination continued. That tension is the key continuity.

## Political Continuities and Changes

**What Changed:** The Enlightenment tradition of natural rights — inherited from the previous period — fueled political movements throughout the Industrial Age. Workers and urban residents demanded voting rights, press freedom, and limits on aristocratic privilege. The Revolutions of 1848 swept European cities: Parisians demanded press freedom, Berliners called for a parliament, and Hungarians sought independence from Austria. Over time, voting rights were gradually extended to city dwellers, non-landowners, and eventually the working class — though nearly always to men only. Labor parties emerged to advocate for minimum wages, shorter workdays, and social insurance.

Germany under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck enacted the most comprehensive worker protections: accident compensation insurance, unemployment insurance, and old-age pensions. These reforms were motivated in part by a desire to undercut socialist and radical movements — demonstrating that change often came from above to prevent change from below.

**What Stayed the Same:** Political inequality persisted. In regions where the middle class was small or weak, authoritarian governments survived. Nationalism — a force present since the Atlantic Revolutions — continued to drive both state-building and inter-state rivalry throughout the Industrial Age. International competition among nation-states, not a new phenomenon, intensified with industrialization as states competed for raw materials and markets.

> [!exam-tip]
> Bismarck's social reforms are ideal complexity evidence in an LEQ: they represent state-directed change *in response to* the threat of more radical change — a nuanced argument about causation and the limits of top-down reform.

## The Big CCOT Argument

The Industrial Revolution was transformative in degree but not always in kind. The *nature* of economic production changed fundamentally (from agrarian to industrial, from mercantilist to capitalist). But the *structure* of inequality — between rich and poor, between industrialized and non-industrialized regions, between men and women — persisted, and in some cases intensified. Students earning a 5 can argue that industrialization *reproduced* inequality in new forms rather than eliminating it.

**Key continuity phrase:** "despite significant economic transformation, social hierarchies based on class, gender, and regional power persisted — and in some cases widened."

**Key change phrase:** "the Industrial Revolution fundamentally restructured how goods were produced, who held economic power, and how states organized political participation."
