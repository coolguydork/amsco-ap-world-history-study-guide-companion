# Themes and Causation — Unit 5.10: Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age

#apwh #unit-5 #period-3

> This file synthesizes continuity and change across all six AP World History thematic lenses for Unit 5. Use it to prepare CCOT essays and the synthesis requirement of DBQs.

---

## Governance: Change and Continuity

**Changed:** The basis of political legitimacy shifted from divine right and hereditary succession to popular sovereignty and nationalist identity. The Atlantic Revolutions created constitutional republics and nation-states governed (in principle) by consent of the governed. The industrial era saw gradual expansion of suffrage and the beginnings of welfare-state governance. By 1900, absolute monarchy had retreated significantly in Western Europe and the Americas.

**Persisted:** Authoritarian governance remained dominant across much of the world — Russian and Chinese autocracy, Ottoman and Habsburg multiethnic empires, colonial rule over Africa and Asia. Even in democratic nations, suffrage was limited by race, gender, and property. The *form* of governance changed in some nations; the *distribution of power* changed more slowly.

> [!exam-tip]
> A strong CCOT governance argument: "While the Atlantic Revolutions established popular sovereignty as the new standard for political legitimacy, authoritarian governance persisted across most of the world through 1900 — demonstrating that the *principle* of popular sovereignty spread faster than its *practice*."

## Economic Systems: Change and Continuity

**Changed:** The energy source (coal/steam replacing human and animal power), the scale of production (industrial vs. artisanal), the organization of production (factory vs. cottage), and the geographic reach of economic integration (global commodity markets vs. regional trading networks) all changed fundamentally. The rate of economic growth accelerated dramatically.

**Persisted:** Capitalism itself — private ownership of productive property, market exchange, profit-seeking — continued from commercial to industrial forms. The core-periphery structure of global trade (industrial nations export manufactures; non-industrial regions supply raw materials) was established in the 16th century and deepened in the 19th. Extreme economic inequality persisted within and between nations.

## Cultural Developments: Change and Continuity

**Changed:** Secularization — the declining authority of religion in public political life — advanced in Western Europe. Nationalism replaced dynastic loyalty as the primary political identity in many nations. Scientific rationalism (germ theory, evolutionary biology, physics) challenged traditional religious explanations of natural phenomena. Consumer culture emerged as a new form of social identity.

**Persisted:** Religion remained a powerful force in personal and public life everywhere — even in France after the Revolution, Catholicism retained enormous cultural authority; in the USA, Protestant Christianity shaped both reform movements and industrial ideology (Social Gospel, Social Darwinism). The global dominance of Western cultural forms — Christian missions, Western education systems, European languages — actually *increased* during industrialization, spreading through colonialism.

## Social Structures: Change and Continuity

**Changed:** The specific class hierarchy changed — from aristocracy/peasantry to bourgeoisie/middle class/proletariat in industrializing nations. Legal abolition of slavery was achieved across the Atlantic World (Britain 1833, USA 1865, Brazil 1888). Women's legal position began to improve (property rights, access to some professions, beginnings of suffrage movements).

**Persisted:** Gender hierarchy — patriarchal control of political, economic, and cultural power — persisted throughout and beyond the period. Racial hierarchy persisted after formal emancipation, reconstituted as sharecropping, Jim Crow, and colonial racial stratification. Class inequality, though in new forms, was as extreme in 1900 as in 1750. Social mobility remained constrained for most people in most societies.

---

## Technology and Environment: Change and Continuity

**Changed:** The energy source fundamentally changed — from renewable biological sources (wood, animals, human muscle, wind, water) to non-renewable geological sources (coal, then petroleum). This change was genuinely unprecedented and had no parallel in prior human history. The *rate* of technological change accelerated dramatically.

**Persisted:** Agricultural technology remained primary for most of the world's population through 1900. The basic environmental challenge of human activity — soil depletion, water contamination, deforestation — continued from pre-industrial patterns; industrialization intensified but did not create these problems. The use of wind and water power persisted alongside steam in many applications.

---

## Human Migration: Change and Continuity

**Changed:** The *scale* of voluntary migration increased dramatically — ~55 million Europeans emigrated between 1820 and 1920; railroad and steamship technology made long-distance migration affordable for working-class people. The Atlantic slave trade ended (abolished by Britain 1807, USA 1808, Brazil 1850). New forms of unfree labor (indentured servitude) replaced chattel slavery.

**Persisted:** Forced and coerced labor continued — indentured workers from India and China replaced enslaved Africans on Caribbean and Pacific plantations. Migration driven by economic necessity (not choice) remained the dominant form. The geographic pattern of labor movement — from poor regions to rich regions — continued from the pre-industrial pattern.
