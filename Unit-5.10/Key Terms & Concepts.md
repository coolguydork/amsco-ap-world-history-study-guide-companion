# Topic 5.10 — Key Terms & Concepts

> [!tip]
> These terms frame the **CCOT analysis** of the Industrial Age. Master not just the definition but the *historical direction*: does this term represent something that changed, something that continued, or a tension between the two? That distinction is exactly what AP essays test.

---

## Economic Systems

### Capitalism
An economic system in which private individuals and companies own the means of production — factories, machines, land — and pursue profit in competitive markets with minimal government regulation. Capitalism replaced **mercantilism** as the dominant economic framework of the industrial era, most influentially argued by [[Concept Notes/Adam Smith|Adam Smith]] in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). Adam Smith's "invisible hand" theory held that individuals pursuing their own self-interest would naturally generate collective prosperity.

> [!exam-tip]
> Capitalism is the *change* that defines industrialization economically. The key shift is from **state-controlled trade** (mercantilism) to **private market competition** (capitalism). But continuities exist: poverty, inequality, and commodity trade persisted even as the system changed.

### Mercantilism
The pre-industrial economic system in which governments tightly regulated trade to maximize national wealth — typically by accumulating gold and silver through exports exceeding imports. European states maintained mercantilist policies through much of the early modern period (c. 1450–c. 1750). Industrialization and capitalism displaced mercantilism by making private market efficiency more profitable than state-managed trade.

*Continuity note:* Some mercantilist logic persisted — governments still used tariffs, colonial extraction, and trade policy to protect their industries even while proclaiming free-market ideals.

### Mass Production
The manufacture of identical goods in large quantities using machines and the division of labor, replacing artisan craft production. Mass production made consumer goods cheaper and more accessible, expanding who could afford manufactured items. It is the defining economic feature of the Industrial Revolution and the cause of both its prosperity and its labor exploitation.

### Division of Labor
The factory practice of breaking production into specialized, repetitive tasks — each worker performs one task rather than crafting a complete product. The division of labor increased efficiency but reduced the skill, creativity, and autonomy of workers. Combined with **interchangeable parts**, it made workers more replaceable, depressing wages through competition.

### Means of Production
[[Concept Notes/Karl Marx|Karl Marx]]'s term for the physical resources and infrastructure — factories, machines, raw materials, land — used to create goods. In capitalism, the bourgeoisie *own* the means of production; the proletariat own only their labor. Marx argued that true freedom required workers to collectively own the means of production.

### Laissez-Faire
French for "let it be" — the economic doctrine that governments should not interfere with markets. Championed by Adam Smith and later economists, laissez-faire held that government intervention distorted efficient markets. In practice, laissez-faire policies meant little regulation of wages, hours, child labor, or working conditions in the early industrial era — until reform movements forced legislation.

## Class and Social Structure

### Proletariat
[[Concept Notes/Karl Marx|Marx]]'s term for the industrial working class — those who own no means of production and must sell their labor to survive. The proletariat is not the same as the pre-industrial peasantry: peasants were tied to land; the proletariat was formally free but economically dependent on wage labor. Marx argued the proletariat was systematically exploited by the bourgeoisie and called on workers to unite and overthrow capitalism.

> [!warning]
> The proletariat is a **new social formation** created by industrialization, not a continuation of serfdom or peasant farming. This distinction matters on CCOT essays — the working class represents *change*, even if poverty itself represents *continuity*.

### Bourgeoisie
Marx's term for the capital-owning middle and upper-middle class — factory owners, merchants, bankers — who own the means of production and employ the proletariat. In common usage, "bourgeoisie" refers broadly to the middle class. Marx argued the bourgeoisie extracted **surplus value** from workers' labor to generate profit, making exploitation structural rather than personal.

### Labor Union (Trade Union)
An organized association of workers who negotiate collectively with employers over wages, hours, and working conditions. Labor unions used **strikes** (work stoppages) and **collective bargaining** (negotiating as a group) to win concessions from factory owners. They emerged as a direct response to the low wages, long hours, and dangerous conditions of industrial labor. Labor leaders also worked to form international unions, recognizing that workers across nations shared common interests.

> [!exam-tip]
> Labor unions are a key example of **change** in Topic 5.10: workers responding to new industrial conditions with a new organizational form. But they also demonstrate **continuity** — resistance to economic exploitation is as old as labor itself.

### Labor Party
A political party representing the interests of the working class in parliamentary systems. Labor parties emerged in the late 19th century in Britain, Germany, and elsewhere, advocating for minimum wages, shorter workdays (eight-hour day), paid sick and holiday leave, and health and unemployment insurance. Their emergence reflects the gradual expansion of voting rights to working-class men.

### White-Collar Worker
A middle-class worker performing administrative, managerial, or professional tasks — office managers, accountants, insurance agents, bankers, small business owners, lawyers, doctors. White-collar employment expanded dramatically with industrialization, as factories, banks, and trading companies required managers and administrators. This class is a major social *change* of the industrial era.

### Welfare State
A system in which the government provides basic social protections — unemployment insurance, accident compensation, old-age pensions, health insurance — for its citizens. The first comprehensive welfare state was initiated by German Chancellor [[Concept Notes/Otto von Bismarck|Otto von Bismarck]] in the 1880s, who introduced these programs to preempt more radical socialist demands. Bismarck's reforms became a model adopted by other industrializing nations.

## Political Concepts

### Natural Rights
The Enlightenment-era idea that all humans possess inherent rights — to life, liberty, and property — that governments cannot legitimately violate. Natural rights arguments powered not only the American and French Revolutions but also the reform movements of the Industrial Age: labor unions, suffrage campaigns, and the Revolutions of 1848 all invoked natural rights as justification for political change.

### Revolutions of 1848
A wave of liberal and nationalist uprisings across European cities in 1848 — Paris, Berlin, Vienna, and cities in Hungary, among others. Protesters demanded greater freedom of the press, representative parliaments, and national self-determination. The revolutions were mostly suppressed but demonstrated the power of middle-class and working-class political pressure, and accelerated gradual reform in subsequent decades.

- **Paris**: Demanded greater press freedom and republican government
- **Berlin**: Called for a parliament to check the monarch's power
- **Hungary**: Demanded freedom from Austrian imperial control

### Suffrage
The right to vote. In the industrial era, suffrage was gradually extended in Western industrial nations — but unevenly. Britain's Reform Bills of 1832, 1867, and 1884 extended voting rights to city-dwellers, non-landowners, and eventually working-class men. Women's suffrage in Western industrial countries came only in the early 20th century.

> [!exam-tip]
> Expanding suffrage is a political *change* of this era — but note the major *continuity*: women were excluded throughout this period. The suffrage expansion was for men only until the 20th century.

### Collective Bargaining
The process by which labor unions negotiate with employers on behalf of all workers, seeking better wages, hours, and conditions. Collective bargaining is the key mechanism through which unions translated worker solidarity into concrete economic gains.

## Global Economy

### Steam Ship / Steam Locomotive
Industrial-era transportation technologies that dramatically reduced the cost and time of moving goods and people. Steam ships transformed oceanic trade; steam locomotives opened interior regions previously inaccessible to large-scale commerce. Together, they enabled Western industrial states to access raw materials from Latin America, Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia at previously impossible scales.

### Telegraph
The electrical communication technology that allowed near-instantaneous long-distance communication, revolutionizing the coordination of global trade, colonial administration, and military operations. The telegraph compressed the world's communication geography — a message that took weeks by ship could now arrive in minutes.

### Raw Materials / Commodity Trade
The pre-industrial pattern of some regions exporting raw materials (cotton, rubber, metals, spices) to others continued into and deepened through the industrial era. Western industrial demand for raw materials was insatiable — and steam transportation made more regions accessible. Latin America and Africa supplied minerals and metals; South Asia and the Caribbean supplied cotton; Southeast Asia supplied rubber, tin, and timber. This flow deepened global inequalities by keeping non-industrial regions locked into commodity-exporting roles.
