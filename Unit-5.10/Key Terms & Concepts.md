# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 5.10 — Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age · Printed pp. 351–356**

> [!tip]
> This is a synthesis topic — no new terms are introduced in 5.10 itself. These terms recur from earlier Unit 5 topics and are the vocabulary the AP exam expects you to deploy in CCOT arguments, LEQs, and DBQs. Definitions here are exam-focused.

---

**Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT)**
The AP historical reasoning skill central to Topic 5.10. CCOT analysis requires identifying *what* changed, *what* stayed the same, and — critically — *to what extent* change occurred. Strong CCOT arguments avoid presenting change and continuity as absolutes; the best exam answers qualify both (e.g., "while the working class expanded, patriarchal gender norms persisted").

**Industrial Revolution**
The shift from agrarian, artisan-based production to mechanized factory manufacturing, beginning in Britain c. 1750 and spreading globally through 1900. The CCOT framing: the Industrial Revolution changed *how* goods were made and *who* held economic power, but did not eliminate inequality — it restructured it. Often compared to the Agricultural Revolution in scale of societal transformation.

**[[Concept Notes/Capitalism\|Capitalism]]**
An economic system in which private individuals or companies own the means of production and compete in markets for profit. Replaced [[Concept Notes/Mercantilism\|Mercantilism]] in Western Europe during the Industrial Age. Associated with Adam Smith (*Wealth of Nations*, 1776), who argued that private pursuit of profit produces general prosperity through market competition. Key change from the previous era's state-directed, trade-surplus-focused mercantilism.

**[[Concept Notes/Mercantilism\|Mercantilism]]**
The pre-industrial economic system in which states tightly regulated trade to accumulate wealth (gold and silver) and maintain a favorable balance of trade. Represents the economic *continuity* from earlier periods that industrialization disrupted — Western Europe's shift away from mercantilism toward free-market capitalism is one of the period's defining changes.

**Proletariat**
Karl Marx's term for the industrial working class — wage laborers who own only their labor power and must sell it to factory owners to survive. Marx argued in *The Communist Manifesto* (1848, co-authored with Friedrich Engels) that the proletariat was exploited by the bourgeoisie and should seize the means of production. The emergence of the proletariat as a distinct social class is a key *change* of industrialization.

**Bourgeoisie**
In Marxist analysis, the capitalist class that owns the means of production (factories, machinery, land) and employs the proletariat. In broader historical usage, the term refers to the expanding middle class of the industrial era — managers, merchants, bankers, lawyers — whose growth is a defining social change of the period. The bourgeoisie's political demands (voting rights, press freedom, parliamentary limits on monarchy) drove much of the era's political change.

**Means of Production**
The physical inputs (factories, machines, raw materials, land) used to create goods. Ownership of the means of production is the axis of Marxist class analysis: the bourgeoisie owns them, the proletariat does not. The question of who should control the means of production animated the era's major political and economic debates.

**Labor Unions**
Worker associations that used collective action — strikes, collective bargaining, slowdowns — to win better wages, shorter hours, and improved working conditions from factory owners. The formation of labor unions represents a social and political *change* of the Industrial Age, as workers organized across individual workplaces to challenge the power of industrial capitalists. See also [[Concept Notes/Labor Unions|Labor Unions]].

**Collective Bargaining**
The process by which labor unions negotiate wages, hours, and working conditions with employers on behalf of all workers in a workplace or industry. A key tool of the labor movement and a political change from earlier periods when workers had no formal mechanism to challenge employer authority.

**Factory System**
The organization of production around centralized, mechanized factories employing wage laborers — replacing the earlier putting-out system (cottage industry) and artisan guild production. Key change: skilled artisans working at their own pace were replaced by unskilled assembly-line workers performing repetitive tasks. Result: more consumer goods, cheaper prices, but deskilled labor and harsh working conditions.

**Working Class (Industrial)**
The wage-dependent laborers who emerged as a distinct social class during industrialization. Characterized by low pay, long hours, dangerous conditions, urban crowding, and complete dependence on factory employment. Represents a major *change* from the agricultural labor of the previous era. The working class's political mobilization — for voting rights, labor protections, and ultimately socialism — is a defining dynamic of the period.

**Middle Class (Industrial Expansion)**
The bourgeoisie in its broader sense: a growing social stratum that expanded during industrialization to include factory managers, bankers, insurance agents, and shipping merchants alongside traditional professionals (doctors, lawyers, shopkeepers). Their political interests — voting rights, legal equality, limits on aristocratic privilege — drove the era's democratic reforms. The growth of a large middle class is strongly correlated with the emergence of democratic governance.

**State-Sponsored Industrialization**
Government-directed efforts to build industrial capacity, as seen in Meiji Japan and Egypt under Muhammad Ali. Both states invested in infrastructure, imported Western technology, and reorganized military and economic institutions. Represents a *change* from laissez-faire Western European development and demonstrates that industrialization was not a single universal process — it took different forms based on political context.

**Otto von Bismarck's Social Reforms**
A suite of worker protections enacted in Germany under Chancellor Bismarck in the 1880s: accident insurance (1884), health insurance (1883), and old-age pensions (1889). The world's first modern welfare state legislation. Motivated in part by Bismarck's desire to undercut socialist movements by showing that the state could address workers' grievances. Spread as a model throughout Europe and eventually the world.

**Revolution of 1848**
A wave of uprisings across European cities in 1848, driven by demands for press freedom, parliamentary government, and national [[Concept Notes/Self-Determination\|self-determination]]. In Paris: press freedom. In Berlin: parliamentary limits on monarchy. In Hungary: independence from Austria. Demonstrates that the Enlightenment tradition of natural rights was a *continuity* driving political change well into the Industrial Age.

> [!exam-tip]
> For LEQs that ask about political change, the best evidence combines specific reform examples (Bismarck's welfare state, British Reform Acts extending suffrage) with the argument that change was often *partial* — voting rights went to men, not women; reforms came from above to prevent more radical change from below.
