# Topic 5.10 — Timeline & Chronology

> [!tip]
> This timeline tracks how the economic, social, and political dimensions of industrialization developed — and how responses to it evolved — across the 19th century. Use this to build CCOT arguments: identify what was *already changing* early in the century vs. what emerged only later, and trace how reform followed disruption with a lag.

---

## Chronological Overview: c. 1750–c. 1900

### 1776 — Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations*

[[Concept Notes/Adam Smith|Adam Smith]] publishes the foundational text of capitalist economics. His argument: private self-interest, guided by the market's "invisible hand," produces collective prosperity better than state-directed trade. This is the intellectual declaration that mercantilism is being replaced by capitalism as the dominant economic framework.

*Significance for CCOT:* The publication date (1776) marks the **beginning** of the intellectual shift from mercantilism to capitalism — a shift that takes most of the 19th century to fully materialize in policy and practice.

---

### c. 1780s–1830s — Western European Industrialization Accelerates

The factory system, steam power, and mechanized textile production transform Britain's economy. By the 1820s–1830s, industrialization begins spreading to Belgium, France, and parts of Germany and the United States. Urban populations surge. The working class — a new social formation — takes shape in the mill towns and mining districts of Britain and Western Europe.

---

### 1832, 1867, 1884 — Britain's Reform Bills

Three successive expansions of the voting franchise in Britain:
- **1832**: Extended suffrage to urban middle-class property owners; redistributed parliamentary seats from "rotten boroughs" to industrial cities
- **1867**: Extended suffrage to urban working-class men
- **1884**: Extended suffrage to rural working-class men

Taken together, these represent the gradual, parliamentary-route expansion of democracy in response to industrial-era social pressure. Women remained excluded until 1918 (partial) and 1928 (full).

*Significance for CCOT:* Political change was *real but incremental and male-only*. The Reform Bills show that industrial Britain responded to popular pressure through reform rather than revolution — a key contrast with France's pattern of revolution and restoration.

---

### 1839–1876 — Tanzimat Reforms (Ottoman Empire)

The Ottoman Empire's program of Westernizing reform — reorganizing the military, legal system, and administration along European lines. Like the [[Concept Notes/Self-Strengthening Movement|Self-Strengthening Movement]] in China, the Tanzimat attempted selective modernization without fundamental political restructuring. Both were ultimately insufficient to reverse imperial decline.

*Comparison:* The Tanzimat is similar to the Self-Strengthening Movement: both attempted to adopt Western *methods* while preserving traditional *power structures*. Both contrast with Japan's more thoroughgoing restructuring.

---

### 1845 — Engels, *The Condition of the Working Class in England*

Friedrich Engels documents the living and working conditions of Manchester's industrial workers with empirical precision — overcrowded tenements, contaminated water, child labor, malnutrition. The book provides the factual foundation for the Marxist critique and remains a landmark primary source for understanding industrial-era conditions.

---

### 1848 — *The Communist Manifesto* / Revolutions of 1848

Two simultaneous events that represent the crisis point of mid-19th-century industrial capitalism:

**The Communist Manifesto** (February 1848): [[Concept Notes/Karl Marx|Marx]] and Engels publish their call for proletarian revolution. "Workers of the world, unite! You have nothing to lose but your chains." The text diagnoses capitalism's exploitation of the proletariat and calls for collective ownership of the means of production.

**Revolutions of 1848** (February–December 1848): Liberal and nationalist uprisings sweep European cities — Paris, Berlin, Vienna, Budapest. Protesters demand constitutions, press freedom, representative parliaments, and national self-determination. Most are suppressed within months, but they demonstrate the political power of the middle and working classes.

*Significance for CCOT:* 1848 is a hinge year. It shows that industrial-era political demands had reached a threshold — governments could no longer ignore them. Even though most revolutions failed, they accelerated gradual reform in subsequent decades.

---

### c. 1850s–1870s — Industrialization Spreads to US and Germany

The United States becomes the world's largest economy by the end of the 19th century. Germany industrializes rapidly after unification (1871), building heavy industry in the Ruhr Valley. Russia begins industrializing with state investment in railways. This period marks the end of British industrial dominance — the beginning of a multipolar industrial world.

---

### 1861–1895 — China's Self-Strengthening Movement

Following military defeats by Britain and France, Chinese reformers push for selective modernization — building arsenals, shipyards, and telegraph networks while preserving Confucian political structures. The movement lacks central coordination and faces consistent conservative resistance at the Qing court. It ends effectively with China's defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War (1895) — humiliatingly lost to a smaller nation that had only begun modernizing in 1868.

---

### 1868 — Meiji Restoration (Japan)

The most dramatic case of state-led industrialization in the industrial era. The Meiji government:
- Abolished feudalism and the samurai class
- Built railways and telegraph networks across Japan
- Created a national education system
- Sent the Iwakura Mission to study Western institutions (1871–73)
- Adopted a constitution (1889) modeled partly on Germany's
- Built a modern conscript army and navy

By the 1890s, Japan had become the only non-Western industrialized nation — and demonstrated its industrial and military power by defeating China (1895) and Russia (1904–05).

*Significance for CCOT:* Japan's Meiji Restoration shows that industrialization was not exclusively a Western European phenomenon — but required comprehensive state commitment to restructuring every dimension of society.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Meiji Restoration begins in **1868** — not the 1850s (when Perry's ships arrived) or the 1870s. Know this date. Also know that Japan retained some cultural continuity (emperor system, social hierarchy) alongside dramatic economic and political change.

---

### 1880s — Bismarck's Social Reforms (Germany)

German Chancellor [[Concept Notes/Otto von Bismarck|Otto von Bismarck]] introduces the world's first comprehensive welfare state:
- **1883**: Health insurance for workers
- **1884**: Accident compensation insurance
- **1889**: Old-age and disability pensions

Bismarck's motivation was explicitly conservative: preempt socialist revolution by giving workers a stake in the existing system. The strategy worked — Germany's socialist movement remained parliamentary rather than revolutionary.

*Significance for CCOT:* The welfare state is the era's key **political change** — a new role for government in protecting workers from capitalism's worst effects. It spread across the industrializing world over the next half-century.

---

### Late 19th Century — Labor Parties Enter Parliaments

As voting rights extended to working-class men, labor parties formed across Western Europe: the British Labour Party (formally 1900), the German Social Democratic Party (1875), and others. These parties advocate for minimum wages, eight-hour workdays, paid leave, and social insurance — translating the labor union movement's demands into legislative programs.

---

### c. 1800–1900 — Raw Material Extraction Deepens Globally

Throughout the century, Western industrial demand for cotton, rubber, metals, and timber intensifies. Steam ships and locomotives open previously inaccessible regions. Latin America and Africa supply minerals; South Asia and the Caribbean supply cotton; Southeast Asia supplies rubber, tin, and timber. This global commodity flow locks non-industrial regions into raw material roles and deepens global economic inequality — the structural foundation for 19th-century imperialism (Topic 6).
