# Topic 5.10 — Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 5.10 is the **CCOT capstone** of Unit 5. The AP exam will test your ability to argue *what changed, what stayed the same, and why* across the economic, social, and political dimensions of industrialization — and to compare outcomes across different world regions. This is one of the highest-yield topics for LEQ prompts.

---

## Essential Question

**How did the Industrial Revolution demonstrate both continuity and change in economic, social, and political life between c. 1750 and c. 1900?**

## Overview: The Big Picture

The Industrial Revolution was the most transformative economic event since the [[Concept Notes/Agricultural Revolution|Agricultural Revolution]] more than 10,000 years earlier. It changed how goods were produced, how people earned their living, and how businesses were structured. Yet it did not erase the past — many social hierarchies, political structures, and global trade patterns *continued*, even as they were reshaped by industrial forces.

Topic 5.10 asks you to hold two ideas at once: the Industrial Revolution was revolutionary *and* it preserved important continuities. The skill of identifying what changed, what didn't, and why separates a 3 from a 5 on the AP exam.

## Economic Continuities and Changes

### What Changed: From Artisan to Factory

Before industrialization, skilled artisans crafted complete products — a cobbler made shoes from start to finish, a weaver produced cloth from the loom. The factory system's **division of labor** and **interchangeable parts** shattered this model. Workers performed a single repetitive task on an assembly line, never seeing a complete product. Skill requirements dropped; wages followed.

The result: **mass production** made goods cheaper, more uniform, and more accessible to more people than ever before. Consumer culture expanded alongside production.

### What Changed: From Mercantilism to Capitalism

Western Europe shifted from a **mercantilist** economic system — in which governments tightly regulated trade to accumulate national wealth — to a **capitalist** system, in which private companies pursued profit with minimal government interference. [[Concept Notes/Adam Smith|Adam Smith]]'s *Wealth of Nations* (1776) provided the intellectual framework: the invisible hand of the market, he argued, would channel private self-interest into general prosperity.

### What Continued: Poverty and Inequality

Capitalism produced enormous aggregate wealth — but its distribution was deeply uneven. The working class lived in squalid conditions and worked brutal hours. [[Concept Notes/Karl Marx|Karl Marx]] argued this was not accidental but structural: the **bourgeoisie** (capital-owning class) could only profit by extracting surplus value from the **proletariat** (wage-working class). He called on workers to unite, seize the means of production, and build a classless society.

## Industrialization Around the World

Not all regions industrialized at the same pace or with the same results. This global variation is central to AP comparison questions.

| Region | Trajectory | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Britain** | First industrializer | Factory system, railways, global trade dominance |
| **Western Europe** | Follow-on (France, Belgium, Germany) | Steam power, heavy industry, Bismarck's welfare state |
| **United States** | Rapid 19th-century industrialization | Railroads, steel, interchangeable parts |
| **Russia** | State-directed industrialization | Trans-Siberian Railroad; serfs as labor force |
| **Japan** | State-sponsored modernization via [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] | Rapid industrialization from 1868; copied Western models |
| **Egypt** | Limited state-sponsored modernization | Muhammad Ali's reforms; undercut by British competition |
| **China** | Self-Strengthening Movement | Partial, ultimately unsuccessful modernization |
| **India / Latin America / Africa** | Raw material suppliers | Western demand undercut domestic industrialization |

> [!exam-tip]
> Japan is the most exam-tested non-Western comparison case. Know that the **Meiji Restoration** (1868) drove Japan's industrialization through *state initiative* — abolishing feudalism, building railways, sending students abroad, adopting a constitution. Contrast this with China's **Self-Strengthening Movement**, which attempted to modernize military technology without restructuring the political system — and ultimately failed.

## Raw Materials and Global Trade

Industrialization created insatiable demand for raw materials. New transportation technologies — the **steam ship** and **steam locomotive** — made previously inaccessible regions exploitable. The **telegraph** enabled the coordination of these far-flung supply chains.

Key raw material flows:
- **Cotton**: Egypt, South Asia, Caribbean → British textile mills
- **Rubber, tin, timber**: Southeast Asia → industrial manufacturing
- **Minerals and metals**: Latin America, Africa → European industry
- **Coal**: mined domestically in Britain, exported knowledge of mining globally

This global supply network was not new — the transatlantic trade of Period 2 had already linked regions. But its scale and speed were transformed by steam technology. And its consequence was stark: Western European and U.S. industrial demand **undercut** early industrialization attempts in China, Egypt, and India by flooding their markets with cheaper manufactured goods.

## Social Continuities and Changes

### What Changed

- **New working class** emerged: propertyless urban wage laborers dependent on factory jobs
- **Middle class expanded**: beyond merchants/professionals to include office managers, bank clerks, insurance agents
- **Industrial capitalists displaced landed aristocracy** as the dominant social group
- **Labor unions** formed: workers organized collectively (strikes, collective bargaining) for better wages and conditions
- **Labor parties** emerged by the late 19th century, advocating minimum wages, shorter workdays, and social insurance

### What Continued

- Women were paid less than men for equivalent work and denied high-wage jobs
- Rigid social hierarchies — now based on capital rather than land — persisted
- Working-class poverty and poor living conditions continued throughout the 19th century, even as average wages slowly rose
- Racial and ethnic hierarchies within and across nations remained entrenched

## Political Continuities and Changes

The [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|Enlightenment]]'s language of natural rights continued to drive political demands into the industrial era. Workers and middle-class citizens alike claimed the rights to petition, protest, and vote.

- **Revolutions of 1848**: Uprisings across European cities — Paris, Berlin, Hungarian cities — demanded press freedom, representative parliaments, and national self-determination. Most were suppressed, but they forced governments to take popular demands more seriously.
- **Gradual expansion of male suffrage**: Britain's Reform Bills of 1832, 1867, and 1884 extended voting rights to city-dwellers, non-landowners, and the working class — but men only. Women gained voting rights only in the early 20th century.
- **Bismarck's social reforms**: To preempt socialist radicalism, German Chancellor [[Concept Notes/Otto von Bismarck|Otto von Bismarck]] introduced workers' accident compensation, unemployment insurance, and old-age pensions — the first modern welfare state.
- **Labor parties**: Representing the working class in parliaments, these parties pushed for minimum wages, paid leave, and health insurance.

## The CCOT Framework: A Summary

| Dimension | What Changed | What Continued |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Artisan → factory; mercantilism → capitalism; mass production | Poverty; unequal distribution of wealth; global commodity trade |
| **Social** | New working class; expanded middle class; industrialists replace aristocracy | Women's subordination; rigid class hierarchy (new form); rural patterns outside industrial zones |
| **Political** | Gradual expansion of voting rights; labor parties; welfare state emerges | Authoritarian governments in many regions; women excluded from politics; aristocratic influence in many states |
| **Global** | Steam-powered trade networks; Western industrial dominance | Raw material regions remain commodity exporters; colonial relationships persist |
