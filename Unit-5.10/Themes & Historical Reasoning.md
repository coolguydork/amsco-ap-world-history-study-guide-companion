# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Topic 5.10 — Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age · Printed pp. 351–356**
**AP Themes:** ECN · GOV · SIO | **AP Skills:** CCOT · Argumentation

---

## AP Theme Analysis

### ECN — Economy (Primary Theme)

The Industrial Revolution's economic impact was the period's most dramatic departure from the past. The shift from **mercantilism to capitalism** restructured who controlled economic production and how wealth was generated. Private companies replaced state-regulated trading monopolies; factory systems replaced artisan guilds; wage labor replaced subsistence farming as the primary labor relation in industrializing countries.

Yet the global economic hierarchy that emerged preserved older patterns of dominance: Western European and American industrial cores continued to extract raw materials from Latin American, African, and South Asian peripheries — a structural *continuity* from the colonial era. The data is unambiguous: the "Rest of World" share of global manufacturing output collapsed from 73% (1750) to 11% (1900), while Europe's tripled. This is economic *change* in who produces and economic *continuity* in who benefits.

**Key ECN CCOT argument:** Industrialization changed the *mechanism* of global economic inequality (from mercantilist extraction to capitalist market dominance) while *continuing* the structural reality of core-periphery inequality.

### GOV — Governance (Secondary Theme)

Political change in the Industrial Age followed a mixed pattern. On one hand, demands for natural rights — rooted in Enlightenment ideology — pushed governments toward democratic reform: expanded suffrage, parliamentary power, labor protections, and constitutional limits on monarchy. The Revolutions of 1848 and subsequent reform legislation in Britain, Germany, and France all reflect genuine political change.

On the other hand, these reforms were partial and often elite-managed. Voting rights were extended first to property-owning men, then to all men — but not to women. Bismarck's welfare state was explicitly designed to *prevent* more radical socialist change. Autocratic governments survived in regions where the middle class remained weak. The *pattern* of reform-from-above to forestall revolution-from-below is itself a political *continuity* dating to earlier periods.

**Key GOV CCOT argument:** Democratic political forms expanded, but the underlying goal of managing class conflict while preserving state authority represents continuity in how governments responded to social unrest.

### SIO — Social Interactions & Organization (Secondary Theme)

Social structures were profoundly reorganized by industrialization — but not leveled. The old rural peasantry gave way to an urban industrial working class; the old merchant middle class expanded to include industrial managers and professionals; the old landowning aristocracy was surpassed by industrial capitalists. These are real *changes* in the composition and relative power of social classes.

However, the *existence* of hierarchy — and of its gendered dimensions — persisted. Women entered industrial workplaces in large numbers, but received lower wages than men, were excluded from skilled positions, and lacked political rights throughout the period. The two-class dynamic (rich industrialists vs. poor workers) that Marx diagnosed was new in form but continuous in principle from earlier patron-client hierarchies.

**Key SIO CCOT argument:** The *agents* of inequality changed (industrial capitalists replaced feudal lords) but inequality itself — structured by class, gender, and ethnicity — persisted as a fundamental social continuity.

## Historical Reasoning: CCOT in Practice

### Structuring a CCOT Argument

The AP rubric requires:
1. **Accurate thesis** — makes a defensible claim with a line of reasoning about change AND continuity
2. **Contextualization** — situates the argument in broader historical context (Agricultural Revolution, Enlightenment, Atlantic Revolutions)
3. **Evidence** — at least two specific pieces of relevant evidence
4. **Historical reasoning** — explicitly uses CCOT to frame the argument
5. **Complexity** (for the top score) — explains nuance, qualification, or contradiction

### Sample Thesis (Strong)

*"Between 1750 and 1900, the Industrial Revolution fundamentally transformed economic production and class composition in Western Europe and the United States by replacing artisan production with factory wage labor and elevating industrial capitalists above the old aristocracy; yet despite these changes, structural inequalities based on class, gender, and regional position persisted and in some cases intensified, as women remained politically subordinate and non-industrialized regions were reduced to raw material suppliers for the industrial core."*

**Why it works:** makes specific claims about both change (factory system, class composition) and continuity (gender, regional inequality), and signals an extent argument.

### Sample Thesis (Weak — Avoid)

*"The Industrial Revolution changed many things but also kept many things the same."*

**Why it fails:** no specific claims, no line of reasoning, no extent language.

## Historiography: Debating the Industrial Age

**Progressive Interpretation:** Early 20th-century historians celebrated industrialization as progress — rising GDP, falling consumer prices, expanding political participation. This view emphasizes *change* as improvement.

**Pessimistic / Marxist Interpretation:** Historians influenced by Marx emphasized the costs to workers: deskilling, urban squalor, exploitation, and widening class inequality. This view emphasizes continuity of exploitation in new industrial forms.

**World-Systems Theory (Wallerstein):** Argues that industrialization deepened a global core-periphery divide that had existed since the 16th century. Western Europe and the United States became the industrial core; Latin America, Africa, and Asia became the periphery — locked into raw material dependency. This view sees industrialization as a continuity in global inequality, changing only its mechanism.

**Gender Historians:** Emphasize that industrialization created contradictions for women — greater economic visibility but continued legal subordination — and that the "domestic sphere" ideology (women as wives and mothers) was partly a *product* of industrialization, not simply a pre-industrial holdover.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP DBQ often includes documents that represent multiple of these perspectives. Grouping documents by historiographical lens (progressive vs. critical vs. gender) can produce a sophisticated argument and earn the complexity point.

## CCOT Evidence Bank

Use these in LEQ body paragraphs:

**Evidence of Change:**
- Manufacturing output shifts (table: Europe 23% → 62%; Rest of World 73% → 11%)
- Shift from mercantilism to capitalism (Adam Smith, laissez-faire)
- Emergence of industrial working class and middle class
- Bismarck's welfare state legislation (accident/health/unemployment insurance)
- Extension of male suffrage in Britain, France, Germany
- State-sponsored industrialization in Japan and Egypt
- Labor unions and collective bargaining rights

**Evidence of Continuity:**
- Women denied suffrage throughout the period
- Women paid less than men for identical work
- Non-industrialized regions remain raw material exporters
- Rigid social hierarchies persist (just with different actors at top)
- Nationalism and inter-state rivalry continue from earlier periods
- Patriarchal gender ideology ("good wives and mothers") continues in Japan and Argentina (DBQ docs)

**Evidence of Complexity (Both/And):**
- Women entered factories (change in economic role) but remained legally subordinate (continuity in political status)
- Bismarck's welfare state expanded social protections (change) to prevent socialist revolution (continuity of conservative governance)
- Japan industrialized successfully (change in Japan's global position) but its workers faced the same exploitative conditions as Western counterparts (continuity of labor exploitation)
