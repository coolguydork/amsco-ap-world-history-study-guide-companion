# Topic 5.10 — Practice & Review

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 5.10 appears in all four question types on the AP exam: MCQ (frequently with economic data, political cartoons, or reformer quotations), SAQ (causation and comparison prompts about industrialization's global spread and political responses), and as evidence in DBQ/LEQ responses on CCOT in the industrial era. The Japan vs. China comparison, Bismarck's welfare state, and the capitalism vs. Marxism tension are the highest-yield evidence items for essays.

---

## Multiple Choice Practice

### Question 1

*"Capital is, therefore, not a personal, it is a social power."*

*— Friedrich Engels and Karl Marx, The Communist Manifesto, 1848*

Which of the following best describes the argument Marx and Engels were making in this passage?

A) Individual capitalists are morally corrupt and choose to exploit workers out of greed
B) Capital accumulation is a natural and inevitable feature of all human societies
C) The capitalist system structurally compels owners to treat workers as resources rather than individuals
D) Workers must be educated about capital before they can organize effectively

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Marx's key insight was structural, not moral: he did not claim capitalists were personally evil. He argued that the *system* compelled them to minimize labor costs and maximize profit — or be outcompeted. "Capital is a social power" means capital ownership gives a class structural power over others, regardless of any individual's intentions. Option A misrepresents Marx's structural argument as a personal moral critique. Option B describes a Smithian view of markets, not Marxism. Option D has nothing to do with the passage's argument.

### Question 2

The table below shows Share of Total World Manufacturing Output (Percentage) for selected economies.

| Country | 1750 | 1800 | 1860 | 1900 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Britain | 1.9% | 4.3% | 19.9% | 18.5% |
| United States | — | 0.8% | 7.2% | 23.6% |
| Germany | 2.9% | 3.5% | 4.9% | 13.2% |
| China | 32.8% | 33.3% | 19.7% | 6.2% |
| India | 24.5% | 19.7% | 8.6% | 1.7% |

*(Source: Adapted from Paul Kennedy, The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers)*

Which of the following best explains the trends shown in the table?

A) China and India voluntarily shifted their economies away from manufacturing toward agriculture
B) Western industrialization, combined with colonial policies, undercut manufacturing in China and India while Western industrial output surged
C) China and India lacked the natural resources necessary to maintain manufacturing output
D) The Industrial Revolution was primarily a technological process that spread evenly across all regions once the technology became available

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The table shows the defining pattern of 19th-century industrialization: Western European and American manufacturing output surged while China and India declined dramatically — from together accounting for over half of world manufacturing in 1750 to a combined small fraction by 1900. This was not voluntary (Option A) or resource-driven (Option C — both China and India have abundant resources). Option D is contradicted by the data itself. The AMSCO text states explicitly that Western industrial states "undercut early industrialization in Egypt, China, and India" by exploiting their raw materials and flooding their markets with cheap manufactured goods.

### Question 3

Otto von Bismarck's introduction of workers' accident insurance, unemployment insurance, and old-age pensions in Germany in the 1880s was primarily motivated by which of the following?

A) A genuine commitment to socialist principles of collective ownership
B) Pressure from German labor unions that had achieved parliamentary majorities
C) A conservative strategy to reduce workers' grievances before socialist movements could exploit them
D) The influence of Adam Smith's laissez-faire economic principles on German policy

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Bismarck was an arch-conservative who despised socialism. His welfare state was a calculated defensive measure: give workers enough security that they would not turn to radical socialist movements demanding revolution. The strategy was effective — Germany's Social Democratic Party remained parliamentary rather than revolutionary. Option A is the opposite of Bismarck's intent. Option B is wrong — German labor unions did not hold parliamentary majorities in the 1880s. Option D is the opposite of Bismarck's approach: laissez-faire opposed state intervention, while Bismarck actively intervened.

### Question 4

Japan's Meiji Restoration (1868) and China's Self-Strengthening Movement (1861–1895) were similar in that both:

A) Achieved successful industrialization and military modernization by 1900
B) Preserved existing power structures while selectively adopting Western technology
C) Responded to the threat of Western dominance by attempting to modernize their societies
D) Relied primarily on private entrepreneurs rather than government direction

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> Both Japan and China were responding to the same stimulus: the military and economic superiority demonstrated by Western powers (Britain's Opium War victories in China; Perry's "black ships" in Japan). Both recognized they needed to modernize or face subjugation. The difference was in *how completely* they responded. Option A is wrong — China's Self-Strengthening Movement failed. Option B applies to China's approach but not Japan's: the Meiji government abolished feudalism, restructured government, and transformed the political system — it did not merely preserve existing structures. Option D is wrong for both — both involved significant state direction.

### Question 5

According to the AMSCO text, which factor best explains why democracy was more likely to emerge in industrializing nations than in non-industrialized ones?

A) Industrial workers were better educated and more politically aware than rural peasants
B) Industrial nations had access to Enlightenment philosophical texts that promoted democracy
C) The size and economic significance of the middle class in industrial nations created pressure for representative government
D) Democratic governments provided better conditions for industrial investment than authoritarian ones

> [!tip] Answer: **C**
> AMSCO states directly: "In countries where the middle class was large and economically significant, democracy emerged. However, in regions where the middle class was small or insignificant, dictatorships remained in place." This is the AMSCO's explicit structural explanation for democracy's uneven spread. Option A overstates worker education. Option B applies to all European societies and doesn't explain variation. Option D gets the causation backwards — the text argues middle-class size explains democracy, not that democracy drives industrialization.

## Short-Answer Questions (SAQ)

### SAQ 1 — Causation (Global Industrialization)

*Use the following information to answer parts a, b, and c.*

> Japan's Meiji government (post-1868) sent the Iwakura Mission to study Western institutions in 1871–73, built a national railway network, created a national education system, abolished the samurai class, and adopted a constitution modeled partly on Germany's. By 1895, Japan defeated China militarily; by 1905, it defeated Russia. Meanwhile, China's Self-Strengthening Movement built arsenals and shipyards but resisted fundamental political restructuring. China was defeated by Japan in 1895.

**a)** Briefly describe ONE specific historical development that contributed to Japan's successful industrialization under the Meiji government.

**b)** Briefly explain ONE reason why China's Self-Strengthening Movement was less successful than Japan's Meiji Restoration in modernizing the state.

**c)** Briefly explain ONE way that the global spread of industrialization in the 19th century deepened economic inequality between industrializing and non-industrializing regions.

> [!tip] Model Responses
>
> **a)** The Meiji government's decision to abolish feudalism — including the samurai class and the domain (han) system — removed the institutional barriers that had divided Japan politically and economically. By centralizing authority under the emperor and creating a unified national government, the Meiji oligarchs could coordinate railway construction, educational reform, and military modernization across the entire country, rather than working through competing regional lords. This political restructuring was the precondition for all subsequent economic modernization.
>
> **b)** China's Self-Strengthening Movement was hampered by the Qing court's internal divisions — conservatives, led by figures like Empress Dowager Cixi, blocked reforms that would have undermined their political power. While Japan's Meiji oligarchs reached consensus that total Westernization was necessary for survival, China's reformers could never overcome the court's resistance to political restructuring. The Self-Strengthening Movement's guiding ideology — "Chinese learning as foundation; Western learning for practical use" — explicitly rejected the political transformation that Japan embraced, and left the Qing state unable to coordinate or fund comprehensive modernization.
>
> **c)** Western industrial states' insatiable demand for raw materials transformed regions like South Asia, Egypt, Latin America, and Africa into commodity exporters whose economies were organized around supplying cotton, rubber, metals, and timber to Western factories. Cheap Western manufactured goods simultaneously flooded these markets, undercutting local manufacturing. Egypt, for example, had attempted to build a modern textile industry under Muhammad Ali in the early 19th century, but British competition and political pressure ended that effort — and Egypt became a cotton exporter to British mills instead. This dynamic trapped non-industrial regions in raw-material roles, concentrating manufacturing wealth in Western Europe and the United States.

### SAQ 2 — Comparison (Political Responses to Industrialization)

**a)** Briefly describe ONE similarity between the political demands of the Revolutions of 1848 and the demands of 19th-century labor unions.

**b)** Briefly describe ONE difference between Bismarck's approach to workers' demands and the approach advocated by Karl Marx.

**c)** Briefly explain ONE way that the gradual expansion of voting rights in Britain in the 19th century represented both change and continuity.

> [!tip] Model Responses
>
> **a)** Both the revolutionaries of 1848 and labor unions drew on Enlightenment natural rights language to argue that workers and citizens deserved political voice and economic protection. The 1848 revolutionaries in Paris demanded freedom of the press and representative government; Berlin protesters called for a parliament; labor unions demanded the right to organize and bargain collectively. Both movements invoked the principle that workers and citizens possessed inherent rights that governments and employers were obligated to respect — applying the same intellectual framework that had powered the American and French Revolutions to the new problems of industrial society.
>
> **b)** Bismarck sought to preserve capitalism and the existing social order by giving workers basic protections — health insurance, accident compensation, old-age pensions — so they would not be attracted to revolutionary socialist movements. His welfare state was a conservative strategy: change specific policies to prevent change to the system. Marx, by contrast, argued that capitalism itself was the problem — no amount of reform could eliminate the structural exploitation built into the system. Workers would need to seize the means of production and abolish private ownership to achieve genuine freedom. Bismarck wanted to save capitalism; Marx wanted to end it.
>
> **c)** The Reform Bills of 1832, 1867, and 1884 represented genuine change: they extended voting rights to urban property owners (1832), urban working-class men (1867), and rural working-class men (1884), creating a much more representative electorate than had existed before industrialization. This was a real departure from the pre-industrial system of very limited, property-restricted suffrage. At the same time, the bills represented significant continuity: women were completely excluded from all three reforms and would not receive voting rights until 1918 (partial) and 1928 (full). The pattern of gradual, male-only suffrage expansion shows that political change was real but incomplete — changing who counted as a citizen while maintaining women's political exclusion.

## Long Essay Question (LEQ) Guidance

### Sample Prompt 1

*Evaluate the extent to which the Industrial Revolution produced significant changes in economic systems in the period c. 1750–c. 1900.*

**Thesis strategy:** Argue for *significant but uneven* economic change. The shift from mercantilism to capitalism, from artisan craft to mass production, and from land-based to capital-based wealth represent major structural transformations (significant change). However, economic inequality persisted, global commodity trade continued earlier patterns, and non-Western regions experienced deindustrialization rather than transformation (significant continuity). Complexity: the degree of change varied by region — profound in Western Europe and Japan; experienced as disruption rather than development in China, India, and Egypt.

**Evidence to use:**
- Adam Smith and capitalism replacing mercantilism
- Mass production: division of labor, interchangeable parts, cheaper goods
- Industrial capitalists displacing the landed aristocracy
- Working-class poverty persisting even as aggregate wealth grew
- Karl Marx's structural critique of capitalist inequality
- Manufacturing output data: Britain/US rising; China/India declining
- Japan's state-led industrialization via Meiji Restoration
- Egypt and India undercut by British competition
- Global raw material flows: Latin America, Africa, South Asia as suppliers

**Complexity point:** Argue that the Industrial Revolution produced *different types of economic change* depending on position in the global economy: transformative industrialization in Western Europe and Japan; structural deindustrialization in South and East Asia; commodity-export intensification in Africa and Latin America. This global variation shows that "the Industrial Revolution" had different meanings depending on where you stood in the global economy.

### Sample Prompt 2

*Compare the responses to industrialization by TWO of the following states in the period c. 1750–c. 1900: Japan, China, the Ottoman Empire, Egypt.*

> [!tip]
> **Best pairing: Japan vs. China.** This comparison has the most AMSCO support, the clearest evidence of contrast, and the clearest causal explanation for different outcomes. The Meiji Restoration vs. Self-Strengthening Movement is the paradigm case for why political will and structural reform determined success or failure. Avoid a pairing you know less well — depth beats breadth on AP essays.
>
> **If using Ottoman Empire:** Compare the Tanzimat (1839–1876) with Japan's Meiji reforms. Both were state-directed; both faced internal resistance. The key difference: Japan abolished its feudal political structure while the Tanzimat tried to modernize within the Ottoman imperial framework. Japan succeeded; the Ottoman Empire continued to decline.

## Common Misconceptions — Watch Out!

> [!warning]
>
> **Misconception 1:** "Adam Smith and Karl Marx were both economists who agreed on how capitalism worked."
> Reality: Smith and Marx shared the observation that capitalism drove production efficiently. Their disagreement was fundamental: Smith thought markets naturally distributed benefits broadly; Marx thought markets structurally concentrated wealth at the top while exploiting workers. They are intellectual opponents, not variations on the same theme.

> [!warning]
>
> **Misconception 2:** "The Meiji Restoration was Japan's Industrial Revolution."
> Reality: The Meiji Restoration was a political transformation that *enabled* Japan's industrialization. The restoration (1868) abolished feudalism and centralized state authority; industrialization then followed over the subsequent decades. Don't conflate the political event with the economic process it made possible.

> [!warning]
>
> **Misconception 3:** "Bismarck was a socialist because he created the welfare state."
> Reality: Bismarck was an arch-conservative who despised socialism. He created the welfare state specifically to *prevent* socialist revolution — to give workers enough security that they would stay loyal to the existing political system. The welfare state was a conservative tool for social stability, not a concession to socialist ideology.

> [!warning]
>
> **Misconception 4:** "The Revolutions of 1848 succeeded in establishing democracies."
> Reality: Almost all the 1848 revolutions were suppressed within a year. Conservative and aristocratic forces regrouped and reasserted control in France, Prussia, Austria, and Hungary. The significance of 1848 is not immediate success but the demonstration that popular political pressure existed — and the acceleration of gradual reform in subsequent decades.

> [!warning]
>
> **Misconception 5:** "China failed to industrialize because it was technologically backward."
> Reality: China had the world's largest manufacturing output as recently as 1800 (see the table in MCQ Question 2). China's decline was caused by political factors — conservative resistance to restructuring, internal court divisions, and British imperial pressure — not lack of technological knowledge or resources. The Self-Strengthening Movement's failure was political, not technical.
