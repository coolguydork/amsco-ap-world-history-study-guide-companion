# Practice & Review

**Topic 5.10 — Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age · Printed pp. 359–363**

---

## Thesis Workshop (p. 359)

The following sample thesis addresses the Egypt/Japan state-sponsored industrialization comparison:

**Strong thesis example:**
*"Between 1750 and 1900, the governments of both Muhammad Ali in Egypt and the Meiji regime in Japan employed state-sponsored programs of industrialization to help them create a European-style military, but a difference in the nature and purpose of each program's products led to significantly differing courses."*

**Why it works:** Historically defensible claim ✓ · Embeds a line of reasoning (similar cause, different outcomes) ✓ · Addresses extent ("significantly differing") ✓

---

## Thesis Revision Exercise

**LEQ Prompt:** Develop an argument that explains the extent to which industrialization changed social relations between 1750 and 1900.

**Weak thesis to revise:**
*"From 1750 to 1900, industrialization changed the way goods were produced and consumed and affected people around the globe."*

**Problems:** No line of reasoning, no specific claim about *social relations*, no extent argument, too vague.

> [!tip] Model Revision
> "Between 1750 and 1900, industrialization fundamentally transformed social relations in Western Europe and the United States by creating a distinct industrial working class and an expanded middle class whose economic interests drove democratic political reform; however, despite these structural changes, patriarchal gender hierarchies and global economic inequality between industrialized and non-industrialized regions persisted throughout the period."
>
> **Why it's stronger:** Specifies what changed (class structure, political reform) and what continued (gender inequality, global hierarchy), signals the extent claim, and provides a clear line of reasoning.

## Long Essay Questions (p. 360)

*Suggested writing time: 40 minutes each. For prompts 3 and 4, choose ONE.*

**LEQ 1 — Comparison (Egypt/Japan):**
In the 1800s and early 1900s, industrialization transformed societies in Africa and Asia, but the process of industrialization varied from country to country. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which the process of industrialization in Egypt under Muhammad Ali and in Japan during the Meiji Era were similar or different.

> [!tip] Approach: LEQ 1
> Similarities: both used state-directed programs; both aimed at military modernization; both imported Western technology. Differences: Japan succeeded and became an imperial power; Egypt became a raw-material exporter dependent on cotton sales to Britain. The key complexity: both *changed* their economic systems, but to dramatically different outcomes. Japan's autonomy (never colonized) vs. Egypt's British occupation (1882) is the pivot.

**LEQ 2 — Causation (Atlantic Revolutions):**
Enlightenment ideals and the concept of nationalism swept the Atlantic world from 1750 to 1900 as people developed new standards of freedom and self-determination. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which intellectual and ideological causes influenced the revolutions that occurred in the Atlantic world during that era.

> [!tip] Approach: LEQ 2
> Strong evidence: Locke/Rousseau → American/French/Haitian Revolutions; Communist Manifesto → labor movement and socialist parties. Complexity: economic grievances (poverty, taxation, class exploitation) also drove revolutions — ideology provided the framework, but material conditions provided the fuel. Evaluate *extent* by acknowledging both ideological and material causes.

**LEQ 3 — Causation (Environmental factors):**
New inventions contributed greatly to industrialization from 1750 to 1900 in Eurasia, Africa, and the Americas, but agricultural productivity and natural resources also played a part. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which environmental factors contributed to industrialization from 1750 to 1900.

> [!tip] Approach: LEQ 3
> Britain's coal and iron deposits; Latin America/Africa as mineral suppliers; Southeast Asia's rubber and timber. Complexity: environmental factors were necessary but not sufficient — political stability, capital, and labor supply also mattered. Japan had fewer natural resources than Britain but industrialized successfully through state capacity.

**LEQ 4 — Comparison (Business organizations):**
In the period from 1750 to 1900, businesses in Eurasia, the Americas, and Africa developed new technologies and new types of business organizations. Develop an argument that evaluates the extent to which the technologies and types of business organizations in Russia, the United States, and China were similar or different from 1750 to 1900.

> [!tip] Approach: LEQ 4
> USA: corporations, railroads, trusts (Carnegie, Rockefeller) — market-led. Russia: state-directed heavy industry (Trans-Siberian Railroad) — weaker consumer sector. China: resisted industrialization until late 19th century; treaty ports introduced limited industrial activity but under foreign control. Key similarity: all three used railroad expansion. Key difference: degree of state control and integration into global capitalism.

## Document-Based Question (pp. 361–363)

**DBQ Prompt:** Using the documents and your knowledge of world history, evaluate the extent to which the roles of women in Japan and Argentina were similar or different in the period from the 1850s to the 1920s.

*(15 minutes planning · 45 minutes writing · use at least 6 of 6 documents · 1+ piece of outside evidence · HAPP analysis for 3+ documents)*

### Document Summaries

**Document 1** — P. F. Siebold, *Manners and Customs of the Japanese in the Nineteenth Century* (1852)
Women in Japan have relative social freedom (education, recreation) compared to other Asian societies, but remain legally dependent on husbands and male relatives — no legal rights, no standing in court.

**Document 2** — Brian Platt, "Educational Reform in Japan, 19th century," *Children and Youth in History*
[Source describes expansion of Japanese primary education — context for Documents 3 and 4]

**Document 3** — Baron Kikuchi, Japanese government minister, speech (1907)
Japanese female education should prepare women to be "good wives and wise mothers." The household, not the individual, is the unit of society. Women's education should serve the family and the Meiji state's goal of raising future generations of productive citizens.

**Document 4** — Journalist describing female silk workers in Japan (1880s)
Silk factory workers labor from dawn to midnight; eat poor food; sleep in squalid quarters. Workers may be "sent out into service" when business is slow, with the employer taking their wages. Local residents view factory girls as degraded. The journalist singles out silk workers as the most pitiable group.

**Document 5** — International Statistics Annual (1920), enrollment data
[Comparative data on school enrollment — provides quantitative context for gender educational access]

**Document 6** — Josefina Pelliza de Sagasta, "Women Dedicated to Miss Maria Eugenia Echenique," Argentina (1876)
Women should be educated on "wholesome principles" — but not to be legislators, professors, or equals of men. Their sacred mission is to be good wives, mothers, and housewives. Rejects the "ridiculous ideas of North American women" seeking political equality. Urges girls to cultivate virtue, modesty, and humility.

---

### DBQ Strategy

> [!tip] Grouping Suggestion
> **Group 1 — Continuity of patriarchal expectations:** Docs 1, 3, 6 — all argue women's proper role is domestic/family-oriented, regardless of country or decade. Point of view: all written by men or conservative women with stakes in maintaining gender order.
>
> **Group 2 — Economic change creates new realities:** Doc 4 — factory labor transforms women's daily lives even as ideology insists on their domestic role. HAPP: journalist's perspective (outsider, reformist) shapes what is emphasized.
>
> **Group 3 — Education as partial change:** Docs 2, 5 — enrollment data shows girls gaining access to education, but Doc 3 shows the state's *purpose* for female education is still domesticity, not equality.

> [!tip] Outside Evidence
> Use the AMSCO narrative's key data: women in industrializing Britain and the US were consistently paid less than men for identical work and denied high-wage positions. The suffragist movement was growing throughout this period but would not achieve major legal victories until after 1920. This qualifies the DBQ argument: even where women gained educational access, political and economic subordination continued.

> [!tip] Complexity Point
> Argue that the *mechanism* of patriarchy shifted: in agricultural Japan and Argentina, patriarchy was rooted in household labor and family hierarchy. In industrial Japan, it was reinforced by factory exploitation AND a new "good wife, wise mother" state ideology designed to serve nationalist goals. The *form* changed; the *function* (subordinating women to male authority) continued.
