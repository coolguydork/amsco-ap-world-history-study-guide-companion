# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Topic 7.6 — Causes of World War II | Printed pp. 503–510**

**AP Skill:** Causation
**AP Themes:** GOV, ECN, CDI

---

## AP Skill: Causation

The central skill for this topic is identifying and explaining multiple, interlocking causes. AMSCO organizes the causes of WWII into three categories, and the AP exam will often ask you to explain one or compare them:

| Category | Key Causes |
|----------|-----------|
| Diplomatic | Failure of appeasement; failure of the League of Nations; imbalance of the Treaty of Versailles |
| Economic | Global Great Depression destabilizing democratic governments and enabling extremism |
| Political | Rise of Hitler and Nazi ideology; Japanese militarism; Mussolini's fascism |

> [!exam-tip]
> When the AP asks you to "explain ONE cause of WWII," go beyond naming it. Explain the mechanism: *How* did the Depression enable Hitler? (Unemployment → resentment → political radicalization → Nazi electoral gains → legal appointment of Hitler as chancellor.) The mechanism is the argument.

**Causation practice (Think As a Historian):**

The AMSCO Think As a Historian exercise for this topic asks you to identify assumptions shaping point of view in the [[Concept Notes/Nuremberg Laws\|Nuremberg Laws]]. This is a Point of View (POV) / Sourcing skill: the laws assume racial hierarchy is real and scientifically valid (an assumption rooted in scientific racism and Social Darwinism). Recognizing this underlying assumption explains why the law's authors saw their work as rational rather than monstrous.

## AP Theme: GOV — Governance and Power

**How did the Nazi state consolidate power?**

Hitler's path to dictatorship was technically legal. The Nazis won elections, Hindenburg appointed Hitler constitutionally, and the Reichstag fire "emergency" was used to legally suspend civil liberties. Once in power, the Nazis outlawed opposition and centralized all authority. This illustrates how democratic institutions can be dismantled from within when checks on power weaken — a pattern relevant to other 20th-century authoritarian takeovers.

**How did Nazi ideology translate into state policy?**

The Nuremberg Laws institutionalized racial persecution as law. Kristallnacht demonstrated the state's willingness to use organized violence while maintaining the fiction of "spontaneous" popular outrage. Territorial expansion followed a deliberate pattern — each annexation tested Western resolve and paved the way for the next.

## AP Theme: ECN — Economic Systems

**The [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]] as a structural cause**

The Depression did not directly cause WWII, but it created the political conditions in which fascist movements could thrive. In Germany, mass unemployment and hyperinflation during the early 1930s made the [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic\|Weimar Republic]] seem incapable of governing. The Nazi promise to restore German economic strength and national pride was a central part of their appeal. The Treaty of Versailles had created economic instability (reparations, lost territories, lost resources) that compounded the Depression's impact.

> [!tip]
> The causal chain: WWI → Versailles → economic instability → Great Depression → political radicalization → Nazi electoral success → Hitler's appointment → WWII. The AP exam rewards students who can trace this full chain rather than treating the Depression and WWII as unrelated events.

## AP Theme: CDI — Cultural and Ideological Change

**[[Concept Notes/Fascism\|Fascism]] as ideology and its spread**

Fascism emerged in post-WWI Europe as a response to economic crisis and perceived national humiliation. It glorified the nation-state, the leader, violence, and military strength. Mussolini's Italian fascism became the model Hitler adapted, radicalizing it with racial pseudoscience (*scientific racism*, *Aryans*) and antisemitism (*Mein Kampf*, Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht).

**Propaganda and the manufacture of consent**

Nazi propaganda was central to the regime's power. The Reichstag fire was staged and blamed on communists to create fear. Anti-Jewish propaganda built social acceptance for the Nuremberg Laws and Kristallnacht. Mass rallies, censorship, and state control of education and media manufactured a sense of national emergency that justified authoritarian rule.

## Historiographical Perspectives

**Intentionalist vs. Structuralist Debate**

Historians have debated whether the [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] and WWII resulted primarily from Hitler's long-held intentions (*intentionalists* — Hitler always planned mass murder and global war) or from the chaotic internal competition and evolving circumstances of the Nazi state (*structuralists* — radicalization came from the system, not solely Hitler's plan). The AP exam does not require you to know these specific terms, but understanding that historians debate the role of individual agency vs. structural forces is valuable for SAQ and LEQ responses.

**Taylor's Revisionism**

British historian A.J.P. Taylor argued in *The Origins of the Second World War* (1961) that Hitler was a conventional opportunistic statesman exploiting Western weakness, not a uniquely ideological madman. This view remains controversial — most historians emphasize the centrality of Nazi ideology — but it is useful for highlighting the role of Western failure (appeasement, League weakness) as an independent causal factor.

> [!exam-tip]
> For AP LEQ/DBQ purposes: a strong argument acknowledges multiple causes and evaluates their relative significance. "The most important cause of WWII was Nazi ideology *because* it drove territorial expansion — but appeasement *enabled* that expansion by signaling that aggression had no cost."

## Continuity and Change

**Continuity from WWI:** The same pattern of alliance systems, nationalist competition, and failure of collective security that caused WWI reappeared — but now with fascist ideology accelerating the dynamic. Versailles created the conditions WWI had not fully resolved.

**Change from WWI:** WWII's causes were more ideological (racial empire, not just national rivalry) and the role of one state's deliberate aggression (Germany) was more direct. Also new: the precedent of appeasement showing that democracies would sacrifice smaller states to avoid war — a lesson authoritarian leaders internalized.

## Comparison: Causes of WWI vs. WWII

| Factor | WWI | WWII |
|--------|-----|------|
| Alliance trigger | Assassination escalated through alliances | Deliberate invasion of Poland |
| Ideology | Nationalism, imperialism | Fascism, racial supremacy, Lebensraum |
| Economic cause | Competition for colonies/markets | Great Depression enabling extremism |
| Collective security | Balance of power failed | League of Nations and appeasement failed |
| Single aggressor? | No — multiple states escalated | Yes — Nazi Germany drove events |
