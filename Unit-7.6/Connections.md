**Topic 7.6 — Causes of World War II | Printed pp. 503–510**

## Builds On

**[[Unit-7.4/Economy in the Interwar Period Overview\|Economy in the Interwar Period Overview]]**
The [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]] is a structural prerequisite for understanding WWII's causes. Weimar Germany's economic collapse — mass unemployment, political polarization — is what gave the Nazi Party its mass appeal. Without the Depression, Hitler's path to power via elections is harder to explain. The causal chain: economic crisis → political radicalization → Nazi electoral success → appointment as chancellor.

**[[Unit-7.5/Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview\|Unresolved Tensions After World War I Overview]]**
The Treaty of Versailles created the specific grievances Hitler exploited — reparations debt, lost territory, military limits, the "war guilt" clause. The [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic\|Weimar Republic]] was politically tainted by its association with Versailles from the start. WWII cannot be understood without WWI's unresolved aftermath. The League of Nations' failures during the interwar period (Japan/Manchuria, Italy/Ethiopia) are also directly connected.

## Compare With

**[[Unit-7.2/Causes of World War I Overview\|Causes of World War I Overview]]**
The AMSCO textbook explicitly asks students to "describe the connection between the militarism that led up to World War I and World War II." Key comparisons:

- Both wars emerged from failed collective security systems (balance of power in 1914; League of Nations in 1939).
- [[Concept Notes/Nationalism\|Nationalism]] drove both, but WWI's was multi-state competition; WWII's was concentrated in one ideologically driven aggressor.
- Alliance systems proved catastrophic in WWI (rapid escalation); in WWII, the failure was the opposite — Western powers refused alliances (appeasement) until war was unavoidable.
- The outcome of WWI directly caused WWII through Versailles's punishing terms.

> [!exam-tip]
> A strong LEQ or DBQ comparison: "WWI was caused by a system failure (interlocking alliances, no one in control); WWII was caused by a state failure (one aggressor exploiting a broken system). Both demonstrate that the absence of effective collective security makes major war inevitable."

## Broader AP Connections

**[[Concept Notes/Fascism\|Fascism]] and Authoritarianism (cross-topic)**
The rise of fascism in Italy (1922), Germany (1933), and Spain (1936–1939 Civil War) is part of a broader interwar pattern of democratic backsliding. The AP exam may ask you to explain why democratic governments were replaced by authoritarian ones in the 1920s–1930s. Key factors: economic crisis, national humiliation, weak democratic traditions, and the appeal of strong-man nationalism.

**Japanese Imperialism and Asian WWII**
Japan's expansion (Manchuria 1931, China 1937) connects to Unit 7.5's discussion of Asian independence movements and Unit 7.4's discussion of Japanese imperialism. The causes of WWII in Asia (military nationalism, resource competition, racial hierarchy as ideology) parallel but differ from European causes.

**The [[Concept Notes/Holocaust\|Holocaust]] — [[Concept Notes/Genocide\|Genocide]] and State Violence**
The Nuremberg Laws and Kristallnacht are the opening moves of what became the Holocaust. This connects to broader AP themes of state-sponsored violence, genocide, and human rights. When writing about WWII's causes, the Holocaust should be understood as both a consequence of Nazi ideology and an intended *goal* — not just a byproduct of the war.
