# People, States & Movements

**Topic 7.6 — Causes of World War II | Printed pp. 503–510**

---

## Key People

**Adolf Hitler (1889–1945)**
Austrian-born fascist leader who became chancellor and then dictator of Germany. He built his political career on resentment of the Treaty of Versailles, extreme anti-Semitism, and a vision of German racial empire (*Lebensraum*). His *Mein Kampf* (1924–25) laid out his ideology in detail before he took power. Once in government, he dismantled the [[Concept Notes/Weimar Republic\|Weimar Republic]] through legal manipulation and manufactured crises (the Reichstag fire), then systematically violated Versailles — rearmament (1935), Rhineland (1936), Austria (1938), Czechoslovakia (1938–1939), Poland (1939). Each unchallenged move emboldened the next. His invasion of Poland started WWII; the Holocaust killed approximately 6 million Jews. AP Skill: Hitler is the pivotal causal agent — but make sure to connect him to the structural conditions (Versailles, Depression) that enabled his rise.

**Neville Chamberlain (1869–1940)**
British Prime Minister who personified appeasement. At Munich (September 1938) he agreed to let Hitler annex the Sudetenland in exchange for Hitler's promise to make no further territorial demands. He returned home declaring "peace for our time." Within six months Hitler violated the agreement by seizing all of Czechoslovakia, and within a year Britain was at war. Chamberlain's name became shorthand for the lesson that conciliation with aggressors fails.

**Hideki Tojo (1884–1948)**
Japanese general and Prime Minister (1941–1944) who led Japan into WWII. Representing the Japanese military's dominant faction, he authorized the attack on Pearl Harbor and expanded Japan's conquest across Southeast Asia and the Pacific. He was later convicted and executed for war crimes at the Tokyo Trials.

**Emperor Hirohito (1901–1989)**
Emperor of Japan during WWII. The extent of his personal responsibility for Japanese wartime decisions remains historically debated — he was a constitutional monarch but had symbolic power that legitimized the military government's actions. After Japan's defeat, Allied authorities kept him on the throne to facilitate the occupation.

**Benito Mussolini (1883–1945)**
Italian fascist dictator ("Il Duce") who came to power in 1922 and became Hitler's model and early ally. He formed the Rome-Berlin Axis (1936) and the Anti-Comintern Pact (1937). Mussolini's Italy was a weaker partner in the Axis — its military underperformed in North Africa and Greece. He was deposed in 1943 and later killed by Italian partisans.

**Joseph Stalin (1878–1953)**
Soviet leader who signed the German-Soviet Nonaggression Pact (August 1939), gaining eastern Poland and the Baltic States while allowing Hitler to focus west. Stalin's calculation: buy time and territory while capitalist powers exhaust each other. The miscalculation: Germany invaded the USSR in June 1941.

## Key States

**Nazi Germany (Third Reich, 1933–1945)**
A totalitarian state built on racial ideology, single-party rule, and aggressive expansionism. Key features: the Reichstag fire as a pretext to suspend civil liberties; Nuremberg Laws systematizing anti-Jewish persecution; rearmament in defiance of Versailles; a series of territorial annexations (Rhineland, Austria, Sudetenland, Czechoslovakia). Germany was the primary aggressor in European WWII.

**Fascist Italy**
Under Mussolini from 1922, Italy became the prototype of fascist governance: extreme nationalism, violence against political opponents, corporate state economics, imperial ambition. Italy's invasion of Ethiopia (1935) demonstrated the League of Nations' impotence and its alliance with Germany formalized in the Rome-Berlin Axis (1936).

**Imperial Japan**
Japan's military-dominated government pursued imperial expansion across East Asia and the Pacific, driven by resource scarcity and a mission of pan-Asian dominance framed as "liberation" from Western colonialism (Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere). Japan invaded Manchuria (1931), China (1937), and eventually attacked the U.S. at Pearl Harbor (December 1941), drawing the U.S. into the war.

**Weimar Republic (1919–1933)**
Germany's democratic government between WWI and the Nazi takeover. Burdened by Versailles reparations, unable to manage the [[Concept Notes/Great Depression\|Great Depression]], and politically undermined by extremists on both left and right, it fell legally — the Nazis won elections, and Hindenburg appointed Hitler chancellor.

## Key Movements

**[[Concept Notes/Nazism\|Nazism]]**
The ideology of the Nazi Party: ultranationalism, racial hierarchy (Aryan supremacy), anti-Semitism, Lebensraum, and totalitarian state control. Nazism drew on fascism (borrowed from Mussolini) but radicalized it with pseudoscientific racism. It was not a fringe movement — the Nazis won parliamentary elections before seizing full power.

**[[Concept Notes/Fascism\|Fascism]]**
A political ideology emphasizing extreme nationalism, authoritarian leadership, suppression of opposition, and glorification of violence and the state. It arose in Italy and Germany in response to economic crisis and perceived national humiliation after WWI. Unlike communism, fascism accepted private property but subordinated it to the state and the "national interest."

> [!tip]
> AP comparison: Fascism vs. Communism — both were totalitarian, but they differed on economics (fascism accepted capitalism; communism abolished private property) and international alignment (fascist states allied against the communist Soviet Union). These ideological differences shaped WWII alliances.

**Appeasement (as policy)**
Not just a mistake — a coherent strategy adopted by British and French leaders who believed: (1) Hitler had limited, rational aims; (2) another European war would be catastrophic; (3) Germany had legitimate grievances from Versailles. Each assumption proved wrong. The Munich Agreement became its defining moment and failure.

> [!warning]
> Don't reduce appeasement to cowardice. The AP exam rewards nuanced answers that explain the logic behind a policy before explaining why it failed.
