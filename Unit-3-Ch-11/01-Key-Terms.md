# Chapter 11: Key Terms

#apwh #unit-3 #key-terms

## Empires & Dynasties

**Ottoman Empire** — Sunni Muslim empire centered in Anatolia (modern Turkey) that controlled the eastern Mediterranean, the Middle East, and North Africa from the 14th to early 20th centuries. (Significance: conquered Constantinople in 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire; dominated Eurasian trade routes for centuries; model of gunpowder-based state expansion.)

**Safavid Empire** — Shia Muslim empire ruling Persia (modern Iran) from 1501 to 1722, founded by [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Shah Ismail I|Shah Ismail I]]. (Significance: its Shia identity created an enduring geopolitical and sectarian rivalry with the Sunni Ottomans, shaping the political geography of the Middle East; Shah Abbas's capital Isfahan became a cultural and economic powerhouse.)

**Mughal Empire** — Muslim empire founded by [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Babur|Babur]] in 1526 that ruled most of the Indian subcontinent until the 18th century. (Significance: governed a majority Hindu population under Muslim rulers, requiring delicate policies of religious accommodation — especially under [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Akbar|Akbar]] — and left iconic architectural monuments like the [[Taj Mahal]].)

**Qing Dynasty** — Manchu-led dynasty that conquered China in 1644 and ruled until 1912. (Significance: the Manchu were an ethnic minority who governed a much larger Han Chinese population using a "dyarchy" — pairing Manchu and Han officials — while demanding symbolic submission through the [[kowtow]] ritual.)

**Russian Empire** — The expanding Russian state, centered in Moscow, that emerged as the Mongol [[Golden Horde]] weakened; under [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Ivan IV|Ivan IV ("the Terrible")]] and later Peter the Great, it expanded dramatically across Siberia and into Central Asia. (Significance: grew from a small principality to the largest land empire on earth, bridging Europe and Asia.)

**Songhai Empire** — The largest empire in West African history, centered at Gao along the Niger River, reaching its height under [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Sunni Ali|Sunni Ali]] and [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Askia Muhammad|Askia Muhammad]] in the late 1400s–early 1500s. (Significance: controlled trans-Saharan gold and salt trade; Timbuktu was a major Islamic intellectual center; the empire collapsed after a Moroccan gunpowder invasion in 1591.)

## Concepts & Strategies

**Gunpowder Empire** — A term historians use for states that used firearms and cannons (gunpowder technology) to conquer and consolidate large territories in the early modern period. (Significance: explains *why* these empires rose simultaneously — access to gunpowder technology was the key variable; links to the [[03-Themes-and-Causation|Technology & Environment theme]].)

**Devshirme** — The Ottoman practice of recruiting Christian boys from the Balkans, converting them to Islam, and training them as elite soldiers ([[Janissaries]]) or imperial administrators. (Significance: gave the sultan a loyal military and bureaucratic class that owed everything to him — not to noble families; a key method of [[Unit-3-Ch-12/01-Key-Terms#Administrative Control|administrative control]].)

**Janissaries** — Elite Ottoman infantry soldiers, mostly recruited through the [[devshirme]] system, armed with firearms. (Significance: the Janissaries were among the most feared military forces of the 15th–17th centuries; they later became a political threat to sultans who tried to reform them.)

**Kowtow** — The ritual prostration performed before the Chinese emperor — bowing and touching the forehead to the ground — required of all visitors, including foreign ambassadors. (Significance: symbolized the Confucian tributary system and the emperor's divine mandate; foreign powers who refused the kowtow, like Britain's Macartney mission in 1793, risked jeopardizing trade relations.)

**Tribute System (Chinese)** — The Chinese diplomatic framework in which neighboring states sent envoys and gifts to the Chinese emperor, acknowledging his supremacy, in exchange for trade access and security guarantees. (Significance: reflected China's view of itself as the center of civilization; structured East Asian international relations for centuries; linked to the [[kowtow]].)

**Millet System** — The Ottoman administrative system that allowed non-Muslim communities (Christians, Jews) to govern themselves through their own religious leaders in personal and religious matters, while paying a special tax (jizya). (Significance: enabled the Ottomans to govern a diverse multi-religious empire with relatively low levels of violent conflict; a form of managed pluralism.)

**Jizya** — A tax levied on non-Muslim subjects (dhimmis) in Islamic empires, particularly the Ottoman and Mughal. (Significance: symbolized the subordinate but protected status of non-Muslims; [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Akbar|Akbar]] controversially abolished it in the Mughal Empire as a gesture of religious tolerance; [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Aurangzeb|Aurangzeb]] later reinstated it, contributing to Hindu-Muslim tensions.)

**Mansabdar System** — The Mughal administrative system in which officials (mansabdars) were assigned a numerical rank (mansab) determining their salary and the number of cavalry soldiers they were required to maintain. (Significance: created a centralized, merit-based hierarchy that reduced the power of hereditary nobles; linked military service directly to imperial administration.)

**Dyarchy** — The Qing practice of appointing both a Manchu official and a Han Chinese official to administer each province, ensuring Manchu oversight while preserving existing governance structures. (Significance: a pragmatic compromise that allowed a small Manchu minority to rule a massive Han majority; a classic example of using existing structures to administer a conquered population.)

**Streltsy** — Russian professional infantry units armed with firearms, similar to the Ottoman Janissaries, established by [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Ivan IV|Ivan IV]] in the 16th century. (Significance: represented Russia's adoption of gunpowder military technology; later brutally suppressed by Peter the Great after they revolted.)

**Cossacks** — Semi-autonomous communities of frontier warriors, primarily of Slavic origin, who served the Russian tsar as soldiers and explorers, pushing Russia's borders eastward into Siberia. (Significance: key agents of Russian territorial expansion; their raids and settlements extended the Russian Empire to the Pacific Ocean by the mid-1600s.)

**Tsar** — The title used by Russian rulers, derived from "Caesar," claiming an imperial legacy comparable to Rome and Byzantium. (Significance: after the fall of Constantinople in 1453, Russian rulers claimed Moscow was the "Third Rome" — the legitimate successor to the Roman and Byzantine empires, which helped justify their authority.)

**Shah** — The Persian/Iranian title for king or emperor, used by rulers of the Safavid and later Iranian dynasties. (Significance: Safavid shahs claimed both secular and religious authority, as descendants of the Sufi order of Safaviyya; [[02-Key-People-and-Groups#Shah Abbas I|Shah Abbas I]] transformed the title into a symbol of Persian imperial splendor.)

**Sunni-Shia Split** — The longstanding division within Islam over the rightful succession after the Prophet Muhammad's death (632 CE): Sunnis accepted the early caliphs, Shia believed Ali (Muhammad's cousin/son-in-law) was the legitimate heir. (Significance: in Unit 3, this split *geopolitically divided* the Middle East — the Ottoman Empire became the champion of Sunni Islam, the Safavid Empire became the champion of Shia Islam, and the two empires fought repeatedly over this divide.)
