# People, States & Movements — Topic 2.2

**AP Themes:** Governance (GOV) · Economic Systems (ECN)

## Key People

**Genghis Khan (Temujin, c. 1162–1227)**
Born Temujin around 1162, he spent his early decades building tribal alliances, eliminating rivals, and creating a unified Mongol power structure. He sometimes promoted talented non-family members over relatives — a pragmatic break from clan tradition. Elected **Great Khan** at the kuriltai of 1206. His conquests began with the Jin Empire (1210) and expanded to the Kara Khitai and Khwarazm Empires by 1219. By his death in 1227, his empire stretched from the North China Sea to eastern Persia. His governance was unexpectedly liberal: religious toleration, scholarly consultation, road-building, and legal codification all characterized his rule alongside military terror. (AMSCO pp. 86–87)

> [!tip]
> AP exam favorite: Genghis Khan is often compared to other empire-builders (e.g., Sundiata, the Romans). Note that his dual identity as a conqueror and an administrator is what made his empire durable.

**Ögedei Khan (r. 1229–1241)**
Son of Genghis Khan and his chosen successor. Ögedei continued westward expansion and authorized Batu Khan's invasion of Russia. His death in 1241 prompted Batu to withdraw from his invasion of Western Europe — likely saving it from Mongol conquest. The kuriltai system required a new assembly to select a successor, which took precedence over military campaigns. (AMSCO p. 88)

**Batu Khan (c. 1205–1255)**
Grandson of Genghis Khan; son of Jochi. Led the western campaign into Russia (1236) and Eastern Europe, commanding the force known as the **Golden Horde**. His army defeated Polish, German, and French knights in 1241 before withdrawing at news of Ögedei's death. He ruled Russia through indirect rule, establishing a pattern of tribute extraction that persisted for over a century. (AMSCO p. 88)

**Hulagu Khan (c. 1217–1265)**
Grandson of Genghis Khan; led the southwestern campaigns. His most consequential act was the **destruction of Baghdad in 1258** — killing the Abbasid caliph, burning the [[Concept Notes/House of Wisdom|House of Wisdom]], and killing perhaps 200,000 people. His expansion was stopped at the **Battle of Ain Jalut (1260)** by the Egyptian Mamluks, defining the western limit of Mongol expansion. He established the **Il-khanate** and eventually converted to Islam. (AMSCO p. 89)

**Kublai Khan (1215–1294)**
Grandson of Genghis Khan; the most powerful of the later khans. Became Great Khan in 1260 and founded the **Yuan Dynasty in 1271** after completing the conquest of Song China. Unlike other Mongol rulers, he closely adapted Chinese customs and extended religious toleration widely. Under his rule, the Pax Mongolica reached its height and China experienced renewed prosperity through trade. His failed attempts to conquer Japan and Southeast Asia weakened Mongol prestige in China. (AMSCO pp. 89–90)

**Zhu Yuanzhang (1328–1398)**
A Buddhist monk from a poor peasant family who led the revolt against the Yuan Dynasty, founding the **Ming Dynasty in 1368**. His origins make him one of history's most dramatic examples of a commoner rising to imperial power. The Ming he founded deliberately reversed Mongol policies, restoring the civil service examination and emphasizing Chinese cultural distinctiveness. (AMSCO p. 90)

**Törengene Khatun**
Wife of Ögedei Khan. After Ögedei's death, and while his son Güyük was away on campaign, she argued persuasively that a regent was needed to prevent state affairs from falling into confusion. She served as regent, directing the Mongol state until a new Great Khan could be chosen at a kuriltai — an example of women exercising significant political authority in Mongol society. (AMSCO practice p. 93)

## Key States & Empires

**Mongol Empire (1206–c. 1370s)**
The largest contiguous land empire in history. At its peak it stretched from the Korean Peninsula to Eastern Europe and from Siberia to Southeast Asia. Founded by Genghis Khan and governed in theory by a single Great Khan, it functionally divided into four major khanates after 1260. Unified by the yam postal system, a common legal code (yasa), and religious toleration. The empire enabled the Pax Mongolica before declining through military overextension, internal succession conflicts, and the Black Death. (AMSCO pp. 85–91)

**Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368)**
The Mongol-ruled dynasty in China, established by Kublai Khan. Notable for: adopting Chinese administrative structures, dismantling the civil service examination (which alienated the scholar-gentry), promoting religious minorities (Buddhists, Daoists, Muslims), and enabling enormous trade prosperity. Fell to the revolt led by Zhu Yuanzhang. (AMSCO pp. 89–90)

**Il-khanate (1256–1335)**
The Mongol khanate covering Persia and much of the Middle East. Governed with Persian administrators. Converted to Islam over time. Notable for ending the [[Concept Notes/House of Wisdom|Abbasid Caliphate]] and devastating the agricultural infrastructure of Iraq. The Battle of Ain Jalut (1260) stopped its westward expansion. (AMSCO p. 89)

**Golden Horde (1242–1502)**
The western khanate covering Russia and the western steppes. Used indirect rule through Russian princes, which paradoxically strengthened Moscow and built the foundations of the modern Russian state. Defeated at the Battle of Kulikovo (1380). (AMSCO pp. 88–89)

**Chagatai Khanate (1226–c. 1340s)**
The Central Asian khanate between the other three. Later conquered by Tamerlane around 1369. Less directly significant to the AP curriculum than the other three khanates.

**Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)**
The Chinese dynasty that replaced the Yuan. Founded by Zhu Yuanzhang. Restored the civil service examination, emphasized Chinese cultural traditions, and later commissioned the voyages of Zheng He (see [[Unit-2.3/Indian Ocean Overview|Topic 2.3]]). (AMSCO p. 90)

## Key Movements & Groups

**White Lotus Society**
A secret Chinese Buddhist organization that organized resistance to Yuan Dynasty rule beginning in the 1350s. Representative of the deep alienation of the Chinese population from Mongol governance. Their organizing contributed to the eventual success of Zhu Yuanzhang's revolt. (AMSCO p. 90)

**Mamluks**
A military slave caste ruling Egypt who defeated the Mongols at the **Battle of Ain Jalut (1260)** in cooperation with Christian Crusaders. This alliance of enemies who shared a common threat defined the western limits of Mongol conquest. (AMSCO p. 89)
