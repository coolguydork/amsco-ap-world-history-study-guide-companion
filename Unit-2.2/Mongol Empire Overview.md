# Topic 2.2 — The Mongol Empire and the Modern World

> [!summary] Essential Question
> How did Eurasian empires grow over time, and how did their expansion **influence trade and communication**?

## Context: Who Were the Mongols?

In the 12th century, the Mongols were pastoral nomads on the arid steppes north of the **Gobi Desert** in East Asia — multiple clans herding goats and sheep, raiding each other, and coveting the wealth of peoples closer to the Silk Roads. They had no writing system, no cities, and no empire.

Within a century, they had built the **largest contiguous land empire in human history**.

This transformation — from nomadic clans to world conquerors — is one of the most remarkable stories in AP World History. More importantly for the exam, what the Mongols *did with their empire* (protect trade, spread plague, transfer technologies) is more significant than the conquests themselves.

## Genghis Khan: Building the Empire

### Rise to Power (1162–1206)

**Temujin** (born 1162) spent decades creating tribal alliances, defeating rival groups one by one, and building personal loyalty networks. He promoted based on talent, not family ties. He was ruthless — killing his own stepbrother — but also strategically brilliant.

In **1206**, Temujin gathered all Mongol chieftains at a *kuriltai* (leadership assembly) and was elected **Genghis Khan** — "ruler of all."

### The Art of Mongol Warfare

Mongol military success rested on several innovations:

- **Superior horsemanship** — every Mongol, male and female, learned to ride. Soldiers could sleep on horseback.
- **Short bow** — fired rapidly from horseback with devastating accuracy
- **Disciplined command structure** — organized by units of 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000
- **Intelligence** — special units mapped terrain before battles
- **The feigned retreat** — Mongols would retreat under apparent defeat, luring enemy forces into a trap, then encircle and destroy them
- **Psychological warfare** — stories of Mongol brutality spread faster than the armies, inducing surrender before battle
- **Incorporated conquered technology** — captured Chinese and Persian engineers provided **siege weapons**: portable towers, catapults, and eventually cannon-like devices

> [!exam-tip]
> The feigned retreat and incorporation of siege technology are the two most frequently tested military tactics. The Mongols *adapted* — they were not just cavalry fighters but sophisticated combined-arms commanders.

### Conquest Strategy

When approaching a city, Genghis Khan sent a small group ahead to demand surrender. If the city surrendered:
- Skilled workers (craftsmen, engineers, literate people) were recruited into the empire
- Others were absorbed as laborers or frontier soldiers

If the city refused:
- The aristocrats were killed
- Civilians were slaughtered or enslaved
- The destruction was total and publicized — to terrify the next city

### Genghis Khan in Peace

Paradoxically, Genghis Khan was a thoughtful administrator. He:
- Established the capital at **Karakorum** (modern Mongolia)
- Consulted Chinese and Islamic scholars for governance advice
- Built more bridges than almost any other ruler in history
- **Instituted religious tolerance** — unusual in the 13th century
- Directed the creation of a Mongol written language (adapted from the Uyghur alphabet)
- Protected the Silk Roads, allowing the third golden age of trade

The period c. 1250–c. 1350 is called the **[[Concept Notes/Pax Mongolica|Pax Mongolica]]** — the Mongolian Peace. Under this umbrella, trade and cultural exchange flourished across Eurasia at levels unseen since the Roman/Han era.

## The Empire Expands: Three Grandsons, Three Khanates

After Genghis Khan's death (1227), his empire continued growing under his successors. Three grandsons created distinct successor states:

### Batu and the Golden Horde (Russia and Western Europe)

In **1236**, **Batu** led 100,000 soldiers into Russia — then a loose network of city-states and principalities. The **[[Concept Notes/Golden Horde|Golden Horde]]** swept westward:

- Conquered and looted Russian kingdoms, forcing tribute payments
- Destroyed Kiev in **1240**
- In **1241**, defeated Polish, German, and French knights in battle
- Threatened Italy and Austria — then suddenly withdrew when news of Ogodei Khan's death arrived

The Mongols never returned to Western Europe. They ruled Russia **indirectly** — working through existing Russian rulers who forwarded tributes. (They preferred not to live in Russian forests.)

**Long-term impact on Russia**:
- Widespread devastation and population decline
- Three centuries of Mongol rule isolated Russia from Western European developments (the Renaissance, early capitalism)
- Russia developed a more distinct cultural identity
- Resistance to the Mongols — led by the city-state of Moscow — created the foundation of the modern Russian state. Moscow defeated the Golden Horde at the **Battle of Kulikovo (1380)**.

### Hulegu and the Islamic Heartlands (Il-khanate)

**Hulegu** marched southwest, destroying the **[[Concept Notes/Abbasid Caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]]** in **1258**. Baghdad — then one of the world's great cities — was sacked, its caliph killed, and perhaps **200,000 residents** massacred.

Hulegu's army was stopped in **1260** by a temporary alliance between Muslim Mamluks and Christian Crusaders in Palestine — one of history's stranger partnerships, born of mutual fear.

The resulting **Il-khanate** was governed by Mongols but administered by Persian officials. Eventually, Hulegu and most Il-khanate Mongols **converted to Islam** — after which they began persecuting Jews and Christians. This illustrates the general Mongol pattern: **conquer first, then adopt the culture of the conquered**.

### Kublai Khan and the Yuan Dynasty (China)

**Kublai Khan** faced the toughest conquest: Song Dynasty China. It took from **1235 to 1271** to complete. Kublai became Great Khan in **1260** and established the **[[Concept Notes/Yuan Dynasty|Yuan Dynasty]]**.

Unlike other khans, Kublai adopted Chinese governing practices:
- Rebuilt the capital at **Dadu** (Beijing), renamed from Zhongdu
- Instituted religious tolerance for Buddhists, Daoists, Muslims, Jews, and Christians
- Brought prosperity through trade and cultural exchange
- Welcomed foreign visitors — including **[[Marco Polo]]**, who spent years at his court

**Mongol weaknesses in China**:
- Hired foreigners instead of native Chinese for government positions
- Dismantled the civil service exam system, alienating the scholar-gentry
- Prohibited non-Mongols from speaking Mongolian
- Failed to conquer Japan (1274, 1281), Java, or Indochina — defeats that undermined Mongol prestige

The **White Lotus Society** organized resistance in the 1350s. In **1368**, Zhu Yuanzhang — a Buddhist monk of peasant origin — overthrew the Yuan and established the **[[Concept Notes/Ming Dynasty|Ming Dynasty]] (1368–1644)**.

> [!warning]
> A common confusion: the Yuan Dynasty was Mongol-run China. The Ming Dynasty that replaced it was ethnically Chinese and deliberately turned away from Mongol practices (and foreign engagement). This context explains Zheng He's voyages — and why they were stopped.

## The Long-Term Legacy of the Mongol Invasions

The Mongols had contradictory impacts — spectacular destruction and unprecedented connectivity:

**Negative:**
- Largest single human death toll from conquest in premodern history
- Destroyed Baghdad, the intellectual center of the Islamic world, ending its golden age
- Russia's isolation from Western Europe for three centuries

**Positive:**
- Pax Mongolica (c. 1250–c. 1350): revitalized trade between Asia, Middle East, Africa, and Europe
- Built and maintained roads; protected merchants along the Silk Roads
- Cultural transfers: Islamic science → China; paper → Europe via Islamic world; Arabic numerals → Western Europe; Greco-Islamic medicine → Europe
- Centralized governance models transferred to successor states
- **Mongol fighting techniques** ended Western Europe's reliance on armored knights — too slow to respond to Mongol speed and surprise

**Ambiguous:**
- The Mongol conquests **transmitted the bubonic plague** (Black Death) from China to Central Asia and then to Europe. Possibly via caravanserai where humans and animals mixed. The plague killed perhaps 75–200 million people across Eurasia. See [[Unit-2.6/Environmental Consequences of Connectivity]].

> [!exam-tip]
> The **Pax Mongolica** is a crucial turning point: it's simultaneously the high point of Silk Roads trade AND the mechanism by which the Black Death spread. One cause, two effects — exactly the kind of complexity the AP exam rewards.

## Mongol Women: A Comparative Note

Mongol women occupied a higher status than women in most contemporary civilizations:
- Tended flocks and raised children alongside men
- Rode horses and wore leather trousers (unlike foot-bound Chinese women)
- Could remarry after widowhood
- Could initiate divorces
- Often served as top advisors to the Great Khan

This is useful comparative evidence for the AP theme of **social structures and gender**.
