# Key Terms & Concepts — Topic 2.2

**AP Themes:** Governance (GOV) · Economic Systems (ECN) · Human Migration and Settlement (MIG)

---

**Mongols**
Pastoral nomadic people of Central Asia, north of the **Gobi Desert**, who in the 13th century united under Genghis Khan and built the largest contiguous land empire in history. Their culture valued horsemanship, courage, and personal loyalty above all. (AMSCO p. 85)

**Khan**
Title meaning "king" or "ruler" in Mongol and Turkic. Genghis Khan means "ruler of all." Subordinate rulers of the empire's regional divisions were also called khan (e.g., Batu Khan, Hulagu Khan, Kublai Khan). (AMSCO p. 86)

**Kuriltai**
An assembly of Mongol chieftains called to elect a great khan or make major collective decisions. Genghis Khan was elected at a kuriltai in 1206. After his death, the return to a kuriltai for succession was the reason Batu Khan halted his invasion of Western Europe. (AMSCO p. 86)

**Khanate**
A regional division of the Mongol Empire ruled by a khan. After Genghis Khan's death, the empire fractured into four main khanates: the Yuan Dynasty (China), the Il-khanate (Persia), the Golden Horde (Russia/Eastern Europe), and the Chagatai Khanate (Central Asia). (AMSCO p. 86)

**Pax Mongolica**
"Mongolian Peace" — the period roughly c. 1250–c. 1350 when unified Mongol control over the Silk Roads made long-distance trade and travel unusually safe. Merchants, diplomats, and missionaries traveled freely from China to Europe. Marco Polo's journeys were made possible by the Pax Mongolica. (AMSCO p. 87)

> [!exam-tip]
> The Pax Mongolica is a classic example of how political unity — even achieved through violence — can enable economic and cultural exchange. Contrast it with the fragmentation that preceded and followed it.

**Yam System**
The Mongol postal relay network, sometimes called a "pony express." Riders on horseback transmitted oral messages rapidly across the empire, allowing Genghis Khan to maintain contact with distant conquests. It was a key tool of Mongol administrative cohesion. (AMSCO p. 87)

**Siege Weapons**
Advanced military technology — portable towers, catapults, trebuchets — adopted and improved by the Mongols from conquered Chinese and Persian engineers. This technology allowed Mongol armies to destroy walled cities that had previously resisted nomadic attackers. The cannon is considered a Mongol synthesis of Chinese gunpowder, Muslim flamethrowers, and European bell-casting. (AMSCO p. 87)

**Religious Toleration**
A hallmark of Mongol governance under both Genghis Khan and Kublai Khan. The Mongols generally allowed conquered peoples to continue practicing their religions and incorporated scholars and clergy of all traditions into their courts. This policy was unusual in the 13th century and was often a source of loyalty from formerly oppressed religious minorities. Note: the Il-khanate's toleration ended after conversion to Islam. (AMSCO pp. 87, 90)

**Golden Horde**
The western Mongol khanate established by **Batu Khan** in Russia and Eastern Europe. The name likely refers to the color of Batu's camp. The Golden Horde ruled Russia through indirect rule — collecting tribute via existing Russian princes rather than direct administration. It was defeated at the **Battle of Kulikovo (1380)** by a Russian coalition under Moscow's leadership. (AMSCO p. 88)

**Indirect Rule**
The Mongol method of governing Russia: instead of replacing existing rulers, the Mongols worked through local Russian princes who collected and forwarded tribute. This preserved Mongol military dominance without requiring them to administer unfamiliar territory. It also allowed Moscow to grow powerful as the primary tribute collector. (AMSCO p. 88)

**Il-khanate**
The southwestern Mongol khanate established by **Hulagu Khan** in Persia and the Middle East. It stretched from Byzantium to the Oxus River (now the Amu Darya). Persians served as local administrators — the Mongols found this arrangement maximized tax collection. Over time, the Il-khanate converted to Islam. (AMSCO p. 89)

**Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368)**
The Mongol dynasty established by **Kublai Khan** in China after defeating the Song Dynasty. Kublai adopted Chinese administrative practices and extended religious toleration to multiple faiths. The Yuan Dynasty eventually fell when the Chinese scholar-gentry class turned against Mongol rule, culminating in the revolt led by Zhu Yuanzhang. (AMSCO pp. 89–90)

**White Lotus Society**
A secret Chinese Buddhist society that organized resistance to Yuan Dynasty rule in the 1350s. Its activities helped lay the groundwork for the revolt that ended Mongol rule in China. (AMSCO p. 90)

**Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)**
The Chinese dynasty founded by **Zhu Yuanzhang** after expelling the Mongols. Zhu was a Buddhist monk from a poor peasant family — one of history's most dramatic examples of social mobility. The Ming reversed many Yuan policies, restoring the civil service examination and emphasizing Chinese cultural traditions. (AMSCO p. 90)

**Bubonic Plague ([[Concept Notes/Black Death\|Black Death]])**
A devastating epidemic disease spread by fleas carried on rodents. The Mongol conquests and the resulting trade networks facilitated its transmission from southern China through Central Asia to Southeast Asia and Europe in the mid-14th century. It killed perhaps one-third of Europe's population. (AMSCO p. 91)

> [!warning]
> The bubonic plague is one of the most important unintended consequences of the Pax Mongolica — the same roads that carried silk and ideas also carried disease. The AP exam often asks about this irony.

**Tamerlane (Timur the Lame)**
A Central Asian conqueror of Turco-Mongol origin who conquered most of the Central Asian territories formerly held by the Mongols around 1369. He is sometimes seen as a transitional figure between Mongol and later [[Concept Notes/Gunpowder Empires|gunpowder empire]] rule. (AMSCO p. 90)

**Uyghur Alphabet**
The script adapted to write Mongolian under Genghis Khan's direction by a captured Uyghur scribe in 1204. Though the attempt to impose a single script empire-wide failed, the adapted Uyghur alphabet remains in use in Mongolia today. (AMSCO p. 87)
