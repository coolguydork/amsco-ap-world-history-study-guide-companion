## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Source: AMSCO pp. 92–93*

Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage:

> "The [Russian] Duke who had spoken before gave a short account of all that had taken place since the death of [Genghis Khan], and the partition of his vast dominions. And then the younger Duke, Wsewolodovics, took up the tale. 'Lord King!' he began, 'these Mongols don't carry on warfare in an honorable, chivalrous way. They fight only to destroy, they are bloodthirsty, merciless; their only object is to plunder, slay, murder, and burn, not even to make any use of what lands they conquer. They are like a swarm of locusts. They stay till everything is eaten up, till all are plundered, and what they can't carry off, that they kill, or reduce to ashes. They are utterly faithless; their words and promises are not in the least to be trusted, and those who do make friends with them are the first upon whom they wreak their vengeance if anything goes wrong. We are telling you no fairy tales! We know to our own cost what they are, we tell you what we have seen with our own eyes. And let me tell you this, my lord king, their lust of conquest and devastation knows no bounds! If it is our turn today, it will be yours tomorrow!'"
>
> — Baron Nicholas Jósika (1796–1865), *'Neath the Hoof of the Tartar, or The Scourge of God*, a novel about the Mongols

---

**1.** What did the Russian duke think motivated the Mongols?

(A) The Mongols were interested primarily in seizing wealth rather than in controlling Russian territory.
(B) The Mongols chose their tactics in warfare because those tactics were used in fairy tales.
(C) The Mongols believed they could fool foes with rumors of atrocities.
(D) The Mongols wanted to be thought of as honorable warriors.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The duke says the Mongols' only object is "to plunder, slay, murder, and burn" and that they don't even make use of the lands they conquer — pointing to wealth extraction, not territorial control. B is wrong: the duke explicitly says "we are telling you no fairy tales." C inverts the logic — the Mongols didn't need to manufacture rumors; their actual behavior created the reputation. D is the opposite of the passage.

---

**2.** The most valid counterargument to the description of the Mongols by the Russian duke was that the Mongols:

(A) had little interest in wealth so they took only what they needed to survive from people they conquered
(B) appreciated architecture and art and hence rarely destroyed cities
(C) had no reason to invade other areas west of Russia
(D) established political institutions and peace in the territories they conquered

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> The Pax Mongolica is the key counterargument — Mongols did establish political order, protect trade, and enable prosperity in conquered territories. A is wrong: Mongols were very interested in wealth and tribute. B is wrong: they destroyed many cities (Baghdad, Kiev). C is wrong: the Golden Horde pushed into Poland and Hungary.

---

**3.** How would the Mongols use the Russian duke's description to their advantage if they chose to invade Hungary?

(A) to frighten the Hungarians into surrendering
(B) to persuade Hungarians to fight against other Christian kingdoms
(C) to cause the Hungarians to convert to Islam
(D) to attract Hungarian knights who admired their style of warfare

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> Psychological warfare was a core Mongol strategy. The duke says "if it is our turn today, it will be yours tomorrow" — precisely the kind of fear the Mongols deliberately cultivated. Many cities surrendered without fighting because Mongol reputation preceded them. B, C, and D are not consistent with Mongol strategy or the passage.

## Short-Answer Questions

*Source: AMSCO p. 93*

**SAQ 1.** Use the following passage to answer all parts of the question:

> "And the Monarch of the World, the Hatim of the Age, [Mengü] Qa'an [Khan] passed away, Güyük, his eldest son had not returned from the campaign against the Qifchaq, and therefore in accordance with precedent the dispatch of orders and the assembling of the people took place at the door of the . . . palace of his wife, Möge Khatun. . . . But since Törengene Khatun was the mother of his eldest sons and was moreover shrewder and more sagacious than Möge Khatun, she [Törengene Khatun] sent messages to the princes, i.e. the brothers and nephews of the Qa'an . . . and said that until a Khan was appointed by agreement someone would have to be ruler and leader in order that the business of the state might not be neglected nor the affairs of the commonwealth thrown into confusion."
>
> — Ala-ad Din Ata-Malik Juvaini, Persian scholar, 13th century, *The History of the Conqueror*

**(A)** Identify ONE way in which Mongol leadership continued unchanged under Törengene Khatun.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity
> Törengene Khatun maintained continuity by insisting that "the old ministers should remain in the service of the Court" so that existing laws (yasas) would not be changed. She preserved the existing bureaucratic structure and legal framework rather than reorganizing the state.

**(B)** Explain ONE reason Mongol nobles accepted a woman's leadership.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> Mongol nobles accepted Törengene Khatun's regency because Mongol culture already granted women significant authority and independence. Unlike sedentary societies of the period, Mongol women managed herds, rode horses, and could remarry or initiate divorce. Her authority was therefore culturally legitimate. Additionally, her argument was pragmatic — without a regent, state affairs would collapse — and she framed her role as temporary until a proper kuriltai could be held, making her claim unthreatening to the nobles.

**(C)** Explain ONE way in which the status of women in Islamic society in the 13th century differed from the status of women in Mongol culture.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> In 13th-century Islamic society, women were generally restricted from holding public political authority or managing affairs outside the household. By contrast, Mongol women like Törengene Khatun could exercise direct political leadership. Mongol women also participated in the pastoral economy by managing livestock and rode horses as freely as men, while Islamic women in urban settings were more constrained by purdah norms that limited public participation.

## Historical Reasoning Practice

*Source: AMSCO p. 94*

**SAQ 2.** Answer all parts of the question that follows.

**(A)** Identify ONE example of a pattern of Mongolian expansion in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Pattern Identification
> A clear pattern of Mongol expansion was their movement outward from Central Asia in all directions simultaneously, absorbing neighboring states and using conquered peoples' technology and administrators to sustain further expansion. For example, after defeating northern China, Mongol armies incorporated Chinese siege engineers and used their expertise to conquer the walled cities of Central Asia and Persia — each conquest enabling the next.

**(B)** Explain ONE way in which Mongol political structures changed when they ruled China.

> [!tip] Model Response — Change
> When Kublai Khan established the Yuan Dynasty in China (1271), the Mongols adopted significant Chinese administrative practices rather than imposing purely Mongol governance. Kublai rebuilt the capital at Dadu (Beijing) following Chinese urban conventions, consulted Chinese scholars, and extended religious toleration to previously marginalized groups including Buddhists and Daoists. This represented a significant change from the purely military governance of the conquest period, as the Mongols adapted to the complex administrative demands of ruling the world's largest agricultural society.

**(C)** Explain ONE example of a connection between Mongolian and Chinese political structures in the period 1200–1450.

> [!tip] Model Response — Connection
> A key connection is the Mongols' use of centralized authority, a feature of both Mongol governance (through the Great Khan) and the Chinese imperial tradition. Kublai Khan's Yuan Dynasty synthesized both — he maintained the Mongol principle of supreme personal authority while adopting the Chinese imperial title and court ceremonies. This synthesis meant that when the Ming Dynasty expelled the Mongols in 1368, Chinese emperors inherited a strengthened model of centralized imperial power partly shaped by Mongol precedents.

## Think As a Historian — Identify Connections

*Source: AMSCO p. 94*

Explain the connection between each development and the overall growth of the Mongol Empire from 1210 to 1369:

1. **The Mongols' bloody reputation spread farther than their conquest.**
The reputation was a strategic force multiplier — cities surrendered before Mongol armies arrived, reducing the cost of conquest. Matthew Paris wrote in terror from England, which was never actually invaded. This psychological reach extended Mongol power beyond physical reach.

2. **The Mongols incorporated the weapons and technology of the people they conquered.**
This adaptive strategy is why the empire could keep expanding across different environments. Chinese engineers provided siege technology to breach walled cities; Persian administrators provided bureaucratic expertise to govern densely populated agricultural regions. Each conquest expanded the empire's toolkit.

3. **Kublai Khan instituted a policy of religious tolerance.**
Religious toleration was a governance tool that secured loyalty from populations that had been persecuted under previous rulers. Buddhists and Daoists, marginalized under the Song, supported Yuan rule. This toleration enabled stable rule over enormously diverse territories — a necessity for any empire spanning multiple religious civilizations.

4. **The Mongols restored interregional trade among Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Europe.**
The Pax Mongolica was the economic consequence of military unification. By securing the Silk Roads, the Mongols generated the tax revenues needed to sustain their empire and created the connected world that enabled the cultural exchange defining the 13th–14th centuries.

5. **The Black Death moved throughout Southeast Asia and Europe with the Mongol conquests.**
The plague was the Pax Mongolica's dark mirror — the same roads that carried silk and ideas also carried infected fleas. The demographic collapse the plague caused weakened the Golden Horde, disrupted the trade networks sustaining the empire, and contributed to the empire's fragmentation after c. 1350.

## Reflect on the Essential Question

**In 1–3 paragraphs, explain how Eurasian empires grew over time and how their expansion influenced trade and communication.**

> [!tip] Model Response — Essential Question
> The Mongol Empire grew through a combination of military innovation, psychological warfare, and adaptive governance. Beginning with Genghis Khan's unification of the Mongol tribes in 1206, the empire expanded by incorporating the technologies and administrators of conquered peoples into its military and bureaucracy. Siege weapons captured from Chinese and Persian engineers allowed the Mongols to defeat walled cities that had stopped nomadic armies before. The empire's reputation for brutal retribution induced many cities to surrender without resistance. By Genghis Khan's death in 1227, the empire stretched from the North China Sea to eastern Persia; his grandsons extended it further into Russia, the Islamic heartlands, and China.
>
> The expansion of the Mongol Empire profoundly influenced trade and communication. Unified control over the Silk Roads created the Pax Mongolica (c. 1250–c. 1350), the third and final golden age of Silk Roads trade. Merchants, missionaries, and diplomats — including Marco Polo — could travel from Venice to Beijing under Mongol protection. The yam relay system provided rapid communication across the vast empire. Goods moved in both directions: Chinese paper eventually enabled Gutenberg's printing press; the Arabic numeral system reached Western Europe; gunpowder technology spread westward. Cultural and scientific exchange between Islamic, Chinese, and European traditions reached its medieval peak.
>
> The Mongol legacy demonstrates that empire-building produces contradictory outcomes simultaneously. The same conquests that destroyed Baghdad and killed hundreds of thousands also created the infrastructure for unprecedented Eurasian connectivity. When the empire declined after c. 1350 — weakened by the Black Death, overextension, and succession conflicts — successor states inherited both the ruins and the administrative and commercial innovations the Mongols had spread.
