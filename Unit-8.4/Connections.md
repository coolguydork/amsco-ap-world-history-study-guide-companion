# Connections

**AP Period 4** | Topic 8.4 — Spread of Communism after 1900

---

## Builds On

**[[Unit-8.2/Cold War Overview\|Cold War Overview]]** 
The ideological and geopolitical framework within which communism spread. The U.S.-Soviet rivalry turned local land reform movements and independence struggles into Cold War proxy conflicts. Without the Cold War context — Soviet military and economic aid to communist insurgencies, U.S. support for authoritarian anti-communist regimes — the specific patterns of communist spread in this topic are impossible to explain.

**[[Unit-8.3/Effects of the Cold War Overview\|Effects of the Cold War Overview]]** 
Topic 8.3 established how the Cold War shaped regional conflicts and decolonization movements globally. Topic 8.4 is the specific application: how communist ideology provided the political vehicle for anti-colonial and anti-feudal grievances within that Cold War framework.

**Reactions to the Industrial Economy** [[Unit-5.8/Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview|Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview]]
The origins of Marxist and socialist ideology: Marx's analysis of capitalism, class conflict, and the inevitability of proletarian revolution. Topic 8.4 shows how that European industrial ideology was adapted — sometimes radically — to agrarian, post-colonial contexts that Marx never envisioned.

## Leads To

**Cold War Conflicts** (Topic 8.5)
The spread of communism in 8.4 directly caused the proxy wars of 8.5: the Korean War, the Vietnam War, Cuban Missile Crisis, and U.S. interventions in Latin America and Africa. The communist governments established in 8.4 became the flashpoints of 8.5.

**Decolonization After 1900** (Topic 8.7) — *not yet built*
The fusion of communism with anti-colonial nationalism (in Vietnam, China, Ethiopia, Cuba) connects directly to the broader decolonization movements of Topic 8.7. Many independence movements drew on Marxist frameworks even when they didn't become formally communist.

---

## Compare With

**[[Concept Notes/Fascism\|Fascism]] and Totalitarianism** [[Unit-7.6/Causes of World War II Overview|Causes of World War II Overview]]
Both communism and fascism were totalitarian ideologies that mobilized mass movements, centralized political authority, and suppressed opposition through violence. Compare the Cultural Revolution's Red Guards with the Nazi SA; compare Mao's personality cult with Hitler's or Mussolini's. Key difference: fascism was explicitly nationalist and anti-Marxist; communism claimed internationalism. Both led to mass atrocities.

**Industrialization and Social Reform** (Topic 5.5/5.6)
The impulse for land reform and economic redistribution that drives communist movements in 8.4 connects to earlier reform movements responding to industrialization's inequalities in Europe and the Americas. Compare communist land reform with European labor reform movements — both respond to concentrated economic power, but take different political paths.

**Decolonization Independence Movements** (Topics 8.5–8.7)
Communist movements and independence movements frequently overlapped but were not identical. Vietnam and China fused them; India pursued independence without communism; Iran pursued revolution through Islamic nationalism. The comparison reveals that anti-colonial grievances could be channeled through multiple ideological paths.

## Key Cross-Topic Concepts

**Land reform as a cause of revolution** appears across multiple AP topics: Topic 5.8 (industrial capitalism's inequality), Topic 7.4 (Depression-era radicalism), Topic 8.4 (communist spread), and Topic 8.5 (proxy conflicts). Use land inequality as a through-line for any essay about causes of revolution or political instability.

**U.S. Cold War interventionism** — the CIA coups in Iran (1953) and Guatemala (1954) appear here and connect to broader U.S. foreign policy patterns in Topic 8.3 and 8.5. The pattern: U.S. backs authoritarian anti-communist regimes over democratic reformers when economic interests are at stake.

**Sino-Soviet split** — From 1961, China and the USSR became rivals. This fractures the assumption of a unified communist bloc and is essential context for understanding why the Cold War did not simply resolve into two competing blocs.
