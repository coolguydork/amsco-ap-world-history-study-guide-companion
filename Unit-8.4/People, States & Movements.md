# People, States & Movements

**AP Period 4** | AMSCO pp. 571–578

---

## People

**Mao Zedong** (1893–1976)
Chairman of the Chinese Communist Party and founder of the People's Republic of China (1949). Mao adapted Marxism by building revolution on China's vast peasantry rather than an industrial working class — a major theoretical departure from Lenin and Stalin. His military strategy during the Civil War (1945–1949) prioritized rural base areas and won peasant loyalty through land redistribution and troop discipline. As leader of the PRC, Mao launched the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. Both caused mass suffering, but Mao maintained power until his death in 1976. He is the defining figure of 20th-century Chinese history and a key example of communist adaptation to agrarian societies.

> [!tip]
> **Mao vs. Lenin:** Lenin based revolution on the urban *proletariat*; Mao based it on the rural *peasantry*. This distinction matters for SAQs comparing communist leaders. Both used vanguard party organization but aimed at different social classes.

**Ho Chi Minh** (1890–1969)
Vietnamese communist leader who combined nationalism with Marxism to lead the independence movement against France and later the United States. Ho founded the Viet Minh (1941) and declared Vietnamese independence in 1945 — citing the U.S. Declaration of Independence. After the partition of Vietnam (1954), he led North Vietnam and supported the communist insurgency in the south. He died before the war's end (1975) but is remembered as the central figure of Vietnamese national reunification. Like Mao, Ho Mi Minh shows how communism succeeded by fusing with anti-colonial nationalism.

**Fidel Castro** (1926–2016)
Cuban revolutionary who overthrew the U.S.-backed Batista dictatorship in 1959 and established a communist state 90 miles from Florida. Castro nationalized American businesses, accepted Soviet military support, and survived multiple CIA assassination and invasion attempts (including the Bay of Pigs, 1961). His alliance with the Soviet Union precipitated the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962). Cuba under Castro became a model and sponsor of communist movements across Latin America and Africa. The Cuban Revolution brought communism into the Western Hemisphere — directly confronting the Monroe Doctrine.

**Kim Il-sung** (1912–1994)
Soviet-backed leader who founded the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North Korea) in 1948 and launched the Korean War (1950–1953) by invading South Korea. Kim established a hereditary communist dictatorship combining Marxist-Leninist ideology with Korean nationalism (Juche ideology). North Korea became the most rigid and isolated communist state, with a personality cult and dynastic succession (Kim Jong-il, then Kim Jong-un). Kim's state demonstrates the extreme end of communist governance — total state control without the liberalizing reforms some later communist states attempted.

**Mohammad Mosaddegh** (1882–1967)
See [[Unit-8.4/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. Key AP significance: democratic leader overthrown by U.S./British intervention, demonstrating how Cold War logic led the U.S. to undermine democracy to preserve Western economic interests.

**Muhammad Reza Pahlavi** (1919–1980)
See [[Unit-8.4/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. The Shah's arc — U.S. ally, authoritarian modernizer, revolutionary target — encapsulates the paradox of Cold War support for anti-communist autocrats.

**Haile Selassie** (1892–1975)
See [[Unit-8.4/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. As a Pan-African symbol and anti-colonial resistance figure, Selassie's overthrow by Marxists illustrates how failed land reform collapsed legitimate post-colonial authority.

**Mengistu Haile Mariam** (1937– )
See [[Unit-8.4/Key Terms & Concepts\|Key Terms & Concepts]] for full entry. Ethiopian case study in Soviet-backed African Marxism.

**Jacobo Árbenz** (1913–1971)
Democratically elected President of Guatemala (1951–1954) who initiated land reform that redistributed unused land from the United Fruit Company to peasants. The U.S. government — responding to United Fruit's lobbying — classified Árbenz as a communist threat and organized a CIA coup (Operation PBSuccess, 1954) that overthrew him and installed a military dictatorship. Árbenz represents the pattern of U.S. Cold War intervention against democratic governments engaged in land reform, treating economic nationalism as communism.

## States

**People's Republic of China (PRC)** — Established 1949 after the Communist victory in the Civil War. The world's most populous country entering the communist bloc fundamentally shifted Cold War dynamics. China followed the Soviet economic model initially (five-year plans, nationalization), then diverged with the Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution. After Mao's death (1976) and the rise of Deng Xiaoping, China pursued market-oriented reforms while maintaining single-party rule.

**North Korea (DPRK)** — Established 1948 under Kim Il-sung with Soviet backing. Invaded South Korea in 1950, drawing in U.S. and eventually Chinese forces. The Korean War ended in an armistice (1953) with Korea still divided. North Korea remains the world's most isolated communist state, with a hereditary dynastic system unique in Marxist history.

**Cuba** — Became communist under Fidel Castro (1959). The closest communist state to the United States, Cuba triggered the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) and became the leading sponsor of communist movements in Latin America and Africa. Cuba demonstrates communism's appeal in Latin America as an anti-imperial alternative to U.S.-aligned capitalist development.

**Iran (Islamic Republic)** — Not communist, but relevant: the 1979 Islamic Revolution that overthrew the U.S.-backed Shah shows that the demand for structural change was not uniquely communist. Ayatollah Khomeini created a theocracy as an alternative to both Western capitalism and Soviet communism. Iran demonstrates the limits of framing all Cold War resistance as communist.

**Ethiopia (Derg)** — After Haile Selassie's overthrow (1974), the Marxist Derg under Mengistu aligned Ethiopia with the Soviet Union, making it a Cold War proxy in the Horn of Africa. The Ethiopian case shows communist takeover in sub-Saharan Africa and the role of land reform failure in destabilizing post-colonial states.

## Movements

**Chinese Communist Party (CCP)** — Founded 1921, the CCP adapted Leninist party organization to China's agrarian society. Mao's Long March (1934–1935) became its founding myth of revolutionary endurance. The CCP's victory in 1949 remains one of the most consequential events of the 20th century.

**Red Guards** — See Key Terms & Concepts. Youth movement weaponized by Mao during the Cultural Revolution; symbol of revolutionary violence turned inward.

**Viet Minh / Viet Cong** — Ho Chi Minh's independence movement (Viet Minh, from 1941) and its southern successor (Viet Cong) combined communist ideology with Vietnamese nationalism. Their success demonstrates how anti-colonial nationalist sentiment amplified communist movements' popular appeal.

**United Fruit Company** — U.S. corporation controlling vast agricultural land in Central America ("banana republics"). UFC's lobbying helped trigger the CIA coup against Guatemala's Árbenz (1954). The company represents how corporate economic interests intersected with Cold War anti-communist policy in Latin America.

> [!exam-tip]
> The **United Fruit Company / Guatemala** case is an excellent LEQ example of economic imperialism in the Cold War era. The U.S. overthrew a democratic government not because Guatemala was communist, but because land reform threatened American corporate interests — illustrating how Cold War ideology was sometimes a pretext for economic imperialism.
