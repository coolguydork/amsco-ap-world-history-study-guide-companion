# Key Terms & Concepts

**AP Period 4** | AMSCO pp. 571–578

---

**land reform**
Government redistribution of agricultural land, typically breaking up large estates and transferring ownership to peasants. Land reform was the central policy through which communist movements gained mass support in agrarian societies — China, Vietnam, Cuba, and Ethiopia all used land redistribution as a revolutionary tool. Non-communist governments also pursued land reform (Iran's White Revolution, India's Kerala reforms) but often with limited reach. The AP exam uses land reform as a lens for causation: why did communism appeal to peasants, and why did some governments fall when reform failed?

**commune**
Large collective farming community in which the state — not individuals — owned the land and directed agricultural production. Communes replaced private peasant farming in China under Mao's Great Leap Forward (1958–1962). Rather than increasing output, the commune system disrupted traditional agricultural practices, suppressed local knowledge, and combined with poor harvests to produce a catastrophic famine killing an estimated 15–45 million people. Comparable to Soviet collectivization under Stalin, communes illustrate the human cost of communist agricultural policy.

**theocracy**
A form of government in which religious leaders hold political authority and religious law governs the state. Iran became a theocracy after the 1979 Islamic Revolution, when Ayatollah Khomeini's clergy replaced the Shah's secular monarchy. The Iranian Guardian Council — composed of civil and religious legal experts — vets all candidates for office and ensures laws comply with Islamic law (shariah). On the AP exam, Iran's theocracy represents an alternative path to revolution that was neither communist nor liberal democratic, complicating the simple U.S.-Soviet binary.

**Great Leap Forward**
Mao Zedong's 1958–1962 campaign to rapidly industrialize China through mass mobilization of peasants into communes and backyard steel furnaces. Mao bypassed expert advice and Soviet-style central planning in favor of decentralized mass campaigns. The result was an agricultural catastrophe: grain exports continued even as famine spread, and an estimated 15–45 million Chinese died of starvation. The program was quietly abandoned by 1960. It stands as one of the deadliest policy failures in modern history and a key case study in the human cost of ideologically driven economic planning.

> [!warning]
> Do not confuse the **Great Leap Forward** (1958–1962, economic/agricultural policy) with the **Cultural Revolution** (1966–1976, ideological purge). They are distinct campaigns with different targets: GLF targeted the economy and peasant agriculture; the Cultural Revolution targeted intellectuals, party officials, and cultural institutions.

**Cultural Revolution**
Mao Zedong's 1966–1976 campaign to re-radicalize Chinese society and eliminate "bourgeois" influence from the Communist Party and cultural life. Mao mobilized youth as Red Guards to attack teachers, intellectuals, and party officials deemed insufficiently revolutionary. Millions were sent to "reeducation" in the countryside; schools and universities closed; cultural heritage was destroyed. The Cultural Revolution paralyzed an entire generation of Chinese education and professional life. Its scope and intensity parallel Stalin's purges in the Soviet Union.

**Red Guards**
Youth militia mobilized by Mao Zedong during the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) to enforce ideological conformity and destroy "bourgeois" and "counterrevolutionary" elements. Red Guards ransacked libraries, attacked teachers, and publicly humiliated party officials. They were the instrument through which Mao purged rivals and silenced critics. Eventually, Mao sent the Red Guards themselves to the countryside as their radicalism became uncontrollable — a pattern of revolutionary movements turning on their own members.

**White Revolution**
Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi's top-down modernization program in Iran (1963–1979), including land redistribution, women's suffrage, a literacy corps, and a social welfare system. Funded by oil revenue, the White Revolution aimed to create a modern capitalist economy and undercut both communist and traditional clerical opposition. However, rapid modernization disrupted traditional social structures; benefits did not reach most peasants; and the Shah's brutal suppression of all political dissent united conservatives and democrats against him. The White Revolution's failures contributed directly to the 1979 Islamic Revolution.

**Muhammad Reza Pahlavi**
Shah (king) of Iran (1941–1979), installed by the Allies during World War II and restored to full power by a U.S.-British coup in 1953 after Mosaddegh's nationalization attempt. The Shah pursued Western-aligned modernization through the White Revolution but relied on his secret police (SAVAK) to suppress opposition. His autocratic rule alienated both the Islamic clergy and secular democrats. Overthrown in the 1979 Islamic Revolution, he fled into exile. The Shah's reign exemplifies Cold War U.S. support for authoritarian rulers over democratically elected leaders seen as threats to Western interests.

**Mohammad Mosaddegh**
Iranian Prime Minister (1951–1953) who nationalized Iran's British-controlled oil industry. Elected democratically, Mosaddegh became a symbol of Iranian national sovereignty. The U.S. and Britain — fearing loss of oil access and communist influence — orchestrated a CIA-MI6 coup (Operation Ajax) that overthrew him in 1953 and restored the Shah. Mosaddegh's overthrow created lasting anti-American sentiment in Iran and is widely cited as a cause of the 1979 revolution. His case is a key AP example of Cold War U.S. intervention against democratic governments.

**Haile Selassie**
Emperor of Ethiopia (1930–1974) who became internationally famous for resisting the Italian invasion of 1935–1941 and leading Ethiopia's post-war modernization. Aligned with Western powers after World War II, Selassie enjoyed economic success from Ethiopia's coffee trade but failed to implement meaningful land reform, leaving land concentrated among an aristocratic elite. By the 1970s, he was seen by many Ethiopians as a corrupt pro-Western pawn. Overthrown in 1974 by a Marxist military junta, Selassie represents the vulnerability of conservative post-colonial regimes that resist structural reform.

**Mengistu Haile Mariam**
Ethiopian military officer who led the Marxist Derg regime after overthrowing Haile Selassie in 1974. Mengistu aligned Ethiopia with the Soviet Union, receiving military and economic aid in exchange for positioning Ethiopia in the Soviet Cold War camp. His regime conducted the "Red Terror" — systematic killings of political opponents — and oversaw a devastating famine in 1983–1985 exacerbated by war and mismanagement. By 1991, he had fled to Zimbabwe. Mengistu is a case study in communist governments in sub-Saharan Africa and the role of Cold War patronage in sustaining authoritarian regimes.
