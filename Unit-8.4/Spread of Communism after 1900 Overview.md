# Spread of Communism after 1900

**AP Period 4** | **Unit 8: Cold War and Decolonization** | AMSCO pp. 571–578

**Essential Question:** How did communism spread globally, and what were its effects on existing political systems?

**Learning Objective:** Explain the causes and effects of the spread of communism after 1900.

**AP Themes:** GOV (Governance), ECN (Economic Systems), CDI (Cultural Developments & Interactions)
**AP Skills:** Causation, Comparison

---

## The Big Picture

The spread of communism after 1900 was inseparable from the legacy of colonialism. Across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, colonial rule had left behind deeply unequal land distribution — a small class of wealthy landlords over a vast landless peasantry. Communist movements offered a simple, powerful appeal: redistribute the land. Where peasants had the most to gain and governments were too weak or corrupt to reform themselves, communism found its most receptive audiences.

The Cold War gave this dynamic an international dimension. Every land reform program, every nationalist revolution, every peasant uprising became a battleground between the United States and the Soviet Union. The U.S. backed authoritarian regimes — like the Shah of Iran and the Guatemalan military — to contain communist influence. The Soviets armed and advised communist movements from Korea to Ethiopia. This superpower competition accelerated the spread of communism while also generating the counter-revolutions that sought to contain it.

China was the central case. Mao Zedong's victory in 1949 brought the world's most populous nation into the communist bloc, fundamentally shifting the Cold War balance. But Mao's China diverged sharply from the Soviet model: the Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) replaced Soviet-style state management with a radical mass-mobilization that killed an estimated 15–45 million people, and the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) turned revolutionary violence inward to purge the party and silence critics. These episodes reveal communism's capacity for catastrophic policy failure even within its own ideological framework.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently connects 8.4 to causation: **why did communism spread?** The core answer is always the combination of (1) land inequality from feudalism and colonialism, (2) peasant support for redistribution, and (3) Cold War superpower competition. Practice framing land reform as the *mechanism* by which communism gained mass support.

## Section Summaries

### Communism in China

After Japan's defeat in 1945, the Chinese Civil War resumed between Mao Zedong's Communists and Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists. The Communists won popular support by redistributing land, opening schools and hospitals, and disciplining their troops — presenting themselves as nationalist as well as communist. In 1949, the People's Republic of China was established. Mao nationalized industries and launched Soviet-style five-year plans emphasizing heavy industry.

The **Great Leap Forward** (1958–1962) reorganized peasant agriculture into large communes (collective farms controlled by the state) and attempted rapid industrialization through backyard steel furnaces. Chronic mismanagement and failed harvests caused a catastrophic famine while China continued exporting grain; an estimated 20 million or more died of starvation. The program was abandoned by 1960.

The **Cultural Revolution** (1966–1976) was Mao's campaign to re-radicalize Chinese society and eliminate perceived ideological enemies. He mobilized youth as **Red Guards** to attack intellectuals, teachers, and party officials, sending millions to "reeducation" in the countryside. The Cultural Revolution paralyzed the Chinese education system and devastated an entire generation of professionals.

> [!warning]
> China and the Soviet Union were **rivals**, not allies, through much of this period. From 1961 onward, Sino-Soviet border skirmishes and competition for influence (e.g., over Albania) characterized their relationship. Do not assume communist states were a unified bloc — the Sino-Soviet split is a key Cold War complication.

### Turmoil in Iran

Iran's story is about land reform without communism — and its consequences. After World War II, the Shah Muhammad Reza Pahlavi was installed by British and Soviet forces as a pro-Western ruler. When Prime Minister **Mohammad Mosaddegh** moved to nationalize British-controlled oil companies in 1951, the U.S. and Britain engineered a coup restoring the Shah to full power (1953). This event burned into Iranian national memory as proof of Western imperialism.

The Shah then launched the **White Revolution** (1963–1979) — land redistribution, women's suffrage, literacy programs — using oil revenue to modernize Iran from above. But the reforms alienated traditional landowners and the Islamic clergy without satisfying democrats or peasants. The Shah's brutal secret police (SAVAK) suppressed all opposition. In 1979, the **Iranian Revolution** overthrew the Shah and established a **theocracy** under Ayatollah Khomeini — not communism, but an equally sharp rejection of Western-aligned capitalism.

> [!tip]
> Iran is the most useful **counter-example** in this topic: land reform and modernization attempted *without* communism still produced revolution when the process was imposed authoritatively and inequitably. Use Iran to argue that peasant grievances were structural, not purely ideological.

### Land Reform in Latin America and Asia

In **Guatemala**, a democratically elected government under Jacobo Árbenz began land reform that threatened the United Fruit Company's holdings. In 1954, a CIA-backed coup overthrew Árbenz — a pattern repeated across Latin America where land reform was equated with communism by U.S. policymakers.

In **Vietnam**, communist forces in the north implemented land reform that won peasant support; the South Vietnamese government's failure to do the same was a key reason it remained politically weak and ultimately lost the civil war.

In **Ethiopia**, Emperor **Haile Selassie**'s failure to implement meaningful land reform eroded his legitimacy. A Marxist military junta (the Derg) overthrew him in 1974, aligning Ethiopia with the Soviet Union. **Mengistu Haile Mariam** led a brutal regime that combined Marxist ideology with state terror and oversaw a devastating famine.

In **India**, land reform was attempted piecemeal; the state of Kerala achieved the most success through a series of laws between 1960 and 1970, but the central government took direct rule of Kerala to slow the program.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Guatemala and Iran cases both illustrate U.S. Cold War policy: the U.S. opposed land reform when it threatened American economic interests or when reformers had any communist connections. This is excellent SAQ/LEQ evidence for U.S. imperialism arguments.
