# Topic 5.8 — Reactions to the Industrial Economy: Overview

**AP Unit:** 5 — Revolutions | **Period:** 3 (c. 1750–c. 1900) | **AMSCO:** pp. 333–337

**Essential Question:** What conditions led to calls for change in industrial societies, and what were the effects of those efforts?

---

## The Big Picture

Industrialization generated enormous wealth — and enormous suffering. Dangerous factories, child labor, 14-hour workdays, urban slums, and chronic poverty provoked reactions from workers, philosophers, and governments. Those reactions took three main forms: **labor organizing**, **intellectual critique** (Mill, Marx), and **state reform**. Meanwhile, non-Western empires facing industrial pressure — the Ottoman Empire, China, Japan — undertook their own reform efforts with varying results.

---

## Labor Unions and Political Reform

### Conditions That Sparked Reform
The **Sadler Report** (1833): Britain's Parliament commissioned a study of factory conditions. It documented children as young as 5 working 14-hour days, dangerous machinery, beatings, and filth. The report shocked Parliament and the reading public.

### Labor Unions
Workers formed **labor unions** — organizations that bargained collectively with employers for better wages and conditions. For most of the 19th century, British unions operated **in secret** (the government treated them as threats to trade). By the 20th century they became legitimate.

**Wins unions achieved:**
- Minimum wage laws
- Limits on hours worked
- Overtime pay
- Five-day work week

### Child Labor Reform
- 1843: children under 10 **banned from coal mines**
- 1881: education mandatory for children aged 5–10

### Voting Rights
Reform bills of **1832, 1867, and 1884** progressively expanded male suffrage — reducing property requirements, giving industrial cities more representation. Full male franchise: **1918**. Women's suffrage: **1928**.

## The Intellectual Reaction

### John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)
British philosopher; championed **utilitarianism** — "the greatest good for the greatest number." He argued capitalism's brutal conditions were correctable through legislation: legalize unions, ban child labor, mandate safe conditions. Unlike Marx, Mill wanted to **reform** capitalism, not replace it.

### Karl Marx (1818–1883)
German scholar who argued for **scientific socialism** — not utopian escape, but a rigorous analysis of how capitalism actually worked and how it would inevitably be replaced.

**Communist Manifesto** (1848, with Friedrich Engels): capitalism divided society into two classes:
- **Proletariat**: working class; performed physical labor in factories and mines; received subsistence wages
- **Bourgeoisie**: middle class and investors; owned factories, machines, and land; received most of the wealth

Marx argued the bourgeoisie drove the proletariat to revolution → **socialism** would replace capitalism → eventually **communism** (no class distinctions). He called on workers to unite.

> [!exam-tip]
> Marxism is one of the most tested concepts in AP World History. Know: (1) the two classes, (2) how capitalism creates exploitation, and (3) Marx's predicted sequence: capitalism → socialism → communism. Also know how Marx's ideas influenced 20th-century events in Russia, China, and elsewhere — see [[Unit-8.1/Overview]].

## Ottoman Response: Tanzimat Reforms

The Ottoman Empire was not an industrial power, but it could not ignore the consequences of industrialization around it. Two sultans drove major reform:

**Sultan Mahmud II (ruled 1808–1839):**
- 1826: abolished the Janissary corps (had them massacred when they revolted in Istanbul)
- 1831: abolished the feudal system — tax collection centralized under the government
- Built roads, postal service; created European-style government ministries

**Tanzimat ("Reorganization") Reforms (1839–1876):**
- Rooted out corruption in central government
- Established secular primary and secondary schools (removed from ulama control)
- Codified laws: commercial code (1850), penal code (1858)
- **Hatt-i Humayun** (1856): equality for all men regardless of religion or ethnicity in education, government appointments, and justice
- Regulated millets (separate religious courts)

**Limits:** Legal reforms mostly benefited men. Women lost some traditional rights under new secular courts. Reforms in the military and professions didn't affect women who were excluded from those spheres. Sultan Abdulhamid (after 1876) eventually reversed many reforms, drove reformers ("Young Turks") into exile, and oversaw the **Hamidian massacres** (1894–1896): 100,000–250,000 Armenians killed — earning him the nickname "the Red Sultan."

## Chinese Reform Efforts

**Self-Strengthening Movement** (late 19th c.): advance military technology, train artisans for shipyards and arsenals. French and British advisors helped. Government also established a diplomatic corps and customs service. Strategy: graft modern technology onto Chinese tradition, avoid systemic change.

After China's defeat in the **Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)**, pressure for deeper reform intensified:
- **Hundred Days of Reform** (1898): Emperor Guangxu + civil servant Kang Youwei; proposed abolishing the civil service exam, eliminating corruption, creating Western-style industrial/commercial/medical systems
- **Empress Dowager Cixi**: staged a coup, imprisoned the emperor, immediately repealed the reforms; blocked railroads and telegraphs from the Chinese interior
- Eventually even Cixi recognized civil service corruption — China finally **abolished the 2,500-year-old civil service exam system**
- **1911**: China became a republic

---

## Japan: Resistance and Adaptation

Japan undertook the most successful non-Western industrialization — but it generated backlash:
- **Samurai** were paid final lump sums (1871) and legally dissolved as a class — no longer allowed to carry swords
- Some became **genros** (elder statesmen); others (especially from Satsuma and Choshu provinces) resisted violently
- Last battle between samurai and emperor's forces: **1870s**
- By the 1920s, army officers again dominated government, rolling back democratic reforms
