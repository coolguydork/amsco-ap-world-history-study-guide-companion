# Topic 5.8 — People, States & Movements

> [!tip]
> On the AP exam, people matter because of what they *did* and what they *represent* — not biographical details. For each person below, ask: What did they argue? Who did they affect? What changed because of them?

---

## Intellectual Reformers in the West

### John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)

Mill was a British philosopher who became the most prominent voice of **[[Unit-5.8/Key Terms & Concepts#Utilitarianism|utilitarianism]]** — the idea that the right policy is whatever produces the greatest good for the greatest number. Mill applied this framework to the problems of industrial capitalism and came out as a cautious but committed reformer.

**What he advocated:** Legalizing labor unions, limiting child labor, ensuring safe factory conditions. He did *not* want to replace capitalism, but to reform its worst abuses through legislation.

**Why he matters for the AP exam:** Mill represents a **moderate, gradualist response** to industrial problems — a middle ground between laissez-faire defenders and socialist radicals. He is often contrasted with Marx on exam prompts.

**Legacy:** Many of Mill's ideas — unions, child labor laws, factory safety — became mainstream policy in Britain and beyond by the early 20th century.

---

### Karl Marx (1818–1883)

A German philosopher and economist, Marx is the most consequential political thinker of the 19th century. His ideas directly inspired the Russian Revolution (1917), the Chinese Communist Revolution (1949), and dozens of liberation movements worldwide.

**Core argument:** Capitalism was not a natural or eternal system — it was a **historical stage** that concentrated wealth in the hands of the [[Unit-5.8/Key Terms & Concepts#Bourgeoisie|bourgeoisie]] while impoverishing the [[Unit-5.8/Key Terms & Concepts#Proletariat|proletariat]]. Market competition forced capitalists to exploit workers; the system was structurally exploitative, not just unfair in practice.

**His solution:** The proletariat must recognize their shared class interest, seize control of the means of production, and build first **socialism** and then **communism** — a classless, stateless society.

**[[Unit-5.8/Key Terms & Concepts#The Communist Manifesto (1848)|Communist Manifesto]] (1848):** Written with Engels, this pamphlet laid out the framework in accessible, urgent language. "Workers of the world, unite!" was its call to action.

**Why he scorned utopian socialists:** Marx called other socialists "utopian" because they imagined ideal communities rather than analyzing the real workings of capitalism. He called his own approach "scientific socialism" — rooted in historical and economic analysis.

> [!exam-tip]
> Marx is almost always contrasted with Mill on AP prompts: Mill = reform capitalism; Marx = replace capitalism. Both responded to the same industrial conditions, but drew very different conclusions.

---

### Friedrich Engels (1820–1895)

Marx's intellectual partner and financial backer. Engels came from a wealthy industrial family with factories in Manchester and Germany — giving him firsthand knowledge of working conditions. His *Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) provided much of the empirical grounding for Marxist theory. He co-authored the *Communist Manifesto* and helped edit and publish Marx's later work.

---

### Mary Harris "Mother" Jones (1837–1930)

An Irish-American labor organizer who spent decades advocating for miners and child workers. Her famous quote — "For a second's sunlight, men must fight like tigers" — captures the dehumanizing reality of underground mine work. She exemplifies the **grassroots labor activist** tradition that paralleled but was distinct from Marxist intellectual theory.

## Ottoman Leaders

### Sultan Mahmud II (r. 1808–1839)

The architect of the **first wave** of Ottoman modernization — before the formal Tanzimat period. His key moves:

- **Abolished the Janissaries (1826):** The old elite military corps that had repeatedly blocked reform. When Istanbul Janissaries revolted, he had them massacred — a shocking but decisive act. The abolition of feudal tax-farming in 1831 completed their defeat.
- **Military modernization:** Rebuilt the army along European lines, trained by European officers.
- **Administrative reform:** Created European-style government ministries, established a postal service, built roads.
- **Centralized revenue:** Tax collection went directly to the government (not to military officers), ensuring bureaucratic loyalty.

**Significance:** Mahmud II shows that Ottoman reform was not a sudden 1839 invention — it had roots in earlier, often violent, state-building efforts.

---

### Sultan Abdulhamid II (r. 1876–1909)

A contradictory ruler: he initially accepted the 1876 constitution and continued education expansion and legal secularization. But fearing "seditious" reform, he became increasingly authoritarian.

- **Exiled the Young Turks** — a reform movement that would eventually overthrow his regime.
- **Hamidian Massacres (1894–1896):** His government incited violence against Armenians and Assyrian Christians; between 100,000 and 250,000 Armenians were killed. He earned the nickname "the Red Sultan."
- **Legacy:** His reign shows the **limits of top-down reform** when a ruler prioritizes regime survival over modernization.

## Chinese Leaders and Reformers

### Empress Dowager Cixi (1835–1908)

The dominant political force in late Qing China. Cixi was the emperor's aunt and adopted mother, and she wielded power behind the throne through two emperors' reigns.

**Conservative phase:** She staged a coup against Emperor Guangxu, imprisoned him, and immediately repealed the Hundred Days of Reform (1898). She blocked railroad and telegraph expansion to limit foreign influence. She is often portrayed as the chief obstacle to Chinese modernization.

**Later shift:** Toward the end of her reign, she acknowledged some need for change and allowed reform of the civil service exam system — China's 2,500-year-old tradition of Confucian-based scholar selection. But her overall conservatism left China too weak to effectively resist foreign pressure.

> [!warning]
> Cixi is often unfairly simplified as simply "anti-reform." The AP exam expects nuance: she opposed reform when it threatened dynastic power, but eventually made concessions. Her conservatism was strategic, not merely ignorant.

---

### Emperor Guangxu (r. 1875–1908)

The Qing emperor who supported the reformer **Kang Youwei** and issued the Hundred Days of Reform edicts (1898). His reform effort was genuine but politically fatal — Cixi outmaneuvered him, imprisoned him, and ruled until her death in 1908. Guangxu died the day before Cixi, under suspicious circumstances.

**Significance:** His story illustrates how **political power structures within a government can block reform** even when an individual ruler is willing. Compare to Japan, where the emperor's authority was backed by a reformist coalition of elites.

---

### Kang Youwei

A Qing civil servant and leading reform intellectual. He organized clubs calling for change after China's humiliating defeat in the [[Concept Notes/Sino-Japanese War|Sino-Japanese War]] (1894–1895). He persuaded Emperor Guangxu to attempt the Hundred Days of Reform. After Cixi's coup, he fled abroad and continued advocating for constitutional monarchy in China.

## Japanese Actors

### The Samurai as a Reactionary Force

The samurai were the warrior class whose privileges were dissolved by the Meiji state in 1871. They received a final lump-sum payment, lost the right to carry swords, and saw bushido reduced from official code to private practice.

Responses varied:
- **Adaptation:** Some became **genros** (elder statesmen) or entered government and business.
- **Resistance:** Others — especially from Satsuma and Choshu provinces — fought back. The final samurai uprising was crushed in the 1870s.

**The great irony:** Many of the samurai who led the resistance had originally *supported* the Meiji Restoration in the 1860s. They wanted to modernize Japan to protect it from the West — but found the modernization itself destroying their world.

## Movements

### Labor Movement (Britain)
A grassroots political force that combined union organizing, parliamentary lobbying, and public pressure to win workers' rights. It operated across multiple fronts: economic (wage bargaining), political (voting rights campaigns), and legislative (child labor and factory laws). The labor movement shows how **industrialization created new forms of political participation** for groups previously excluded from power.

### Young Turks
A reform movement in the late Ottoman Empire, driven mostly into exile by Abdulhamid II. They eventually returned and led the coup that deposed Abdulhamid in 1909, establishing a constitutional government. Their story continues in [[Unit-7.2/Decline of Empires|Decline of Empires]].

### Meiji Reformers vs. Samurai Conservatives
Within Japan, the Meiji Restoration created an internal fault line: progressive modernizers (often former samurai who had embraced Western ideas) vs. traditional conservatives (samurai who saw modernization as cultural erasure). This tension — rapid change producing conservative backlash — is a recurring historical pattern the AP exam loves to test.
