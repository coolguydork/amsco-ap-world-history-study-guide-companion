# People, States & Movements

*Source: AMSCO pp. 332–342*

---

## Key Individuals

**Karl Marx** (1818–1883)
German philosopher, economist, and revolutionary thinker. Author of the *Communist Manifesto* (1848, with Engels) and *Das Kapital* (1867). Marx argued that capitalism was inherently exploitative — the bourgeoisie owned the means of production and extracted profit from proletarian labor. His "scientific socialism" rejected utopian alternatives as escapist; he wanted to analyze how capitalism actually worked and predict its inevitable collapse. His ideas became the intellectual foundation for socialist and communist movements worldwide — from Russia's 1917 revolution to Mao's China. On the AP exam, Marx is the pivotal intellectual figure of this topic and a frequent comparison target against Mill.

**Friedrich Engels** (1820–1895)
Marx's lifelong collaborator and financial patron. His *Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) provided the empirical evidence of industrial misery that grounded Marxist theory. Co-authored the *Communist Manifesto*. Without Engels's support, Marx likely could not have completed his major works.

**Robert Owen** (1771–1858)
British factory owner who became a leading utopian socialist. He improved conditions in his own mills at New Lanark (Scotland) and argued that a good environment produced good workers and citizens. Attempted to establish model communities in Britain and America. Represents the reformist, idealistic wing of socialist thought that Marx later criticized.

**John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873)
British philosopher and liberal reformer. His philosophy of utilitarianism — "the greatest good for the greatest number" — provided the intellectual basis for moderate industrial reform. Championed labor unions, child labor laws, and safe factory conditions. Sought to improve capitalism rather than replace it. Key contrast with Marx: Mill = reform within the system; Marx = replace the system.

**Samuel Gompers** (1850–1924)
Founder of the American Federation of Labor (AFL); the leading figure in the American labor movement. Focused on practical "bread and butter" gains — wages, hours, conditions — rather than revolutionary politics. Represents the trade union path to improving workers' lives within capitalism.

**Mahmud II** (r. 1808–1839)
Ottoman sultan who initiated the modernization of the empire: abolished the Janissaries (1826), centralized tax collection, built infrastructure, and created European-style ministries. His reforms were necessary but insufficient — the empire continued to decline relative to European powers.

**Sultan Abdulhamid II** (r. 1876–1909)
Initially accepted the Ottoman constitution and continued reform, but became increasingly authoritarian. Drove "Young Turk" reformers into exile and incited violence against Armenian minorities (Hamidian massacres, 1894–1896). Earned the nickname "the Red Sultan." Represents the conservative backlash within the Ottoman reform movement.

**Emperor Guangxu** (r. 1875–1908)
Chinese emperor who supported the Hundred Days of Reform (1898); imprisoned by Empress Cixi after her coup reversed the reforms. Represents the reform faction within the Qing court that was ultimately defeated.

**Empress Dowager Cixi** (1835–1908)
The most powerful political figure in late Qing China. Initially blocked Western-style reforms, imprisoned Emperor Guangxu, and halted railroad and telegraph expansion. Later, under pressure, she accepted the abolition of the civil service exam. Her conservatism is widely seen as a key factor in China's failure to modernize fast enough.

## Key Movements and States

**Luddite Movement (c. 1811–1816)**
British artisans who destroyed factory machinery to protest the destruction of skilled craft work by industrialization. The Luddites were among the earliest organized forms of working-class resistance to industrial capitalism. The term "Luddite" later became shorthand for opposition to technology.

**Labor Union Movement**
The collective effort by industrial workers to bargain with employers. Unions won minimum wages, limits on hours, overtime pay, and the five-day work week. Illegal in Britain through most of the 19th century; gained legal recognition by the 20th. Sparked broader political movements for voting rights and social reform.

**Utopian Socialists**
Thinkers and activists who tried to create model communities demonstrating alternatives to capitalism. Robert Owen (New Lanark, Scotland; New Harmony, Indiana) was the most prominent. Marx dismissed them as dreamers who wanted to escape capitalism rather than analyze and overturn it.

**Ottoman Tanzimat Reformers**
Government officials and sultans (especially Mahmud II and Abdülmecid I) who pushed the Ottoman reorganization reforms (1839–1876). Their goal was to modernize the empire enough to compete with European powers while maintaining Ottoman sovereignty. Succeeded in modernizing law and education; failed to prevent gradual territorial losses.

**Young Turks**
Reform-minded Ottoman officials who pushed for constitutional government and modernization; exiled by Sultan Abdulhamid II. Eventually returned to power and deposed Abdulhamid in 1908. They continued to reform but also promoted Turkish nationalism.

**Self-Strengthening Movement (China, c. 1861–1895)**
Led by officials like Li Hongzhang and Zeng Guofan; aimed to modernize China's military and industry while preserving Confucian values. Strategy: "Chinese learning as the base, Western learning for practical use." The Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895) exposed its failure to truly modernize China's military capacity.

**Meiji Government (Japan, 1868–1912)**
Launched Japan's rapid Westernization after the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]]: abolished the feudal domain system, dissolved the samurai class, established compulsory education, built railroads and industry, and created a Western-style constitution. Japan's success in modernizing is often contrasted with China's failure.

> [!exam-tip]
> For comparison questions, remember the three paths: **Bottom-up** (labor unions, working class), **Intellectual** (Mill's reform, Marx's revolution), **Top-down government** (Ottoman Tanzimat, China's reforms, Meiji Japan). The AP exam frequently asks you to explain *why* different groups or governments responded differently to industrialization.

> [!warning]
> Don't say the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] *caused* Japan's industrialization. The Restoration created the political conditions for rapid industrialization — but the decision to industrialize was a deliberate government policy choice made *after* 1868.
