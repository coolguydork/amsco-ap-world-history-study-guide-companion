# Topic 5.8 — Karl Marx & Socialism

**AMSCO:** pp. 333–335

---

## The Intellectual Landscape

The brutal realities of industrial capitalism provoked two major intellectual responses: **utilitarianism** (reform capitalism from within) and **Marxism** (replace capitalism entirely). Both emerged from Enlightenment-era thinking about reason, justice, and human potential, but they arrived at very different conclusions.

---

## John Stuart Mill and Utilitarianism

**John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873): British philosopher and the leading voice of **utilitarianism** in the 19th century.

**Core principle:** "The greatest good for the greatest number of people." Rather than state eternal moral rules, utilitarianism judged actions and policies by their outcomes — did they increase well-being?

**Mill's specific reforms:**
- Legalize labor unions
- Limit child labor
- Mandate safe working conditions in factories

**Mill's position:** He did NOT want to destroy capitalism. He wanted to **fix its worst abuses** through legislation. He viewed himself as a moderate, rational reformer — a practical alternative to Marx's radicalism.

> [!tip]
> Mill and Marx are perfect comparison figures for the AP exam. Both responded to industrial capitalism's inequalities, but Mill argued for reform within the system; Marx argued the system was irreparably flawed and must be replaced.

---

## Karl Marx: Scientific Socialism

**Karl Marx** (1818–1883): German philosopher and economist. Where utopian socialists wanted to escape capitalism by creating ideal communities, Marx wanted to **understand how capitalism actually worked** — and predict how it would inevitably collapse.

He called his approach **"scientific socialism"**: systematic, historical analysis rather than wishful idealism.

### The Communist Manifesto (1848)

Written with his patron and collaborator **Friedrich Engels**, the *Communist Manifesto* is the foundational text of Marxism. Its opening line — "A specter is haunting Europe — the specter of communism" — set its urgent tone.

**Marx's argument in four steps:**

1. **Capitalism is an advance over feudalism** — it created enormous wealth and productive capacity

2. **But capitalism generates needless poverty** — because it divides society into two antagonistic classes:
   - **Proletariat**: the working class — work in factories and mines; own only their labor; paid subsistence wages
   - **Bourgeoisie**: the owning class — own the means of production (factories, machines, land); receive most of the wealth

3. **Market competition drives exploitation**: the bourgeoisie must squeeze labor costs to compete → workers receive as little as possible while doing dangerous, exhausting work

4. **The inevitable sequence**: workers will recognize their shared class interests → **socialist revolution** → the proletariat takes control of the means of production → wealth shared fairly → eventually **communism** (no class distinctions, state withers away)

> [!exam-tip]
> Know these three stages: capitalism → socialism → communism. The AP exam frequently asks about Marxism's influence on 20th-century revolutions in Russia (1917), China (1949), Cuba, Vietnam, etc. Marx's ideas had enormous historical consequences.

---

## Marx vs. Utopian Socialists

Marx was **contemptuous** of utopian socialists like Robert Owen and Charles Fourier. He thought they wanted to run away from capitalism's problems rather than confront them. He called his own approach superior because it was grounded in historical analysis, not fantasy.

| | Utopian Socialists | Karl Marx |
|--|-------------------|-----------|
| Approach | Create ideal communities as alternatives | Analyze capitalism scientifically; predict its collapse |
| Change through... | Voluntary communities; demonstration projects | Class struggle; revolution |
| View of capitalism | Flawed; escape it | Doomed by internal contradictions; replace it |
| Examples | Owen (New Lanark), Fourier (communities) | *Communist Manifesto*; *Das Kapital* |
| Legacy | Limited, mostly failed communities | Soviet Union, Maoist China, Cuba, global left movements |

---

## Marxism's Global Impact

Marx wrote in the 1840s–1880s, but his ideas reverberated throughout the 20th century:
- **Russia**: Bolshevik Revolution (1917) — Lenin adapted Marxism → Soviet Union
- **China**: Communist Revolution (1949) — Mao adapted Marxism → People's Republic of China
- **Decolonization**: Many independence movements drew on Marxist analysis of imperial exploitation
- **Labor movements**: Marxist ideas strengthened union organizing worldwide

> [!warning]
> Marx himself would have been surprised and possibly horrified by many 20th-century communist regimes. He expected revolution in the most industrialized countries (Germany, Britain) — instead it happened first in agricultural Russia and China.
