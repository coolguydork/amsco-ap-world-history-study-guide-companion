# Topic 5.8 — Labor Unions & Reform

**AMSCO:** pp. 333–335

---

## Working Conditions That Demanded Reform

Factory work in the 19th century was brutal. Workers — including young children — endured:

- **14-hour days, six days a week**
- Dangerous machinery with no safety guards
- Coal dust and textile fiber that destroyed lungs
- Wages too low to survive without sending children to work
- No overtime pay, no sick leave, no vacation, no minimum wage

> [!tip]
> Use Charles Dickens's novels (*Oliver Twist*, *Hard Times*) as supporting evidence on AP exams — they depicted exactly these conditions and are contemporaneous primary sources.

### The Sadler Report (1833)
A British parliamentary committee led by Michael Sadler produced a report documenting factory conditions in meticulous, horrifying detail. Its publication shocked Parliament and the educated public, creating political pressure for reform. It is a landmark document in labor history.

## Labor Unions

A **labor union** is an organization of workers that negotiates collectively with employers — using the threat of a **strike** (work stoppage) as leverage.

- For most of the 19th century, British unions operated **in secret** — the government treated them as enemies of trade
- By the 20th century, unions became accepted and membership expanded
- Workers in other industrializing countries (Germany, France, United States) also organized

### What Unions Won
Through strikes, collective bargaining, and political pressure, unions eventually achieved:
- **Minimum wage** laws
- **Maximum hours** limits
- **Overtime pay**
- **Five-day work week**
- Workplace safety regulations

> [!exam-tip]
> Unions are a key example of **workers using collective action to change their conditions** — a bottom-up response to industrialization. Contrast this with the top-down approach of Bismarck's social insurance reforms (see [[Unit-5.10/Overview|Overview]]).

## Child Labor Reforms

Reformers targeted child labor as one of the most visible injustices:
- Children as young as **5** worked in textile mills
- Children in coal mines faced extreme heat, heavy loads, lung damage from dust, and risk of collapse or flooding
- Small bodies and nimble fingers made children "useful" for climbing into machinery

**Key legislation (Britain):**
- **1843**: children under age 10 banned from working in coal mines
- **1881**: education made mandatory for children aged 5–10 — redefined childhood as a time for learning, not labor

## Voting Rights: The Reform Bills

Unions sparked a broader movement for political empowerment. Britain's **Parliamentary Reform Bills** progressively expanded who could vote:

| Reform Bill | Year | Change |
|------------|------|--------|
| First | 1832 | Extended vote to middle-class men; gave new industrial cities representation |
| Second | 1867 | Extended to urban working-class men |
| Third | 1884 | Extended to rural working-class men |
| (Full male suffrage) | 1918 | All men could vote |
| (Women's suffrage) | 1928 | All women gained equal suffrage |

Each bill reduced **property-ownership requirements** for voting. The pattern illustrates how industrial capitalism created pressures for democratic reform — workers who built the economy demanded a voice in governing it.

## "Mother" Jones and Labor Organizing

**Mary Harris "Mother" Jones** (1837–1930): American labor organizer who described the suffering of coal miners who spent their entire days underground. Her quote — about men fighting "like tigers" for the privilege of seeing their children's eyes in sunlight — captures the human cost of industrial capitalism. She represents the global dimension of labor organizing, which extended well beyond Britain.
