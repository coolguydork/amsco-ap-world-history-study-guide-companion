# Reactions to the Industrial Economy

**AP Themes:** SIO · CDI · GOV · ECN  
**AP Skills:** Causation  
**Printed pages:** 332–342

**Essential Question:** How did workers, intellectuals, and governments respond to the inequalities created by industrialization?

**Learning Objective:** Explain the causes and effects of the various reactions to the development of industrial economies.

---

## The Big Picture

Industrialization created enormous wealth — and enormous misery. Low wages, brutal working conditions, child labor, and crushing hours provoked resistance on three fronts: workers organized collectively through **labor unions**, intellectuals proposed alternative economic systems from **utilitarianism** to **Marxism**, and governments in the Ottoman Empire, China, and Japan enacted top-down reforms to modernize without losing power. All three paths involved tension between tradition and change, and all three produced both progress and backlash.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam loves this topic for comparison and causation. Be ready to compare different responses to industrialization (workers vs. intellectuals vs. governments) and explain why some reforms succeeded while others failed.

---

## Labor Unions: Workers Respond from Below

The Sadler Report (released by a British parliamentary committee) documented the horrific conditions inside factories — long hours, dangerous machinery, child labor — and shocked the public. Workers' response was to organize. **Labor unions** advocated for the right to collectively bargain with employers, but for most of the 19th century they operated in secret in Britain because the government treated them as threats to trade. By the 20th century, unions gained legal recognition and won concrete gains: minimum wage laws, limits on working hours, overtime pay, and the five-day work week (pp. 332–333).

Union organizing spilled into broader political movements. The British Parliament passed reform bills in the 1830s, 1860s, and 1880s expanding the male voting franchise by reducing property ownership requirements. Universal male suffrage came in the early 1900s; equal suffrage for women not until the late 1920s (p. 333).

Child labor also came under reform. Laws banned children under 10 from coal mines, and by 1881 education became mandatory for British children aged 5–10, permanently redefining childhood away from labor (p. 333).

## The Intellectual Reaction: Reformers and Revolutionaries

### Utopian Socialists and John Stuart Mill

As transnational businesses grew, thinkers questioned capitalism itself. **Utopian socialists** tried to create alternative communities demonstrating that a better system was possible. More influential was **John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873), whose philosophy of **utilitarianism** judged policies by whether they achieved "the greatest good for the greatest number." Mill championed legal reforms — labor unions, child labor limits, safe factory conditions — but worked *within* capitalism, seeking gradual improvement rather than revolution (pp. 333–334).

### Karl Marx: Scientific Socialism

**Karl Marx** (1818–1883) was uninterested in gradual fixes. Scorning utopian socialists as escapists, he called his approach "scientific socialism" — an analysis of how capitalism actually operated and why it would inevitably collapse. In 1848, Marx and **Friedrich Engels** published the *Communist Manifesto*, arguing that capitalism had divided society into two irreconcilable classes:

- The **proletariat** — factory workers who sold their labor for bare survival
- The **bourgeoisie** — owners of the **means of production** (factories, mines, land) who extracted the wealth workers created

Market competition, Marx argued, drove the bourgeoisie to maximize profit by exploiting the proletariat. The solution was for workers to seize control of the means of production. **Socialism** would replace capitalism; then **communism** — a classless society with no private ownership — would be the final stage (p. 334).

> [!tip]
> Key contrast for the AP exam: **Mill** wanted to reform capitalism from within. **Marx** wanted to replace it entirely. Both responded to the same conditions; their diagnoses and prescriptions were opposite.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse socialism and communism. In Marx's framework, socialism is the transitional stage (state controls production) and communism is the final goal (no classes, no state). On the AP exam, Marxism refers to the entire analytical framework.

## Non-Western Responses: Reform from Above

While Western workers and intellectuals grappled with industrialization's social costs, governments in the Ottoman Empire, China, and Japan faced a different pressure: industrialized Western powers were encroaching on their sovereignty. Their response was to modernize — selectively and often reluctantly.

### Ottoman Empire: Tanzimat Reforms

**Mahmud II** (r. 1808–1839) launched early reforms: abolishing the Janissaries (1826), centralizing tax collection, building roads, and creating European-style ministries. His successors continued with the **Tanzimat** ("reorganization") reforms (1839–1876): secularizing education, codifying commercial (1850) and penal (1858) laws, and issuing the **Hatt-i Hümayun** (1856), which declared legal equality for all men regardless of religion or ethnicity. The **millets** system — separate religious courts for non-Muslim communities — was also regulated (pp. 335–336).

The reforms had limits. They benefited men far more than women; the Tanzimat edicts didn't even mention women. Sultan Abdulhamid later cracked down on reformers, exiling "Young Turks" and inciting violence against Armenian minorities — the Hamidian massacres (1894–1896) killed 100,000–250,000 Armenians (p. 336).

### China: Self-Strengthening Movement and Hundred Days

China's **Self-Strengthening Movement** (c. 1860s–1890s) tried to modernize military technology and infrastructure while grafting Western methods onto Chinese tradition — not replacing it. After defeat in the Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895) exposed the movement's inadequacy, reform pressure intensified. **Emperor Guangxu** embraced the **Hundred Days of Reform** (1898): abolishing the civil service exam, eliminating corruption, establishing Western-style institutions. **Empress Cixi** responded with a coup, imprisoned the emperor, and repealed all edicts. Only late in her reign did Cixi accept some reform (pp. 336–337).

### Japan: [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and Backlash

Japan dismantled its samurai class (1871) and launched rapid Westernization through the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]]. Some samurai adapted as **genrō** (elder statesmen); others from Satsuma and Choshu provinces violently resisted. The last samurai rebellion was crushed in the 1870s–1880s. Japan modernized impressively — literacy, industry, democracy's trappings — but by the 1920s military officers had reasserted dominance (pp. 337–338).

> [!tip]
> **Three-way comparison:** Turkey began reforms first but grew conservative under Abdulhamid. China's Cixi started conservative and softened only late. Japan moved fastest and most thoroughly but faced a reactionary backlash. All three show the same pattern: reform provokes resistance from traditional elites.
