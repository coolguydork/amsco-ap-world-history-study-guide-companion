# Topic 5.8 — Key Terms & Concepts

> [!tip]
> These terms appear frequently in AP exam stimulus questions and answer choices. Learn not just the definition but the **significance** — why did this matter? Who opposed it? What changed because of it?

---

## Labor & Workers

### Labor Unions
Organizations of workers that collectively bargained with employers for better wages, hours, and working conditions. In Britain, unions initially operated in secret because the government considered them threats to commerce. By the 20th century, union membership had grown enormously and unions had won major legal victories: minimum wage laws, overtime pay, limits on working hours, and the five-day work week. Unions also sparked broader demands for political representation, directly contributing to voting reform bills in 1832, 1867, and 1884.

> [!exam-tip]
> On the AP exam, labor unions are often connected to the theme of **social structures** and **political change from below**. They represent workers using collective action — a form of grassroots resistance — rather than top-down government reform.

### Sadler Report (1833)
A British parliamentary study that documented the horrific conditions of industrial labor — child labor in mines, dangerous machinery, brutal hours. The report shocked the public and Parliament and helped justify labor legislation. It is an example of how **reform came from investigation and awareness**, not just ideological argument.

### The Franchise (Voting Rights)
The right to vote. British reform bills in 1832, 1867, and 1884 progressively expanded suffrage to more men by reducing property qualifications. Full male suffrage came in 1918; women achieved equal suffrage in 1928. Union activism helped build momentum for these expansions by demonstrating the political capacity of the working class.

## Intellectual Reactions

### Utilitarianism
A philosophical and economic framework championed by **[[Unit-5.8/People, States & Movements#John Stuart Mill|John Stuart Mill]]** (1806–1873). Utilitarianism judged policies by whether they produced "the greatest good for the greatest number." Rather than replace capitalism, utilitarians wanted to reform it — legalizing unions, limiting child labor, improving factory safety. Mill was a moderate: he believed in capitalism but thought it needed guardrails.

> [!tip]
> Think of utilitarianism as **reform capitalism** — keep the system, fix the worst abuses. Contrast with Marxism, which called for replacing capitalism entirely.

### Laissez-Faire Capitalism
French for "leave it alone." The economic idea, traced to Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776), that markets work best with minimal government interference. By the mid-1800s, critics like Mill and Marx argued that laissez-faire policies caused poverty, child labor, and exploitation — the system left to itself produced misery for workers.

## Marxism

### Karl Marx (1818–1883)
German philosopher and economist who developed **scientific socialism** — a theory that history moves through predictable stages of class conflict. See [[Unit-5.8/People, States & Movements#Karl Marx|People, States & Movements]] for his full profile.

### Friedrich Engels (1820–1895)
Marx's wealthy collaborator who co-authored the *Communist Manifesto* (1848). Engels provided financial support and intellectual partnership. His own research into Manchester factory conditions — published as *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) — provided much of the empirical foundation for Marxist critique.

### The Communist Manifesto (1848)
The pamphlet co-written by Marx and Engels that summarized their critique of capitalism and called workers to action. Its opening line — "A specter is haunting Europe — the specter of communism" — deliberately invoked fear of a new revolutionary force. It introduced the core framework of bourgeoisie vs. proletariat.

### Proletariat
In Marxist theory, the **working class** — factory workers, miners, and laborers who owned no means of production. The proletariat sold their labor to survive. Marx argued they were systematically exploited: they did the physical work but received only subsistence wages while the bourgeoisie pocketed the profits.

### Bourgeoisie
In Marxist theory, the **middle class and capitalist owners** — those who owned factories, machinery, mines, and land. The bourgeoisie profited from the labor of the proletariat. Marx did not see them as villains by character; rather, market competition *forced* them to maximize profits by minimizing what they paid workers.

### Means of Production
The factories, machines, land, and raw materials needed to produce goods. Control of the means of production is, in Marxist theory, the source of class power. Whoever owns them owns the wealth; whoever is excluded from them must sell their labor for wages.

### Socialism → Communism (Marx's Stages)
For Marx, capitalism would eventually collapse under its own contradictions. It would be replaced by **socialism** — worker control of production — which would then give way to **communism**, a classless society with no private property and no state. Marx called communism the endpoint of historical development.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse Marx's theoretical communism with the 20th-century Soviet Union or Maoist China. Marx died in 1883, long before these regimes existed. His ideas were interpreted and applied (and distorted) in very different ways later.

## Ottoman Terms

### Tanzimat (1839–1876)
Arabic/Turkish for "reorganization." The sweeping series of reforms carried out under Ottoman sultans in the mid-19th century. Key changes included: secular law codes (commercial 1850, penal 1858), a secular education system replacing ulama control, root-and-branch anti-corruption efforts, and the **Hatt-i Humayun** edict of 1856.

### Hatt-i Humayun (1856)
The Ottoman Reform Edict of 1856, issued by Sultan Abdulmecid I. It declared legal equality for all Ottoman subjects regardless of religion or ethnicity — in education, government appointments, and justice. It also regulated the **millets** system. It was controversial: Christians in the Balkans feared losing autonomy; conservative Muslims protested its break with tradition.

### Millets
Separate legal courts established by different religious communities within the Ottoman Empire, each using its own religious law. The Tanzimat reforms sought to regulate the millets and integrate them into a more uniform legal system — which pleased some and angered others.

### Mahmud II (r. 1808–1839)
The Ottoman sultan who launched the first major wave of modernization reforms *before* the Tanzimat. He abolished the [[Concept Notes/Janissaries|Janissaries]] in 1826 (massacring those who revolted), built roads, created a postal service, established European-style government ministries, and reorganized the military along European lines. He also ended feudal tax collection to centralize government revenue.

### Sultan Abdulhamid II (r. 1876–1909)
Initially supported reform and accepted a constitution. But fearful of losing power, he drove reformers (the "[[Concept Notes/Young Turks|Young Turks]]") into exile and tightened authoritarian control. He incited violence against Armenians and Assyrian Christians — the **Hamidian Massacres** (1894–1896) killed between 100,000 and 250,000 Armenians. He earned the nickname "the Red Sultan."

## Chinese Terms

### Self-Strengthening Movement
China's major reform effort of the late 19th century under the Qing Dynasty. The goal was to modernize China's military technology and build arsenals and shipyards — to "strengthen" China enough to resist foreign pressure — while grafting modern ideas onto Chinese tradition rather than replacing it. French and British advisors assisted. The movement was pragmatic but limited; it did not reform China's political structure.

### Hundred Days of Reform (1898)
A set of sweeping reform edicts issued by **Emperor Guangxu** after meeting with reformer **Kang Youwei**. The reforms included abolishing the outdated civil service exam, eliminating corruption, and establishing Western-style industrial, commercial, and medical systems. The reforms lasted only about 100 days before **Empress Dowager Cixi** staged a coup, imprisoned the emperor, and repealed the edicts.

### Empress Dowager Cixi (1835–1908)
The most powerful political figure in late Qing China. Initially a conservative who blocked reform and imprisoned Emperor Guangxu, she famously halted railroad and telegraph expansion to limit foreign influence. Ironically, toward the end of her rule she came to acknowledge the need for some change — particularly reforming the civil service — but her overall conservatism prevented effective modernization.

### Kang Youwei
A Qing civil servant and intellectual who led the reform club that persuaded Emperor Guangxu to attempt the Hundred Days of Reform. He represented the strand of Chinese reformers who believed China could modernize without abandoning its cultural core — a Chinese version of Japan's Meiji approach.

### Emperor Guangxu
The Qing emperor who supported the Hundred Days of Reform (1898). He was politically outmaneuvered and imprisoned by Empress Dowager Cixi, who wielded the real power at court. His reform effort's failure illustrates how **personal power dynamics within a government can block reform** even when a ruler is willing.

## Japanese Terms

### Genros
Elder statesmen in Meiji Japan — often former samurai who adapted to the new order by serving the modernized government as senior advisors and political figures. The genros wielded significant informal influence over the Meiji state.

### Samurai (as reactionary force)
Under the Meiji Restoration, the samurai class lost its legal privileges. In 1871, samurai received a lump-sum payment and their status was formally dissolved — they could no longer carry swords or receive stipends. Some adapted (becoming genros or bureaucrats); others, especially from Satsuma and Choshu provinces, resisted. The final samurai uprising was crushed by the 1870s. The samurai became, ironically, the **primary victims of the very modernization some of them had helped launch**.

### Bushido
The samurai code of conduct — emphasizing loyalty, honor, and martial discipline. Under the Meiji reforms, bushido was demoted from an official state code to a private personal matter. It nonetheless persisted culturally and would be revived and militarized by the Japanese state in the early 20th century.
