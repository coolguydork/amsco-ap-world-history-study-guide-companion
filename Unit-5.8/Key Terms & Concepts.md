# Key Terms & Concepts

*Source: AMSCO pp. 332–342*

---

**socialism**
Political-economic system advocating collective or government ownership of the means of production; broader than communism and takes many forms; emerged as a response to the inequalities of industrial capitalism. Both **utopian socialists** and Marx used this term, though they disagreed sharply on method.

**Karl Marx**
German philosopher and economist (1818–1883) who developed "scientific socialism"; argued capitalism inevitably exploited workers and would be replaced first by socialism, then by communism; co-authored the *Communist Manifesto* (1848) with Engels; also wrote *Das Kapital* (1867). His ideas spawned global revolutionary movements across the 20th century.

**Friedrich Engels**
Marx's collaborator and financial supporter; co-authored the *Communist Manifesto* (1848); his *Condition of the Working Class in England* documented industrial poverty through firsthand observation. Without Engels's financial support, Marx likely could not have written *Das Kapital*.

**Communist Manifesto**
Pamphlet by Marx and Engels (1848) analyzing capitalism as a system that divided society into bourgeoisie and proletariat; called on workers to unite and overthrow the capitalist order; foundational text of communist and socialist movements worldwide. Its final line — "Workers of the world, unite!" — became a rallying cry.

**means of production**
Marxist term for the capital assets — factories, machines, mines, land — used to produce goods. The bourgeoisie's ownership of these assets is what gave them power over the proletariat; workers owned only their labor. Control of the means of production is at the center of Marx's class analysis.

**communism**
Political-economic system in which the state owns the means of production and property is communally held; in Marx's framework, it is the final stage of historical development — after capitalism gives way to socialism, socialism would eventually give way to a classless, stateless communist society.

**John Stuart Mill**
British philosopher (1806–1873) whose philosophy of utilitarianism sought "the greatest good for the greatest number"; championed legal reforms for labor unions, child labor limits, and safe factory conditions; sought to reform capitalism from within rather than replace it. Represents the moderate, reformist response to industrialization — in contrast to Marx.

**utilitarianism**
Philosophical approach (associated with Mill) that judges policies and actions by whether they produce the greatest good for the greatest number of people. Rather than timeless moral rules, utilitarians asked: what works? This pragmatic approach drove moderate welfare reform — contrasted with Marxist revolution and with utopian escapism.

**labor unions**
Worker organizations that bargained collectively with employers for better wages, hours, and conditions; illegal in Britain for most of the 19th century (had to organize in secret), but by the 20th century they gained legal recognition. Won minimum wage laws, overtime pay, limits on working hours, and the five-day work week. Samuel Gompers founded the AFL in the U.S.

**proletariat**
Marxist term for the industrial working class — those who sell their labor for wages; work in factories and mines for minimal compensation. Contrasted with the bourgeoisie. Marx argued the proletariat would eventually recognize their shared class interest and overthrow the capitalist system.

**bourgeoisie**
Marxist term for the capitalist class who own the means of production; extract profit from the labor of the proletariat. In Marx's analysis, market competition forces the bourgeoisie to maximize profits by exploiting workers — it's structural, not personal. The bourgeoisie's ownership of factories, land, and machines is the source of their class power.

**bushido**
The Japanese warrior code of the samurai, emphasizing loyalty, frugality, martial arts mastery, and honor unto death; parallel to European chivalry. After the [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] dissolved the samurai class (1871), bushido became a cultural ideal rather than a legal status — preserved as personal identity even as the samurai's political role ended.

**genrō**
Elder statesmen in Meiji Japan; former samurai who adapted to the end of their warrior status by serving the new government as senior political advisors. Illustrates how Japan co-opted traditional elites rather than simply destroying them — a key reason Japan's modernization was more stable than China's.

**Mahmud II**
Ottoman sultan (r. 1808–1839) who launched early modernizing reforms: abolished and massacred the Janissaries (1826), centralized tax collection, built roads, established a postal service, and created European-style government ministries. His reforms set the stage for the Tanzimat period.

**Tanzimat**
Ottoman "reorganization" reform period (1839–1876); secularized education and law, codified commercial (1850) and penal (1858) codes, reduced government corruption, and issued the Hatt-i Hümayun (1856). Created partial legal equality for non-Muslims, though practical implementation was uneven. Reforms were resisted by both conservative Muslims and non-Muslim minorities who feared loss of autonomy.

**Hatt-i Hümayun**
Ottoman Reform Edict (1856) declaring equality for all men in education, government appointments, and justice regardless of religion or ethnicity; part of the Tanzimat reforms; also regulated the millets system. Opposed by Muslims (violated Islamic tradition) and by Christians (threatened their autonomous millet courts).

**millets**
Separate legal courts for different religious communities (Christian, Jewish, Armenian) within the Ottoman Empire, each applying its own religious law. The Tanzimat reforms sought to regulate and partially standardize them; Christian communities in the Balkans protested these changes, fearing loss of legal autonomy.

**Self-Strengthening Movement**
Chinese reform effort (c. 1860s–1895) that tried to modernize military technology and train artisans for shipyards and arsenals while preserving Chinese cultural tradition — "Chinese learning as the base, Western learning for practical use." Exposed as insufficient after China's defeat in the Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895).

**Emperor Guangxu**
Chinese emperor (Guangxu; r. 1875–1908) who supported the Hundred Days of Reform (1898); imprisoned by Empress Cixi after her coup ended the reforms. Represents the reform-minded faction within the Qing court that was ultimately defeated by conservative resistance.

**Hundred Days of Reform**
Sweeping 1898 reforms by Emperor Guangxu: abolishing the outdated civil service exam, eliminating corruption, and establishing Western-style industrial, commercial, and medical systems. Ended after 103 days when Empress Cixi staged a coup, imprisoned the emperor, and repealed all edicts.

**Empress Cixi**
Empress Dowager of China (1835–1908); initially conservative, she staged a coup against Emperor Guangxu's Hundred Days of Reform and imprisoned him; blocked railroad and telegraph expansion to limit foreign influence. Later in her reign she accepted some civil service reform, but her overall conservatism is seen as a key reason China failed to modernize fast enough to resist Western and Japanese pressure.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam frequently compares reform responses across regions. Know this trio: **Ottoman Tanzimat** (top-down legal reforms, partial success), **China Self-Strengthening / Hundred Days** (incomplete, reversed by conservatism), **Meiji Japan** (rapid, comprehensive, provoked samurai backlash). All three were responses to the same external pressure from industrialized Western powers.
