# Topic 5.8 — Comparative Responses to Industrialization

> [!exam-tip]
> Comparison is one of the AP's core historical thinking skills. This topic is *designed* for comparison prompts. Be ready to compare any two or all three of these societies — Ottoman Empire, China, Japan — on reform efforts, limits to reform, and outcomes. This note is your cheat sheet.

---

## The Core Question

All three societies — the Ottoman Empire, China, and Japan — faced the same external pressure in the 19th century: industrializing Western powers that threatened their sovereignty through economic coercion, military superiority, and territorial ambition. Each chose a different path. Why did those paths differ? And what were the results?

---

## Side-by-Side Comparison Table

| Category | Ottoman Empire | China (Qing) | Japan (Meiji) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trigger for reform** | Military defeats + Western economic penetration | Opium Wars + Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) | Perry's arrival (1853) + threat of colonization |
| **Reform start date** | 1808 (Mahmud II); formal Tanzimat 1839 | Late 1860s (Self-Strengthening) | 1868 (Meiji Restoration) |
| **Pace** | Gradual, uneven | Slow, then stalled | Rapid, systematic |
| **Depth** | Modernized military, law, education — but political structure resisted | Modernized military/shipyards — avoided political restructuring | Abolished feudalism, built constitutional monarchy, mass education, modern military |
| **Women** | Reforms largely excluded women; Tanzimat didn't mention them; some Mahmud II reforms *reduced* women's property rights | Women largely unaffected by Self-Strengthening or Hundred Days reforms | Female mill workers exploited — same as Western pattern |
| **Key reformers** | Mahmud II, Tanzimat sultans | Kang Youwei, Emperor Guangxu | Meiji oligarchs, former samurai reformers |
| **Key conservatives** | Sultan Abdulhamid II | Empress Dowager Cixi | Satsuma/Choshu samurai |
| **Treatment of conservatives** | Exiled (Young Turks); massacred minorities | Cixi imprisoned the emperor | Samurai crushed militarily by 1870s |
| **Outcome by 1900** | Empire weakening; ethnic nationalism rising; "sick man of Europe" | Qing on the verge of collapse; foreign spheres of influence established | Rising industrial and military power; soon to defeat Russia (1905) |
| **Long-term outcome** | Collapsed after WWI; became Republic of Turkey | Revolution 1911; republic, then civil war | Aggressive imperial expansion; WWII; defeat; democratization |

## What Each Society Got Right (and Wrong)

### Ottoman Empire — Early Reformer, Uneven Results

**Strengths:** The Tanzimat reforms were genuinely ambitious — secular law codes, modern schools, anti-corruption drives, military modernization. They began earlier than China or Japan's equivalent efforts.

**Weaknesses:** Reform was top-down and dependent on each sultan's will. Abdulhamid II reversed the liberal direction and used reform as a tool of centralization rather than genuine modernization. The reforms also failed to address the empire's fundamental problem: ethnic and religious diversity that European powers could exploit. The millets system gave autonomy to minorities, but Abdulhamid's violence against Armenians showed how fragile that protection was.

**The gender gap:** The Tanzimat reforms of 1839 didn't even mention women. Mahmud II's legal secularization *reduced* women's property rights under shariah. This is a key AP detail — modernization often benefited some groups while harming or ignoring others.

---

### China — Late, Limited, and Stalled

**Strengths:** The Self-Strengthening Movement showed practical intelligence — build arsenals, train a modern military, use foreign advisors. The Hundred Days of Reform (1898) was genuinely sweeping in ambition.

**Weaknesses:** The Self-Strengthening Movement tried to modernize China's tools without changing its political structure — "Chinese learning as the base, Western learning for use" (zhōng xué wéi tǐ, xī xué wéi yòng). This was not enough. The Hundred Days of Reform was crushed by Cixi before it could take effect. China's weakness was not ignorance of reform but **political dysfunction**: Cixi prioritized dynastic survival over national survival.

**Contrast with Japan:** Japan's central government *unified* behind modernization. China's central government was *divided* — with Cixi blocking what Guangxu tried to do. This internal division, more than any other factor, explains why China fell further behind.

---

### Japan — Rapid, Systematic, Successful (with Costs)

**Strengths:** Japan's response to the Perry crisis was decisive. The Meiji Restoration (1868) abolished feudalism, created a constitutional monarchy, built a mass education system, modernized the military (Prussian model), and subsidized key industries. Japan did not just copy Western technology — it sent delegations to study Western institutions and *selected* what fit Japan's needs. The result was the fastest industrialization in history.

**Weaknesses:** Japan's reforms came with costs. Female mill workers were exploited in conditions mirroring the worst of British industrialization. The samurai class was destroyed as a social group. The speed of change created conservative backlash that, by the 1920s, allowed militarists to dominate the government — setting Japan on a path to WWII.

**The zaibatsu model:** Once industries were established, the government sold them to powerful family business conglomerates ([[Unit-5.6/Industrialization — Government's Role#Japan|zaibatsu]]), combining state direction with private capital.

---

## Why Did Japan Succeed Where Others Struggled?

This is a classic AP comparison question. Several factors explain Japan's relative success:

**1. Unified political will:** The Meiji oligarchs replaced the shogunate with a strong central government and *collectively* committed to reform. No single conservative figure could halt the process.

**2. Island geography:** Japan's distance from the European heartland gave it slightly more time to reform before being overwhelmed. The Ottoman Empire bordered Europe directly; China was bordered by colonized territories.

**3. No prior colonial humiliation:** Japan had not suffered an Opium War or a formal sphere of influence. Its wounds from the Perry crisis were real but not yet catastrophic — motivating reform without destroying institutional capacity.

**4. Selective Westernization:** Japan chose what to adopt (Prussian military model, German medicine, American education ideas) and what to preserve (emperor as national symbol, cultural identity). This was different from wholesale imitation or wholesale rejection.

---

## Patterns and Themes for the AP Exam

**Continuity:** In all three cases, reform was resisted by traditional elites — Janissaries, Cixi, samurai. This is a *cross-cultural continuity*: those who benefited from the old order resisted change.

**Change:** All three societies fundamentally changed their legal, military, and educational systems — even if incompletely. The question is always *how much* and *for whom*.

**Causation:** Western imperial pressure was the external cause of reform in all three cases. But internal political dynamics (who held power, how reform was organized, who was included/excluded) determined the outcomes.

**Comparison point:** Japan's reforms touched political structure (constitutional monarchy, abolition of feudalism); the Ottoman and Chinese reforms largely avoided fundamental political restructuring. This is why Japan industrialized successfully while the others lagged.
