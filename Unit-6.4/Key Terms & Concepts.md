# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 6.4 — Global Economic Development | AMSCO pp. 398–406**

## Environment: Natural Resources

**Resources**
The AP category encompassing the raw materials extracted from colonial territories — cotton, rubber, palm oil, ivory, copper, tin, gold, diamonds, guano — to fuel industrialized economies. Colonial extraction restructured colonized regions as export economies dependent on commodity prices set by industrial powers. The acquisition and control of resources was one of the three central economic motives for imperialism (alongside new markets and cheap labor).

**guano**
Bat and seabird excrement rich in nitrates and phosphates, making it an excellent natural fertilizer. Vast deposits accumulated in Peru and Chile due to the dry coastal climate; mining began in the 1840s and peaked around 1840–1880, when millions of tons were exported to Europe and the U.S. Mining was performed by indentured Chinese and Polynesian laborers under dangerous conditions. President Fillmore's 1850 message to Congress calling for government action to secure guano imports illustrates how critical it was to American agriculture — and how early Latin American resources were integrated into the global economy.

**cotton**
A plant fiber that became the lifeblood of British textile manufacturing. By the mid-19th century, Britain imported 80% of its raw cotton from the American South. The U.S. Civil War Union blockade cut off this supply, triggering a global scramble; Egypt expanded production so rapidly that cotton came to represent 93% of its export revenue by 1900. This dependence exemplifies imperial-imposed monoculture: Egypt's entire economy was restructured around a single crop for export to Britain.

**rubber**
Natural rubber from the latex sap of tropical trees and vines became essential for industrial production after Charles Goodyear's vulcanization process (1839), which stabilized it against temperature changes. Used for tires, hoses, gaskets, and waterproof goods, rubber demand exploded with bicycles and automobiles. The Amazon (South America) and Central African vines initially supplied it, but brutal forced labor by "rubber barons" — including mass mutilation of workers who missed quotas in the Belgian Congo — made it one of imperialism's most notorious human rights scandals. Britain transplanted rubber seedlings to Ceylon (Sri Lanka) and Malaya, creating Southeast Asian plantation economies that dominated supply by the early 20th century.

**palm oil**
Oil extracted from West African oil palms, used to lubricate European factory machinery and to produce candles. Africans had cultivated oil palms as a food staple and trade currency for over 5,000 years before European industrial demand converted it into a colonial cash crop. Prisoners of tribal war were sometimes enslaved to work palm oil harvests. European colonists also established plantations in Malaya and the Dutch East Indies, spreading the crop globally.

**ivory**
Material derived from elephant tusks, prized for piano keys, billiard balls, knife handles, and ornamental carvings. Africa was the primary source, since both male and female African elephants carry large tusks averaging six feet. European commercial demand for ivory preceded and foreshadowed the Scramble for Africa — ivory traders' knowledge of African geography and peoples made political colonization easier. The Ivory Coast (Côte d'Ivoire) takes its name from French ivory trading posts established there.

**copper**
Mineral ore essential for telegraph cables and electrical power lines. Chile, Northern Rhodesia (modern Zambia), and the Belgian Congo were major producers. Copper is a direct link between the communications technology revolution (telegraph) and colonial resource extraction — the global information network literally ran through African and South American mines.

**tin**
Mineral ore used primarily for manufacturing tin cans, which allowed food to be preserved and transported globally. Bolivia, Nigeria, Malaya, and the Dutch East Indies were principal producers. Demand for tin cans grew alongside urbanization: city populations could not grow their own food and depended on canned imports. This illustrates how industrial consumer needs in Europe directly drove colonial extraction patterns halfway around the world.

**gold**
Precious mineral mined in Australia, South Africa (Witwatersrand discovery 1886), West Africa, and Alaska. The 1886 Witwatersrand gold discovery made Cecil Rhodes — already dominant in diamonds — the most powerful man in southern Africa. Gold rushes accelerated settler colonialism in Australia and South Africa, driving indigenous dispossession as settlers flooded into new territories.

**diamonds**
Gemstones mined primarily at Kimberley, South Africa. Cecil Rhodes arrived in South Africa in 1870, joined the diamond rush in 1871, and formed the De Beers Mining Company in 1880. By 1891, De Beers controlled 90% of world diamond production. The diamond monopoly is the AP's clearest example of corporate imperialism: private enterprise using imperial political structures to dominate global markets.

## Economics: Companies & Activities

**De Beers Mining Company**
Diamond mining monopoly founded by Cecil Rhodes in South Africa in 1880. By 1891 it controlled 90% of world diamond production. De Beers exemplifies the fusion of corporate power and imperial expansion: a private company used its wealth to influence colonial politics, fund infrastructure projects (the Cape-to-Cairo railroad), and shape British imperial policy in southern Africa. Rhodes's diamond profits also financed his stake in South African gold fields and his political career.

**cash crops**
Agricultural products — tea, cotton, sugar, rubber, coffee, palm oil — grown for commercial export rather than local consumption. Under imperial control, subsistence farmers (who grew food for themselves) were forced or incentivized to switch to cash crops. As food production declined, local food prices rose and populations became dependent on markets they did not control. Cash crop agriculture was the agricultural mechanism through which colonies were integrated into the global extraction economy.

**export economies**
Economic systems in which colonies produced raw materials for export to industrial nations rather than developing diversified domestic manufacturing. The structure was designed by imperial powers to keep colonies as raw-material suppliers and captive consumers of finished European goods. This dependency persisted as a structural legacy long after formal independence — former colonies continued exporting cheap commodities and importing expensive manufactured goods.

**monocultures**
Agricultural systems dominated by a single crop; imposed by imperial cash-crop demands. Monocultures depleted soil fertility faster than diverse farming, made colonies economically vulnerable to a single commodity price collapse, spread crop diseases more easily (no plant diversity to slow spread), and eliminated food self-sufficiency. AMSCO notes that many former colonies were still unable to rediversify their agricultural land into the modern era because the damage was so severe and lasting.

> [!exam-tip]
> Monocultures are the AP's key environmental and economic consequence of colonial cash-crop agriculture. When you see an LEQ prompt about long-term consequences of imperialism, the monoculture / export dependency argument connects 19th-century extraction to 20th-century global inequality.

## Technology: Inventions

**railroads**
Steam-powered rail networks built by imperial powers in colonies to move raw materials from interior regions to coastal export ports. Britain built the most extensive colonial rail system in the world across India. Cecil Rhodes sought a Cape-to-Cairo railroad to link all British African territories. Europeans presented railroads as modernizing infrastructure; in practice, they served resource extraction and military mobility. African and Indian workers built them at wages far below European rates, and profits and goods flowed outward, not inward.

**steamships**
Steam-powered vessels that transformed global trade in the 19th century. Early steamboats operated on navigable rivers (the Ganges in India, the Congo in Africa), carrying mail, people, and goods into colonial interiors. After 1870, more efficient engines made long-distance ocean voyages economically viable. The 1870s introduction of compression refrigeration equipment on ships allowed perishable meat and dairy from Argentina, Uruguay, and New Zealand to reach European markets — integrating Southern Hemisphere ranching into the global food economy for the first time.

**telegraph**
Communication technology that transmitted electrical signals over wires, invented in 1832. The telegraph reduced communication time from weeks to seconds. Submarine cables crossed oceans: the first permanent transatlantic cable (1866) linked the U.S. and Britain; cables soon connected Europe to India, Australia, and South America. The telegraph enabled commodity traders, military commanders, and colonial administrators to coordinate instantaneously across hemispheres, making a truly integrated global economy possible.

> [!tip]
> The AP treats railroads, steamships, and the telegraph as an interconnected technological system rather than three separate inventions. Railroads → interior to coast; steamships → coast to coast (across oceans); telegraph → instantaneous price and order coordination. Together they created the first global economy.

## Society: Hierarchy

**apartheid**
South African system of institutionalized racial segregation and white minority rule, formally enacted in 1948 and dismantled in 1994 with Nelson Mandela's election. AMSCO traces its roots to Cecil Rhodes, whose racist policies as Prime Minister of the Cape Colony (1890s) laid the ideological and legal groundwork for apartheid. The term appears in Topic 6.4 as a connection between 19th-century economic imperialism and 20th-century racial political systems — a long-term consequence of colonialism.
