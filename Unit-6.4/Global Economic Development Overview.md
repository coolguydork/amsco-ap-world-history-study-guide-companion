# Global Economic Development

**Unit 6 — Consequences of Industrialization | AP Period 3 | AMSCO pp. 398–406**

**Essential Question:** How did industrialization and imperialism reshape the global economy?

**Learning Objective:** Explain the economic causes and effects of industrialization and imperialism.

**AP Themes:** ECN (Economics), TEC (Technology), ENV (Environment)

---

## What This Topic Is About

Topic 6.4 examines how industrial demand reshaped the entire global economy between 1750 and 1900. It answers a deceptively simple question: why did European nations want colonies? The core answer is economic — raw materials, new markets, and cheap labor. The topic then traces the technologies that made this extraction possible (railroads, steamships, telegraph) and the damaging consequences for colonized peoples (cash crops, monocultures, export dependency). This is one of the highest-yield topics for AP causation questions.

---

## The Economic Logic of Imperialism

Three forces drove economic imperialism:

**Raw materials** — Industrial factories needed inputs: cotton for textiles, rubber for tires and hoses, copper for electrical wiring, palm oil to lubricate machinery. European states and corporations could not find sufficient quantities at home, so they looked to Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Colonies were reorganized around supplying these materials as cheaply as possible.

**New markets** — Industrialized nations produced more manufactured goods than domestic consumers could absorb. Colonial populations served as captive markets for European exports. This logic explains why Britain banned Indian cotton textiles in 1721: protecting its own manufacturers by eliminating local competition.

**Low-wage labor** — Colonial workers built railroads, mined minerals, and harvested crops at wages far below what European workers would accept. Cecil Rhodes's railroad workers in Africa were paid a fraction of European rates. This wage differential was not incidental — it was the business model.

> [!exam-tip]
> AP questions frequently ask you to identify multiple causes of imperialism. Always distinguish economic motives (raw materials, markets, labor) from political (national prestige, strategic bases) and cultural/ideological ones (Social Darwinism, civilizing mission). Topic 6.4 gives you the economic argument in full.

## Technologies That Made It Possible (pp. 398–399)

Three technologies enabled the extraction economy:

**Railroads** connected interior colonial regions to coastal ports, slashing the cost of moving raw materials. Britain built an extensive rail network across India specifically to funnel cotton and other goods to ships. Cecil Rhodes envisioned a Cape-to-Cairo railroad linking all British African colonies — the project was never finished, but it illustrates how transportation infrastructure served imperial, not local, interests. Europeans framed railroads as modernizing gifts; in practice, they served the colonizers.

**Steamships** transformed ocean trade. Early steamboats worked on navigable rivers — the Ganges in India, the Congo in Africa — carrying mail and cargo into otherwise inaccessible interiors. After 1870, more efficient engines made long-distance ocean voyages practical. Refrigerated steamships (1870s) allowed perishable meat and dairy from Argentina, Uruguay, and New Zealand to reach European tables. The global food supply became integrated for the first time.

**Telegraph** (invented 1832) eliminated the lag in global communications. News and commodity prices that once took months to cross oceans now traveled instantaneously. The first permanent transatlantic cable (1866) linked the U.S. and Britain. By the 1870s, telegraph cables connected Europe to India, Australia, and South America. Combined with railroads and steamships, the telegraph made a truly global market possible.

> [!tip]
> Think of these three technologies as a system: railroads moved goods from interior to coast → steamships moved goods across oceans → telegraphs coordinated prices and orders. Remove any one and the global extraction economy breaks down.

## Agricultural Products (pp. 399–400)

Under imperial pressure, subsistence farmers — who grew food to eat — were forced to switch to **cash crops**: tea, cotton, sugar, oil palms, rubber, coffee. These crops were grown for their commercial export value, not for local consumption. As food crops disappeared, local food prices rose. Colonial populations became dependent on world commodity markets they did not control.

**Guano** (bird and bat excrement) from Peru and Chile was an early and striking example. Rich in nitrates and phosphates, it was the best fertilizer available before synthetic alternatives. Between roughly 1840 and 1880, millions of tons were mined by hand — mostly by indentured Chinese and Polynesian laborers — and shipped to Europe and North America to fertilize the fields feeding industrial urban populations.

**Meat exports** illustrate how refrigeration expanded agricultural export economies. Argentine and Uruguayan cattle ranches, and Australian and New Zealand sheep herders, now exported beef, lamb, and mutton to European cities. The ranching economy of the Southern Hemisphere was built to feed the industrial cities of the Northern.

## Raw Materials (pp. 401–403)

The demand for industrial inputs turned colonies into **export economies** — regions organized not to produce for local needs but to supply raw materials to industrial centers. Key examples:

**Cotton** — By the mid-19th century, Britain's textile mills imported 80% of their cotton from the American South. The U.S. Civil War and Union naval blockade cut off this supply, triggering a global cotton scramble. Egypt rapidly ramped up production and by 1900, 93% of Egyptian export revenue came from cotton — a textbook monoculture imposed by imperial demand.

**Rubber** — Vulcanization (1839, Goodyear) created mass demand for rubber tires, hoses, and gaskets. The Amazon and Central Africa initially supplied it — but "rubber barons" enslaved indigenous laborers, killing or maiming those who missed quotas. Britain then transplanted rubber tree seedlings from Brazil to Ceylon and Malaya, creating Southeast Asian plantation economies that soon dominated world supply.

**Palm oil** — West Africans had used palm oil as food and currency for 5,000 years. European demand converted it to a colonial cash crop for lubricating industrial machinery and making candles. Plantations spread to Malaya and the Dutch East Indies.

**Ivory** — Elephant tusks, used for piano keys and billiard balls, were Africa's first major colonial export. European demand for ivory preceded and foreshadowed the Scramble for Africa; it was commercial extraction that made European powers pay attention to African territories.

**Minerals** — Chile and Rhodesia supplied copper for telegraph cables and electrical wiring; Bolivia, Malaya, and Nigeria supplied tin for food canning; South Africa and Australia supplied gold. Each mineral extraction industry was built on colonial labor and infrastructure, with profits flowing to European investors.

> [!warning]
> Students often describe railroads as a "benefit" Europeans gave to colonies. The AP expects you to evaluate this claim critically. Railroads were designed to serve colonial extraction and military control, not local economic development. Indian and African workers built them at suppressed wages. This is a classic historiographical debate — see Topic 6.3 and the Themes note.

## Global Consequences (p. 403)

The extraction economy left structural damage that outlasted colonialism:

**Monocultures** — Forcing farmers to grow a single cash crop depleted soil fertility, spread crop diseases more easily (no diversity of plants to act as buffer), and eliminated food self-sufficiency. Many former colonies could not rediversify their agricultural land even after independence because the damage was so severe.

**Economic dependency** — Export economies structured around raw material extraction meant colonies never developed manufacturing capacity. They exported cheap raw materials and imported expensive finished goods — a deeply unfavorable exchange that kept colonial populations poor and dependent on commodity price swings they did not control.

**Ecological harm** — Forest clearing for plantations (rubber, palm oil, cotton) reduced biodiversity and altered local climates. Cash crops like cotton rapidly depleted soil nutrients. These environmental effects were global in scope and long-lasting.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP loves asking about long-term consequences. The monoculture / export dependency argument is one of the strongest you can make in an LEQ about how imperialism shaped global inequality into the 20th century. Practice connecting 6.4 to Topics 9.4 (global economics in the modern era) and 6.5 (economic imperialism).

## Cecil Rhodes: The Face of Economic Imperialism

Cecil Rhodes (1853–1902) is the AP's go-to example of the fusion between corporate power and imperial ambition. Arriving in South Africa for health reasons in 1870, he joined the diamond rush, formed **De Beers Mining Company** (1880), and by 1891 controlled 90% of world diamond production. He also held a major stake in South Africa's Witwatersrand gold fields (discovered 1886). By age 29 he was elected to the Cape Parliament; by the 1890s he was Prime Minister of the Cape Colony.

Rhodes's vision — a Cape-to-Cairo railroad connecting all British African territories — embodied the logic of economic imperialism: control of land, resources, transportation, and markets. His racist policies as Prime Minister laid groundwork for the apartheid system that would define South Africa through most of the 20th century.
