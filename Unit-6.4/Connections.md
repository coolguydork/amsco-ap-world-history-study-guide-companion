## Builds On

**[[Unit-5.5/Technology in the Industrial Age Overview\|Technology in the Industrial Age Overview]]**
The railroads, steamships, and telegraph that define Topic 6.4 all originate in the Industrial Revolution covered in Topic 5.5. Without steam power, efficient engines, and electrical technology, the global extraction economy of 6.4 would not have been possible. When you describe how railroads lowered the cost of moving raw materials, you are describing an application of the technological developments introduced in 5.5.

**[[Unit-6.2/State Expansion Overview\|State Expansion Overview]]**
Topic 6.2 covers the political mechanisms of imperialism — how European states expanded territory through colonization and treaty-making. Topic 6.4 explains *why* they wanted to: economic extraction. The political and economic dimensions of imperialism are analytically distinct but historically inseparable. States used political power to impose economic structures; economic extraction financed further political expansion. Cecil Rhodes's career — moving from mining to Parliament to Prime Minister — illustrates this fusion directly.

## Compare With

**Topic 2.1–2.3 (Indian Ocean Trade / [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads\|Silk Roads]])**
Pre-modern long-distance trade also moved commodities (spices, silk, porcelain, gold) across vast distances. What changed in the 19th century was scale (industrial demand was orders of magnitude larger), coercion (colonial governments forced economic restructuring), and technology (railroads, steamships, telegraph made extraction economically viable at unprecedented scale). The *structure* of long-distance trade was continuous; the *intensity and coercive character* were new.

**[[Unit-4.3/Columbian Exchange Overview\|Columbian Exchange]]**
The [[Unit-4.3/Columbian Exchange Overview\|Columbian Exchange]] restructured global agriculture in the 15th–17th centuries by transferring plants, animals, and diseases. Topic 6.4's extraction economy restructured global agriculture again in the 19th century — but this time deliberately, coercively, and in service of industrial capitalism rather than as the unplanned consequence of contact. Note that rubber itself was transplanted (Brazil to Southeast Asia) in a deliberate colonial botanical transfer echoing the Columbian Exchange.

**Topic 6.3 (Indigenous Responses)**
Topic 6.3 covers how colonized peoples resisted imperial political control. Topic 6.4 adds the economic dimension: the same peoples were simultaneously losing control of their land, labor, and agricultural systems to cash-crop imperatives. Resistance was not only military (6.3) but also economic — some African merchants successfully competed in palm oil and ivory trade before formal colonization eliminated their commercial independence.

## Cross-Topic Exam Arguments

This topic provides excellent evidence for several recurring AP argument types:

**On consequences of industrialization:** Use 6.4 to argue that industrialization's most significant global consequences were economic restructuring of non-industrial regions — turning them into raw material exporters and manufactured goods consumers — rather than just factory production in Europe.

**On environmental change:** Use monocultures and deforestation from 6.4 to argue that 19th-century imperialism caused lasting environmental damage — soil depletion, biodiversity loss, disrupted water cycles — that compounded the political damage of colonialism.

**On continuity and change in trade:** Use 6.4 in contrast with pre-modern trade (2.1–2.3) to argue that the 19th century saw a qualitative change in global trade: from luxury goods exchanged through commercial networks to coercively extracted raw materials restructuring entire national economies.
