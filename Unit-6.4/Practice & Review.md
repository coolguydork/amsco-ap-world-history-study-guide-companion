## Multiple-Choice Practice

*Questions 1–3 refer to the following passage (AMSCO p. 404):*

> "Fermented drinks such as alcohol also have the benefits of killing parasites and bringing liquid calories to the diet, but by the start of the 18th century beer production was eating up nearly half of the wheat harvest in Britain. There was no possible way for Britain's domestic agriculture to feed the rapidly expanding population and keep them in beer, too. There just wasn't enough farmland for every new mouth in the industrial era. Calories had to come from an outside source, one beyond the boundaries of the British Isles, from the wider shores of the empire. The pursuit of food has always shaped the development of society, and in the days of the Victorian empire, the very start of our modern industrialized global food-chain, tea with milk and sugar became the answer to Britain's growing need for cheap nutrition."
>
> — Sarah Rose, *For All the Tea in China*, 2009

**Question 1.** The example in the passage highlights which common imperial pattern?

A. The importation of cash crops from the colonies
B. The development of new transportation technologies
C. The export of foodstuffs to colonial holdings
D. The transfer of industrial developments to colonies

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The passage describes Britain importing colonial products (tea, sugar) to feed its industrial population — the classic pattern of colonies supplying agricultural commodities to the metropole. Option C reverses the direction (Britain is importing, not exporting). Option B is about technology, which the passage doesn't address. Option D describes technology transfer, also absent here.

---

**Question 2.** How does Britain's answer to the "growing need for cheap nutrition" relate to the Western Hemisphere?

A. The Americas provided dairy products for Britain.
B. The Americas provided tea for Britain.
C. The Americas provided indentured servants for Britain.
D. The Americas provided sugar for Britain.

> [!tip] Answer: **D**
> Sugar was grown on Caribbean plantation economies (Western Hemisphere) and imported to sweeten British tea. Tea itself came from Asia (India, Ceylon), not the Americas. Dairy came from British domestic agriculture and later from New Zealand/Australia. Indentured labor came from various regions but was not described in this passage.

---

**Question 3.** What impact did the Industrial Revolution have upon nonindustrial countries?

A. They became export countries.
B. The Industrial Revolution spread to these areas.
C. The colonial economy became self-sufficient.
D. Profits earned by these nonindustrial countries were used to create a growing middle class.

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> This is the core argument of Topic 6.4: industrial demand restructured nonindustrial countries as export economies supplying raw materials. Option B is false — colonialism actively *prevented* industrialization in colonized regions by keeping them as raw material suppliers. Option C is false — export economies created dependency, the opposite of self-sufficiency. Option D is false — profits flowed to European investors, not to colonial populations, whose wages were suppressed.

## Short-Answer Practice

*Questions 1–2 (AMSCO p. 405)*

---

**SAQ 1.** *Use the passage below to answer all parts:*

> "Around the middle of the 19th century, Uruguay was dominated by the latifundium [a large landed estate], with its ill-defined boundaries and enormous herds of native cattle, from which only the hides were exported to Great Britain and part of the meat, as jerky, to Brazil and Cuba... Often the landowners were also the caudillos [military or political leaders] of the Blanco or Colorado political parties, the protagonists of civil wars that a weak government was unable to prevent."
>
> — Luis Bértola, *An Overview of the Economic History of Uruguay since the 1870s*, 2008

**Part A:** Identify ONE way in which Uruguay's resource export economy was similar to the economy in Argentina.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> Like Uruguay, Argentina developed a large-scale ranching economy organized around exporting meat and agricultural products to European markets, particularly Britain. Both economies were structured around large landholdings (latifundia in Uruguay, estancias in Argentina) and relied on export revenues from beef and other animal products rather than developing diversified industrial economies. Both were integrated into the global economy as raw material suppliers rather than manufacturers.

**Part B:** Explain ONE way in which the outcomes described by Bértola were caused by developments in Uruguay in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> The development of refrigerated steamships in the 1870s enabled Uruguay to export fresh and frozen beef to European markets rather than only preserved products like jerky and hides. This technological change dramatically increased demand for Uruguayan cattle and the value of the latifundium system, concentrating wealth and political power in the hands of large landowners (caudillos). The expansion of the export economy thus reinforced the political instability Bértola describes, as wealthy landowners used their economic power to dominate Blanco and Colorado party politics and trigger civil conflicts over control of the export economy.

**Part C:** Explain ONE historical situation in the period 1750–1900, other than the one illustrated in the passage, in which resource export economies impacted the political structures of other states.

> [!tip] Model Response — Evidence Beyond the Passage
> Egypt's cotton export economy, which by the 1890s represented 93% of Egypt's export revenue, directly shaped Egypt's political structure. British demand for Egyptian cotton led to massive expansion of cotton cultivation under both the khedivate and later British occupation. When Egypt accumulated debts to European creditors (partly through Suez Canal construction costs), Britain used financial leverage to justify its 1882 military occupation, imposing a colonial political structure that replaced Egyptian sovereignty. Egypt's export dependency in cotton thus made it politically vulnerable to British imperial control.

**SAQ 2.** *Answer all parts:*

**Part A:** Identify ONE way that urbanization affected the growth of export economies in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Causation
> As European cities grew with industrialization, urban populations could not produce their own food and depended on imported agricultural goods. This created mass demand for imported commodities like sugar, tea, beef, and canned goods, driving the expansion of colonial export economies in the Caribbean (sugar), South Asia (tea), South America (beef), and elsewhere. Urban demand was thus a direct driver of export economy growth: the larger European cities grew, the larger the market for colonial agricultural exports.

**Part B:** Explain ONE way in which the profits from raw materials affected global economies in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Continuity and Change
> Profits from raw material extraction by corporations like De Beers Mining Company flowed primarily to European investors and metropolitan economies rather than to colonized regions. These profits funded further imperial investment — Rhodes used diamond profits to finance gold mining and railroad projects — concentrating capital in European financial centers. As a result, industrialized economies grew wealthier and more capable of financing further extraction, while colonized regions remained unable to accumulate capital for their own development, deepening global economic inequality.

**Part C:** Explain ONE way in which commercial extraction of raw materials in Egypt was different from the commercial extraction of raw materials in the Amazon in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Comparison
> In Egypt, commercial extraction focused on agricultural production — cotton grown on existing farmland by Egyptian farmers who were compelled to switch from subsistence agriculture. The transformation was primarily one of land use: fields that had grown food were converted to cash crops. In the Amazon, commercial rubber extraction focused on a wild forest resource (rubber trees growing in dispersed rainforest), using indigenous laborers who were forced or coerced into tapping trees deep in the jungle. The Amazon extraction was characterized by brutal forced labor by "rubber barons" who physically coerced workers, whereas Egyptian cotton production, though also coercive, operated more through taxation and market mechanisms that compelled farmers to grow cotton to earn cash for tax payments.
