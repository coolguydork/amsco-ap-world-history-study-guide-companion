# People, States & Movements

**Topic 6.4 — Global Economic Development | AMSCO pp. 398–406**

## Cecil Rhodes (1853–1902)

The AP's defining figure for the fusion of corporate capitalism and imperial ambition. Rhodes arrived in South Africa in 1870 for health reasons and joined the Kimberley diamond rush in 1871. After completing a degree at Oxford, he systematically bought up mining claims and founded the **De Beers Mining Company** in 1880. By 1891, De Beers controlled 90% of global diamond production. He simultaneously acquired a major stake in the Witwatersrand gold fields — discovered in 1886 in South Africa — making him the wealthiest and most powerful person in the region.

Elected to the Cape Parliament by age 29 and Prime Minister of the Cape Colony by 1890, Rhodes turned corporate wealth into political power. His imperial vision centered on a Cape-to-Cairo railroad that would link all British African colonies in an unbroken north-south corridor — connecting South African mines to Mediterranean ports while binding the entire continent under British economic control. The project was never completed because Britain never controlled enough intervening territory.

Rhodes's legacy is one of the clearest cases the AP offers of how imperialism combined racial ideology with economic extraction. His policies as Prime Minister deliberately disadvantaged Black Africans and laid the political foundations for the apartheid system that would formally govern South Africa from 1948 to 1994.

> [!exam-tip]
> Cecil Rhodes appears as an AP example of **economic imperialism** (private enterprise driving colonial expansion), **corporate monopoly capitalism** (De Beers's 90% market share), and **racial ideology in colonialism** (his policies foreshadow apartheid). Know all three angles.

## Ferdinand de Lesseps (1805–1894)

French engineer and diplomat who designed and built the **Suez Canal** (1869), the 100-mile waterway connecting the Red Sea to the Mediterranean Sea through Egypt. The canal dramatically cut travel time between Europe and Asia — a voyage from London to India that once required circumnavigating Africa now traveled through the canal, saving thousands of miles.

De Lesseps used corvée (forced Egyptian) labor on a massive scale in construction, a practice that drew criticism even in his day. While the canal was a French engineering project, Britain quickly recognized its strategic importance: in 1882, Britain occupied Egypt to protect its access to the canal, making the Suez Canal the linchpin of British imperial control over the route to India.

> [!tip]
> Think of the Suez Canal as the geographic key to the British Empire. It cut the Europe-to-India route by weeks and made Egypt indispensable — which is exactly why Britain seized Egypt in 1882. The canal's strategic importance explains British policy in the Middle East from 1882 through the Suez Crisis of 1956.

## Cyrus McCormick (1809–1884)

American inventor of the mechanical reaper (1831), a horse-drawn harvesting machine that dramatically increased agricultural productivity. The reaper enabled fewer workers to harvest more grain in less time, contributing to the commercialization of American agriculture and the ability of the U.S. to export food surpluses. Though not directly imperial, McCormick represents the broader technological transformation that drove demand for raw materials (including fertilizers like guano) to feed rapidly expanding agricultural output.

## Charles Goodyear (1800–1860)

American inventor who developed vulcanization (1839), a heat treatment process that stabilized natural rubber against temperature changes — making it flexible in cold and firm in heat. Vulcanization created the modern rubber industry and sparked global demand for natural rubber for tires, hoses, gaskets, and waterproof goods. Goodyear's invention is the reason rubber became a major colonial commodity driving extraction in the Amazon and Central Africa, and why Britain transplanted rubber cultivation to Southeast Asia.

## Key States & Regions

**Egypt**
A case study in how colonial cash-crop demands reshaped an entire national economy. Egypt had already developed high-quality long-staple cotton; when the U.S. Civil War eliminated American cotton exports, Egypt dramatically ramped up production. By 1900, cotton represented 93% of Egypt's export revenue — a monoculture so extreme that Egypt's economic fate was determined by British textile demand and global commodity prices. Britain's 1882 occupation of Egypt was motivated not just by Suez Canal strategy but by the need to secure cotton supply and protect British financial investments.

**India**
The largest and most economically integrated of Britain's colonial possessions. Britain banned Indian cotton textiles in 1721 to protect its own wool industry, structurally preventing India from developing its own manufacturing base. Under the Raj, Britain built India's vast railroad network — the largest colonial rail system in the world — to funnel raw materials (cotton, indigo, jute) from the interior to coastal ports. Telegraph lines followed rail routes. India was also the destination of the first rubber seedlings that Britain transplanted from Brazil, via Ceylon and Singapore, to build Southeast Asian rubber plantations.

**South America (Peru, Chile, Argentina, Uruguay)**
South America integrated into the global extraction economy in distinct but related ways. Peru and Chile supplied guano — mined at immense human cost by indentured laborers — that fertilized European and American agricultural fields. Argentina and Uruguay developed vast cattle ranches supplying beef to European markets, made possible by refrigerated steamships. These regions were not formal British colonies but were economically dependent on British markets, investment, and transportation — classic examples of **informal imperialism**.

**West Africa**
Source of palm oil (used to lubricate European machinery and make candles), ivory (elephant tusks for luxury goods), and various minerals. West Africa's ivory trade preceded formal colonization — it was commercial demand for ivory that drove early European penetration. Palm oil had been central to West African culture and trade for millennia before European industrial demand converted it into a plantation crop. The Ivory Coast takes its very name from the French ivory trading posts established there, illustrating how commercial resource extraction preceded and shaped the Scramble for Africa.

**Southeast Asia (Malaya, Dutch East Indies, Ceylon, Indochina)**
After Britain transplanted rubber tree seedlings from Brazil (via Ceylon) in the 1870s, thousands of acres of Southeast Asian forest were cleared for rubber plantations. Malaya and the Dutch East Indies (modern Indonesia) also became major palm oil producers. These territories illustrate how imperial powers actively reorganized colonial ecologies — transplanting crops and clearing forests — to optimize resource extraction.
