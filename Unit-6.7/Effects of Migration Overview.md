# Effects of Migration

**Unit 6 — Consequences of Industrialization | AP Period 3 | AMSCO pp. 429–438**

**Essential Question:** How did mass migration transform both home and receiving societies?

**Learning Objective:** Explain the effects of migration from c. 1750 to c. 1900.

**AP Themes:** MIG (Migration and Settlement), CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions), SIO (Social Interactions and Organization), ECN (Economic Systems)

---

## The Big Picture

Mass migration in the 19th century — whether voluntary or coerced — reshaped societies on both ends of the journey. Laborers tended to be male and to cluster by ethnicity, creating diaspora communities that maintained home cultures while absorbing influences from their new locations. At the same time, the departure of so many workers transformed the societies they left behind, particularly for the women and families who stayed. And everywhere migrants arrived, they encountered nativist hostility that translated into formal legal discrimination. This topic is fundamentally about **three effects**: (1) changes in home societies, (2) the formation of ethnic communities in receiving societies, and (3) the systematization of racial discrimination through law.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam tests this topic heavily on *effects* — not just where people went, but what happened as a result. Know the effects on home societies (gender roles, remittances), on receiving societies (ethnic enclaves, cultural blending), and on law (Chinese Exclusion Act, White Australia Policy). Causation SAQs and LEQs on migration often require both cause and effect.

## Changes in Home Societies (pp. 429–430)

When men emigrated, the families they left behind experienced significant demographic and social shifts. The effects varied by culture. In societies with strong patriarchal norms, females were placed under the supervision of male relatives, and women's roles remained largely unchanged. In other societies, women gained genuine autonomy — managing households, making economic decisions, and taking on responsibilities formerly held by their husbands.

**Remittances** — funds sent home by migrant workers — were a major economic force. When remittances were large enough, women reduced outside work and exercised considerable control over household spending. Historians have noted that remittances correlated with *girls' longer school attendance* in some regions, while boys benefited more in others. This makes remittances an excellent AP evidence point for gender and economic effects of migration on home societies.

> [!tip]
> Think of the effect on home societies through two lenses: **gender roles** (did women gain or lose autonomy?) and **economics** (did remittances raise or lower the family's standard of living?). Both are exam-testable effects.

## Ethnic Enclaves in Receiving Societies (pp. 430–435)

Migrants carried their cultures with them and formed **ethnic enclaves** — clusters of neighborhoods in major cities where they spoke their home language, ate familiar foods, and maintained religious practices. These enclaves were not isolated bubbles; they also influenced receiving-society culture by introducing new foods, music, and traditions.

### Chinese Diaspora

Chinese migration expanded dramatically in the second half of the 19th century, driven by poverty and opportunity:

- **Southeast Asia:** Chinese migrants thrived under colonial rule, managing opium farms in Malaya, engaging in commerce in Indochina, and holding colonial government posts in the Dutch East Indies. By the end of the 19th century they dominated trade across the region.
- **The Americas:** The California gold rush drew Chinese immigrants in large numbers; they also built the first U.S. transcontinental railroad and worked in San Francisco's garment industry. About 225,000 Chinese laborers went to Cuba and Peru on eight-year contracts, working sugar plantations and building the Andean railroad.
- **Australia:** The gold rushes of the 1850s–1860s swelled the Chinese population to around 50,000. After the rushes, Chinese turned to gardening, trade, furniture making, and pearl diving, with major concentrations in Queensland and the Northern Territory.

**[[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]]** was a product of the same era — educated Indians drawing on Western legal traditions to organize politically, paralleling Gandhi's work in South Africa.

### Indian Diaspora

After Britain abolished slavery in 1833, **indentured servants** replaced enslaved laborers across British colonies. Indians were among the first:

- **Africa:** Large numbers went to Mauritius and Natal (now South Africa) for sugar plantations; 32,000 went to Kenya for railroad construction. The Hindu and Muslim Indian communities in South Africa were divided by class and religion, but united in facing discrimination — a context that drew [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]] into activism.
- **Southeast Asia:** Between roughly the 1830s and 1900s, India was the primary labor source for British colonies in Ceylon, Burma, and Malaya. Indentured servitude was eventually replaced by the **kangani system**, under which whole families were recruited. Around 6 million Indians migrated to Southeast Asia this way.
- **Caribbean:** So many Indians went to Caribbean plantations that they became the largest ethnic group in Guyana and Trinidad and Tobago. Their Hindu and Muslim traditions, cuisine, and music became central to Caribbean culture.

### Irish Diaspora

Before the American Revolution, most Irish immigrants to North America were Protestant Scots-Irish who came as indentured servants. After the Revolution, poorer Catholic Irish settled in northern U.S. cities and built the canal system. The **Great Famine** (1845–1852) drove a massive wave — half of the 2 million who fled came to North America, where they faced nativist anti-Catholic hostility.

The Irish shaped U.S. culture profoundly: spreading Catholicism, promoting [[Concept Notes/Labor Unions\|labor unions]], introducing St. Patrick's Day traditions, and producing second-generation figures who became prominent in sports, entertainment, and politics (the Kennedys, the Fitzgeralds).

### Italian Diaspora

Argentina attracted more immigrants than any country except the United States. The 1853 Argentine Constitution guaranteed civil rights to foreigners. Italians made up nearly half of all European immigrants to Argentina in the late 19th–early 20th century; today people of Italian descent make up over 55% of the Argentine population. Argentine Spanish absorbed Italian vocabulary, and Italian is still spoken in Buenos Aires.

## Nativist Backlash and Legal Discrimination (pp. 434–435)

In both the United States and Australia, native-born workers resented immigrant labor — especially Chinese workers willing to accept lower wages. Governments responded by institutionalizing racial discrimination:

**United States:**
- The 1879 California constitution prohibited Chinese workers from public employment, denied citizenship rights, and encouraged their segregation or removal.
- The **Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** was the first major U.S. law targeting a specific ethnic group by name. Made permanent in 1902, repealed only in 1943.
- Many excluded Chinese migrants relocated to Mexico, where President Porfirio Díaz welcomed immigrants and they found roles as farmers and merchants.

**Australia:**
- The Chinese Immigration Act (Victoria, 1855) limited Chinese arrivals per ship.
- After violent attacks on Chinese goldfields communities in 1860–1861, New South Wales passed the Chinese Immigration Regulation and Restriction Act (1861).
- The Influx of Chinese Restriction Act (1881) added an entrance tax.
- After federation in 1901, the new Australian parliament implemented the **White Australia Policy**, which restricted non-British immigration and remained in effect until the mid-1970s.

> [!warning]
> Don't confuse the multiple Australian anti-Chinese laws — the AP usually tests the pattern and culmination (White Australia Policy) rather than individual acts. Know that discrimination escalated from the gold rush era through federation.
