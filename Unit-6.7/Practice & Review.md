# Practice & Review

**Topic 6.7 — Effects of Migration | AMSCO pp. 436–437**

---

## Multiple-Choice Questions

*Questions 1–3 are based on the political cartoon in AMSCO p. 436 depicting nativist sentiment against Chinese immigration.*

**Question 1.** An American who agreed with the cartoon would most *disagree* with:

- A. The installation of the Statue of Liberty in New York Harbor
- B. The elevation of the territory of Hawaii to statehood status
- C. The admission of Puerto Rico as an American territory
- D. The Chinese Exclusion Act

> [!tip] Answer: **A**
> The Statue of Liberty symbolizes the welcoming of immigrants and the ideals of open immigration. A nativist who supported Chinese exclusion would most disagree with that message. Options B and C involve territorial expansion, not immigration policy. Option D (Chinese Exclusion Act) is exactly what the nativist cartoon *supports*, not opposes.

**Question 2.** The point of view of the cartoonist was that he:

- A. Recognized the contributions East Asians made to industrial development in the United States
- B. Was promoting the ideals of the nativist movement
- C. Saw East Asians as political radicals and criminals
- D. Wanted to discourage Irish and Italian immigration to the United States

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The cartoon promotes nativist sentiment — fear and resentment of Chinese immigration. Option A is the opposite of the cartoonist's intent. Option C may partially describe the imagery, but the core point of view is nativism (B), not specifically the criminal framing. Option D targets the wrong immigrant groups; nativist cartoons of this period focused on Chinese, not Irish or Italian, immigrants.

**Question 3.** By the end of the 19th century, the United States had passed laws that:

- A. Restricted most immigration from all countries
- B. Prevented Chinese from entering the United States
- C. Discriminated only against Japanese immigrants
- D. Deported American citizens with Asian ancestry

> [!tip] Answer: **B**
> The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882), made permanent in 1902, specifically targeted Chinese laborers. Option A is too broad — early U.S. immigration law was not a general restriction on all countries. Option C is incorrect; Japanese immigrants were the target of later discrimination (early 20th century), not this period. Option D goes beyond what the Exclusion Act did; it suspended immigration but did not deport citizens.

## Short-Answer Questions

**SAQ 1.** Use the map of Chinese Emigration in the 19th Century (AMSCO p. 437) to answer all parts.

**Part A.** Identify ONE cause of Chinese migration during the 19th century.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A
> One cause of Chinese migration in the 19th century was the demand for cheap labor in industrializing countries and colonial economies. The California gold rush (c. 1848) and construction of the transcontinental railroad created enormous demand for workers, and Chinese migrants were recruited — or sought opportunity — to fill those roles. Poverty and economic instability in China (exacerbated by the Taiping Rebellion and other disruptions) also pushed migrants to seek opportunities abroad.

**Part B.** Identify ONE effect of Chinese migration on a country other than China during the 19th century.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B
> One effect of Chinese migration on Australia was the development of exclusionary immigration legislation. As Chinese miners flooded into the goldfields during the 1850s–1860s gold rushes, their willingness to work for lower wages provoked violent attacks by white miners and nativist hostility. In response, Australian colonial governments passed escalating restrictions — the Chinese Immigration Act (1855), the Chinese Immigration Regulation and Restriction Act (1861), and the Influx of Chinese Restriction Act (1881) — culminating in the White Australia Policy (1901). Chinese migration thus directly caused the institutionalization of racial discrimination in Australian immigration law.

**Part C.** Explain ONE effect of migration during the 19th century on the families of people who remained at home.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C
> One effect of migration on home societies was a shift in gender roles for the families left behind. Since migrant laborers were predominantly male, women in some societies gained significant autonomy when their husbands departed: they managed household finances, made economic decisions, and took on responsibilities formerly held by men. This was especially true where cultural norms did not require women to be placed under male-relative supervision. Additionally, remittances sent by male migrants raised household incomes in sending regions and in some cases correlated with longer school attendance for children, changing the economic trajectory of families left behind.

**SAQ 2.** Answer all parts of the following question.

**Part A.** Identify ONE way in which migrations in the period 1750–1900 impacted social structures.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part A
> Migration in the period 1750–1900 created new ethnic hierarchies in receiving societies. In colonies like Natal (South Africa) and Malaya, Indian indentured laborers occupied a distinct social position below white colonial administrators but in complex relationship with indigenous populations. In the United States, Chinese immigrants faced legal exclusion from citizenship and public employment under California's 1879 constitution, placing them outside the normal social ladder entirely. Migration thus produced layered social structures defined by race and legal status in receiving societies.

**Part B.** Explain ONE way in which migrants incorporated their cultures into new environments in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part B
> Chinese migrants incorporated their cultural traditions into receiving societies through the formation of ethnic enclaves — Chinatowns — in cities across Australia, the Americas, and Southeast Asia. Within these enclaves, Chinese language, food, religious practices, and commercial networks were maintained. At the same time, Chinese cultural influences permeated surrounding societies: some Peruvian cuisine became a fusion of Chinese and indigenous ingredients and techniques (known today as chifa). This bidirectional cultural exchange — migrants maintaining traditions while influencing the receiving culture — illustrates the CDI (Cultural Developments and Interactions) pattern central to the AP exam.

**Part C.** Explain ONE reason why states regulated the increased flow of immigrants in the period 1750–1900.

> [!tip] Model Response — Part C
> States regulated immigration primarily because of economic competition between immigrant and native-born workers. In California and Australia, white miners and laborers resented Chinese workers who were willing to accept lower wages, which threatened their own earning power and job security. This economic grievance was politically powerful — in California, Denis Kearney's Workingmen's Party successfully lobbied for provisions in the 1879 state constitution targeting Chinese workers, and in Australia, anti-Chinese leagues pushed for legislative restrictions. Governments responded to these organized interests by institutionalizing discrimination, demonstrating that nativist regulation of immigration was often driven by labor-market competition translated into political pressure.

## Key Review Questions

1. What were the THREE main groups of effects of 19th-century migration, and give one specific example of each?
2. How did migration affect gender roles in *sending* societies? What factors determined whether women gained or lost autonomy?
3. Trace the escalation of anti-Chinese legislation in Australia from 1855 to 1901. What was each law's trigger?
4. How did Mohandas Gandhi's experience in South Africa connect to the later Indian independence movement?
5. Compare the experiences of Indian indentured servants with those of Italian voluntary migrants to Argentina. What accounts for the differences in their reception and social mobility?

> [!exam-tip]
> Topic 6.7 pairs naturally with Topic 6.6 in LEQ prompts asking you to "explain causes AND effects" of migration. Practice moving from the 6.6 causes (push-pull factors) to the 6.7 effects (enclaves, discrimination laws, gender role shifts) in a single coherent argument. The strongest essays will show that the *type* of migration (voluntary vs. coerced, European vs. Asian) shaped the *nature* of the effects.
