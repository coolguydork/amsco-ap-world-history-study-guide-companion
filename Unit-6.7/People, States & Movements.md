# People, States & Movements

**Topic 6.7 — Effects of Migration | AMSCO pp. 429–438**

---

## Key People

**Mohandas Gandhi (1869–1948)**
Indian lawyer who arrived in Pretoria, South Africa in 1893 to practice law and was immediately confronted by systematic racial discrimination against Indians. Gandhi transformed from a London-educated barrister into an activist after experiencing the injustice firsthand. He founded the **Natal Indian Congress** (1894) to organize resistance to anti-Indian discrimination and developed his philosophy of *satyagraha* (nonviolent resistance/devotion to truth) during his South African years. He returned to India in 1914 and led the mass nationalist movement against British colonial rule that culminated in Indian independence in 1947. Gandhi is the most important individual in Topic 6.7 because his trajectory connects 19th-century migration patterns directly to 20th-century decolonization — the AP may ask you to trace this causation.

> [!tip]
> Gandhi's South Africa story is a perfect example of **migration creating political activists**: discrimination against immigrants can mobilize resistance movements that ultimately challenge imperial power in the home country as well.

**Denis Kearney (1847–1907)**
Irish-American labor leader in California who led the anti-Chinese movement in the 1870s–1880s. Kearney's Workingmen's Party of California mobilized working-class white voters against Chinese labor competition and pushed for the provisions in California's 1879 constitution that targeted Chinese residents. His movement directly contributed to the passage of the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882). Kearney represents the dark side of immigrant assimilation: newly arrived groups (here, Irish) sometimes channeled nativist hostility toward more recently arrived groups (Chinese), using political organization against them.

> [!warning]
> The AP may test Kearney as an example of how nativism worked within immigrant communities — not just against all immigrants, but directed at specific ethnic groups by other immigrant groups seeking to secure their own economic position.

**Porfirio Díaz (1830–1915)**
Mexican president (in power 1876–1911) who promoted immigration and economic development as a path to modernity. After the Chinese Exclusion Act, some Chinese immigrants relocated to northern Mexico, where Díaz welcomed them. Rather than working as manual laborers in mines and railroads (as they had in the U.S.), Chinese immigrants in Mexico worked as truck farmers, shopkeepers, and manufacturers. Díaz shows how one country's discriminatory policies redirected migration flows toward more welcoming states — the interconnection between nativism and migration patterns.

## Key States & Colonies

**Natal (Colony, present-day South Africa)**
A British colony on the southeastern coast of Africa where large numbers of Indian indentured servants worked on sugar plantations and built railways. Natal had a significant Indian population that faced systematic racial discrimination under British rule — the context in which Gandhi became an activist. The Natal Indian Congress, founded there in 1894, was one of the earliest organized Indian civil-rights bodies. Natal's example shows how colonies became sites of complex racial and ethnic hierarchies, with Indians occupying an ambiguous position between the white colonial rulers and the Black African majority.

**Australia (Federation 1901)**
The six separate British self-governing colonies of Australia united as a single federal government in 1901. The new parliament's first major immigration acts implemented the **White Australia Policy**, restricting non-British immigration through discriminatory dictation tests. This policy was the culmination of decades of anti-Chinese legislation — from Victoria's Chinese Immigration Act (1855) through New South Wales's restriction acts of 1861 and 1881. Australia's history shows how gold-rush-era racial tensions became institutionalized as permanent national policy.

**Argentina**
By the early 20th century, Argentina had become one of the wealthiest nations in Latin America — fueled heavily by European immigration, especially Italian. The 1853 Argentine Constitution guaranteed civil rights to foreign nationals. Italians made up nearly half of all European immigrants in the late 19th century; people of Italian descent today comprise over 55% of the Argentine population. Argentine Spanish absorbed Italian vocabulary, and Italian is still spoken in Buenos Aires. Argentina is the AP's main example of European immigration transforming a Latin American nation's demography and culture.

## Key Movements

**Nativist Movement (United States & Australia)**
A political movement of native-born residents who viewed immigrants as economic and cultural threats. In the United States, nativism targeted Chinese workers through California's 1879 constitution and the federal Chinese Exclusion Act (1882). In Australia, nativist hostility to Chinese goldminers led to violent attacks in the 1860s and a cascade of exclusionary legislation culminating in the White Australia Policy. The nativist movement illustrates the predictable receiving-society reaction when immigrant labor competition is perceived as a threat to wages and social cohesion.

**Irish Labor Movement**
Irish immigrants in the U.S. — facing anti-Catholic nativism themselves — channeled their solidarity into the labor movement. Through their large numbers and willingness to organize, Irish workers had a lasting influence on American [[Concept Notes/Labor Unions\|labor unions]]. This is a significant AP connection: the same migrants who faced discrimination became agents of labor reform in the receiving society.

**Indian Diaspora Organizations**
The Natal Indian Congress (1894) founded by Gandhi was an early model of diaspora political organizing: migrants in a colonial context using legal and nonviolent means to challenge racial discrimination. This model prefigures 20th-century anti-colonial organizations like the [[Concept Notes/Indian National Congress\|Indian National Congress]] in India and Pan-African movements in Africa and the Caribbean.

> [!exam-tip]
> Know the **Natal Indian Congress** specifically for the AP — it shows that migration led not only to cultural blending but also to **political organization**. Gandhi's activism in South Africa is a direct causation chain from 6.7 (migration effects) to later decolonization topics (Unit 8).
