# Key Terms & Concepts

**Topic 6.7 — Effects of Migration | AMSCO pp. 429–438**

*23 terms — all resolved*

---

**ethnic enclaves**
Clusters or neighborhoods in cities where immigrants from the same country or region lived together, maintaining their home language, foods, and religious practices while also creating community institutions. Chinatowns, Irish neighborhoods, and Italian quarters were examples. Enclaves both preserved migrant cultures and influenced receiving-society culture — Argentine Spanish absorbed Italian vocabulary; Caribbean music blended Hindu and African traditions. Key AP evidence for the CDI theme.

**remittances**
Funds sent by migrant workers back to their home countries. Remittances were a primary economic effect of migration on home societies: they raised household incomes, influenced gender dynamics (giving women more decision-making power over spending), and in some regions correlated with longer school attendance for children. An excellent AP evidence point for economic and social effects on sending societies.

**indentured servants**
Workers bound by contract to labor for a fixed period (typically 5–7 years) in exchange for passage, food, and lodging, then legally free. The British Empire used Indian and Chinese indentured workers extensively after abolishing slavery (1833) to replace enslaved labor on Caribbean, African, and Southeast Asian plantations. The system was criticized as a "new form of slavery" because conditions were often exploitative.

**kangani system**
Labor recruitment system that replaced indentured servitude in British Southeast Asia (Ceylon, Burma, Malaya). A kangani (headman/foreman) recruited entire families to work on tea, coffee, and rubber plantations. Workers had fewer restrictions than indentured laborers and kept family units together. An estimated 6 million Indians migrated under this system before its abolition.

**Chinese Exclusion Act**
1882 U.S. federal law that suspended immigration of Chinese laborers — the first major American legislation targeting a specific ethnic group by name. Initially limited to ten years, it was repeatedly extended, made permanent in 1902, and not repealed until 1943. It exemplifies nativist backlash against immigrant labor competition and the racialization of immigration law in receiving societies.

**Chinese Immigration Act**
1855 law passed by the Victorian parliament in response to Chinese population growth during the gold rushes; limited the number of Chinese passengers per ship. Many Chinese circumvented it by landing in South Australia. The first in a series of escalating Australian anti-Chinese laws.

**Chinese Immigration Regulation and Restriction Act**
1861 law passed by New South Wales after violent white miner attacks on Chinese goldfield settlements; attempted to restrict Chinese immigration into the colony. Repealed in 1867 but established the precedent for government-sanctioned racial exclusion that culminated in the White Australia Policy.

**Influx of Chinese Restriction Act**
1881 New South Wales law that restricted Chinese immigration through an entrance tax once the gold rushes ended. Part of an escalating pattern of Australian anti-Chinese legislation; the White Australia Policy (1901) was its culmination.

**White Australia Policy**
Unofficial but government-enforced policy adopted after Australian federation in 1901 to restrict non-British immigration, justified as preserving "white Australia." Implemented through dictation tests in any European language. Remained in effect until the mid-1970s. Represents the culmination of decades of anti-Chinese legislation and exemplifies the racial dimensions of receiving-society nativism.

**gold rush**
Rapid migration of prospectors to newly discovered goldfields. The California gold rush (c. 1848–1855) and Australian gold rushes (1850s–1860s) were major pull factors for Chinese migrants. Both triggered violent nativist backlash and the first formal government restrictions on Chinese immigration, making gold rushes a key causal link between migration and discrimination.

**Natal Indian Congress**
Political organization founded by [[Concept Notes/Mohandas Gandhi\|Mohandas Gandhi]] in 1894 in the British colony of Natal, South Africa, to resist discrimination against Indians. One of the earliest formal Indian civil-rights organizations; its founding marks Gandhi's transformation from lawyer to activist. Gandhi developed his philosophy of nonviolent resistance (satyagraha) in South Africa before returning to India in 1914.

**Scots-Irish**
Protestants of Scottish descent who had previously migrated to Ireland before emigrating to North America before the American Revolution; most came as indentured servants. Distinct from the later waves of Catholic Irish immigrants who came after the Great Famine. The distinction matters on the AP because the pre-Revolution and post-Revolution Irish immigration waves had very different social profiles.

**Chinatowns**
Ethnic enclaves established by Chinese immigrants in cities around the world — Australia, the Americas, and Southeast Asia. They provided familiar language, food, and community networks, and served as centers of cultural preservation. They also became visible symbols that fueled nativist resentment in receiving societies because of their perceived foreignness.

**popular culture**
Mass entertainment and cultural forms appealing to broad audiences. Second-generation Irish immigrants became prominent in late-19th-century American popular culture as boxers, baseball players, and vaudeville performers. This illustrates a recurring AP pattern: second-generation immigrants seek assimilation through mainstream cultural participation even as they maintain ethnic community ties.

**Mohandas Gandhi**
Indian lawyer and activist who arrived in South Africa in 1893 intending to practice law; experienced racial discrimination and became an activist. Founded the Natal Indian Congress (1894) and developed his philosophy of nonviolent resistance (satyagraha) while fighting for Indian rights in South Africa. Returned to India in 1914 and became the central figure in the Indian independence movement against British rule. His story connects Topic 6.7 directly to 20th-century decolonization.

**Porfirio Díaz**
Mexican president (1876–1911) who promoted immigration and foreign investment as tools of economic development, especially in northern Mexico. After the Chinese Exclusion Act drove some Chinese immigrants out of the United States, Díaz welcomed them; many settled in northern Mexico as truck farmers, shopkeepers, and manufacturers rather than manual laborers. Connects the effects of U.S. nativism to Mexican immigration policy.

**Trinidad and Tobago**
British Caribbean colony that received large numbers of Indian indentured laborers on sugar plantations after abolition (1833). Indians became the largest ethnic group and brought Hinduism, distinctive music, and cuisine that shaped the colony's culture permanently. Today Indians remain a major demographic and cultural presence. Representative of the Caribbean as a whole: Indian migration transformed the region's demographics.

**Mauritius**
Island colony off the southeast coast of Africa and one of the first large-scale destinations for Indian indentured labor after abolition. The British established the indenture pattern here before extending it to Natal, the Caribbean, and Southeast Asia. Key AP evidence for the global scale and British Empire-wide nature of indentured servitude.

**Natal**
British colony on the southeast coast of Africa (today part of South Africa) where Indian indentured workers labored on sugar plantations and built railways. The site of significant anti-Indian discrimination that drew Mohandas Gandhi into activism. Indians remain a significant demographic group in the region today, exemplifying the permanent demographic effect of 19th-century migration.

**Guyana**
British colony in South America (formerly British Guiana) that received so many Indian indentured laborers that Indians became the largest ethnic group in the country — a demographic reality that persists today. Represents the Caribbean/South American dimension of the Indian diaspora, alongside Trinidad and Tobago.

**blue-collar**
Adjective describing manual or industrial wage labor (factory work, construction, mining). Second-generation Irish immigrants in the U.S. often worked as skilled blue-collar workers, representing upward mobility from the unskilled first-generation immigrants. Contrasted with white-collar (professional/managerial) work; the distinction reflects social stratification in industrializing receiving societies.

**Transportation**
19th-century transportation infrastructure — railroads, canals, and shipping — was built largely by migrant labor. Chinese laborers were indispensable for the U.S. transcontinental railroad; Irish laborers built the canal system. Migrant workers were thus foundational to the industrialization of receiving societies even as those same societies discriminated against them.

**canal system**
Network of artificial waterways built to move goods inland before railroads. Irish immigrants to the United States constructed much of the antebellum canal network — a major source of employment for first-generation Irish and an example of how migrants built the infrastructure of receiving societies despite facing significant discrimination.
