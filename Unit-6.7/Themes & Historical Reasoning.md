# Themes & Historical Reasoning

**Topic 6.7 — Effects of Migration | AMSCO pp. 429–438**

**AP Skills:** Causation, Continuity and Change Over Time

---

## AP Theme Analysis

### MIG — Migration and Settlement

Topic 6.7 is the central MIG topic for Unit 6. Migration in the 19th century was unprecedented in scale and global reach. The key AP argument for this theme is that migration was not a one-way story: it transformed both sending and receiving societies simultaneously and created new hybrid cultures in between. The formation of ethnic enclaves, the persistence of home-country religions and foods, the intermarriage and cultural exchange that produced Peruvian-Chinese cuisine or Caribbean Hindu music — all of these are evidence that migration reshaped cultural identities globally.

Key evidence by migrant group:

| Group | Major Destinations | Cultural Effect on Receiving Society |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Chinese | SE Asia, U.S., Australia, Cuba, Peru | Chinatowns, fusion cuisine, trade dominance in SE Asia |
| Indian | Mauritius, Natal, SE Asia, Caribbean | Hinduism, music, cuisine in Caribbean; political organizations in Africa |
| Irish | U.S., Canada | Spread of Catholicism, labor unions, St. Patrick's Day, political dynasties |
| Italian | Argentina | 55%+ of Argentine population; Italian vocabulary in Spanish; Italian still spoken |

### SIO — Social Interactions and Organization

The social effects of migration operated in three directions. First, **receiving societies** developed new ethnic hierarchies and discriminatory legal systems targeting immigrants. Second, **home societies** experienced shifts in gender roles and economic conditions as men departed. Third, **migrant communities** created new social organizations — ethnic enclaves, labor unions, political advocacy groups — to navigate their position in receiving societies.

Gender effects are especially important for the SIO theme: men's departure could increase women's autonomy (managing household finances, making decisions) or leave them more dependent on male relatives, depending on cultural norms. The AP expects students to recognize this *variation* — there is no single effect of migration on gender.

### CDI — Cultural Developments and Interactions

Ethnic enclaves were the primary mechanism of cultural interaction. Migrants brought languages, religions, cuisines, and music that influenced receiving-society culture while simultaneously absorbing elements of their new environment. The result was often syncretism: Peruvian-Chinese cuisine, Caribbean Hindu festivals, Argentine-Italian language. This cultural exchange was not equal — receiving-society culture often dominated — but neither was it one-directional.

### ECN — Economic Systems

Migrant workers were indispensable to 19th-century industrial development: Chinese laborers built the U.S. transcontinental railroad; Irish workers built the canal system; Indians built East African railways. The economic contribution of migrants to receiving societies was enormous even as those societies discriminated against them. Meanwhile, remittances back to home societies created important economic flows that affected living standards and even school attendance in sending regions.

## AP Skill: Causation

AMSCO structures Topic 6.7 around causation, and the Think as a Historian exercise asks students to connect causes of emigration to effects of migration — on home countries, receiving countries, and emigrants themselves.

**Causation structure for the AP:**

*Cause (6.6) → Effect (6.7):*
- Push factor (poverty, famine, coercion) → Chinese/Indian/Irish migration → ethnic enclaves + nativist backlash
- Pull factor (gold rush, railroad construction, plantation labor demand) → migration → discrimination laws (Chinese Exclusion Act, White Australia Policy)
- Indentured labor system → Indian diaspora in Africa/Caribbean/SE Asia → permanent demographic transformation

*Relative significance of causes:*
An excellent LEQ argument could compare the relative significance of coerced vs. voluntary migration in shaping the effects. Voluntary migrants (Italians to Argentina) tended to have more economic mobility and face less severe discrimination. Coerced or semi-coerced migrants (Indian indentured servants, Chinese contract laborers) faced more restrictive conditions and more intense legal discrimination — suggesting the *type* of migration influenced the *severity* of discrimination in receiving societies.

> [!exam-tip]
> A common AP Causation prompt: "Explain the causes AND effects of migration in the period 1750–1900." You need to address *both* sides. Use 6.6 for causes; use 6.7 for effects. Don't let the two blend together — clearly label each in your essay.

## AP Skill: Continuity and Change Over Time

### What Changed

- The legal status of migration shifted: unregulated in the early 19th century → heavily restricted by 1900 (Chinese Exclusion Act, White Australia Policy). This represents a significant CCOT in how states managed population flows.
- Women's roles in sending societies: in some cultures, male emigration increased female autonomy; in others, it reinforced patriarchal control through male-relative supervision. Both patterns represent change from pre-migration norms.
- Migrant communities evolved across generations: first-generation immigrants formed ethnic enclaves and faced severe discrimination; second-generation immigrants often achieved skilled blue-collar or white-collar status and entered mainstream culture (Irish boxers, politicians, entertainers).

### What Continued

- The fundamental pattern of labor exploitation persisted: enslaved labor → indentured servitude → contract labor → kangani system. The forms changed but the underlying coercion of poor, nonwhite workers for colonial enterprises continued throughout the period.
- Ethnic and racial prejudice in receiving societies was a continuity: nativism against Chinese in the 1850s and nativism against Irish Catholics in the 1840s are parallel phenomena, both rooted in economic competition and cultural anxiety.

## Historiographical Perspectives

AMSCO does not include a dedicated Historical Perspectives section for Topic 6.7, but the Think as a Historian section raises the core question: **how do we connect the historical development of emigration to the historical development of systematized discrimination?**

The central historiographical debate is between two framings:

1. **Economic interpretation:** Discrimination against Chinese and Indian migrants was primarily driven by labor competition — native workers feared wage depression and job displacement. The legal exclusion laws followed economic grievances articulated by labor movements (Denis Kearney in California; anti-Chinese leagues in Australia).

2. **Racial/ideological interpretation:** Discrimination was primarily driven by racial ideology — ideas about racial hierarchy and the preservation of "white" civilization that preceded and outlasted specific economic conditions. The White Australia Policy persisted until the 1970s long after the gold rush competition had ended, suggesting that race, not economics, was the durable driver.

Most historians today accept elements of both: economic grievances provided the spark, but racial ideology provided the enduring legal framework. For the AP, acknowledge both dimensions when explaining *why* states discriminated against migrants.

> [!tip]
> When answering an SAQ or LEQ about discrimination against migrants, you can earn complexity points by acknowledging both the economic and ideological dimensions — and by noting that discrimination was not uniform (Chinese and Irish both faced discrimination, but the legal forms differed dramatically).
