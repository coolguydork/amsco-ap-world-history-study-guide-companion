# Unit 5 Overview — Revolutions
**c. 1750–c. 1900 | AP Period 3**

---

> [!summary] Unit 5 at a Glance
> Between 1750 and 1900, two revolutions remade the world. The first was **intellectual and political**: Enlightenment ideas about natural rights and reason generated a cascade of revolutions — American, French, Haitian, Latin American — that destroyed old monarchies and built new nations. The second was **economic and technological**: the Industrial Revolution transformed production from artisan craft to factory, from farm to city, from local to global. By 1900, the world had a new class structure, new ideologies, new empires, and a new set of political demands. Unit 5 is the story of how those two revolutions collided, combined, and remade everything.

---

## The Big Causal Chain

Both revolutions of Unit 5 flow from the same intellectual source — but they produce very different kinds of change.

```
Enlightenment: reason, natural rights, social contract (5.1)
        │
        ├──→ Political Revolutions (5.2)
        │         American (1776) → French (1789) → Haitian (1791–1804)
        │         → Latin American independence (1810s–1820s)
        │         → European nationalism and unification (1840s–1871)
        │
        └──→ Economic Liberalism: free markets, private property (5.3)
                  │
                  ↓
          Industrial Revolution begins in Britain (5.3)
          Coal + rivers + capital + textile demand + steam engine
                  │
                  ↓
          Industrialization spreads globally (5.4 + 5.5)
          US, Germany, Belgium → railroads, steel, electricity
                  │
                  ├──→ State-directed industrialization (5.6)
                  │    Japan (Meiji): success | China (Self-Strengthening): failure
                  │    Ottoman (Tanzimat): partial | Egypt (Muhammad Ali): undercut
                  │
                  ├──→ Economic innovations (5.7)
                  │    Corporations, global banks, consumer culture, commodity trade
                  │
                  └──→ Reactions and consequences (5.8 + 5.9 + 5.10)
                       Marx + labor unions + working class + feminism
                       + environmental damage + global inequality
                       ↓
                   [Points toward New Imperialism in Unit 6]
```

Understanding this chain is the key to Unit 5. The Enlightenment is not just Topic 5.1 — it is the intellectual engine of everything that follows. And the Industrial Revolution is not just economic history — it generated new ideologies, new social formations, and new political demands that are still shaping the world.

## Topic-by-Topic Synthesis

### [[Unit-5.1/Enlightenment Overview|5.1 — The Enlightenment]]

The Enlightenment was the intellectual revolution that made every other revolution in Unit 5 possible. Building on the Scientific Revolution and Renaissance humanism, 17th- and 18th-century thinkers applied **reason** to human society and concluded that governments, economies, and social arrangements were not divinely fixed — they could be rationally reformed or replaced.

Key thinkers and their contributions:

| Thinker | Key Idea | AP Significance |
|---|---|---|
| John Locke | Social contract; natural rights to life, liberty, property | Foundation of American Revolution; limits on government |
| Jean-Jacques Rousseau | General will; popular sovereignty | Foundation of French Revolution; radical democracy |
| Voltaire | Religious toleration; skepticism of monarchy | Defines Enlightenment critique of church and state |
| Montesquieu | Separation of powers; checks and balances | Directly shaped U.S. Constitution |
| Mary Wollstonecraft | Natural rights apply to women (*Vindication*, 1792) | Foundation of feminist argument |
| Adam Smith | Free markets and capitalism (*Wealth of Nations*, 1776) | Intellectual shift from mercantilism to capitalism |

The Enlightenment also generated the three great "-isms" that would compete throughout Unit 5 and into Unit 6: **liberalism** (individual rights, constitutional government), **conservatism** (tradition, hierarchy, gradual change), and **nationalism** (loyalty to shared language and culture as the basis for political legitimacy).

> [!exam-tip]
> The Enlightenment is evidence, not just background. When an AP essay asks about the causes of the French Revolution or the intellectual roots of Marxism, cite specific Enlightenment thinkers by name — Locke, Rousseau, or Smith — and explain *which specific idea* they contributed.

### [[Unit-5.2/Nationalism and Revolutions Overview|5.2 — Nationalism and Revolutions]]

Topic 5.2 applies Enlightenment ideas to political action. The result was a cascade of revolutions that, together, established the principle that governments derive legitimacy from the consent of the governed — not from divine right.

**American Revolution (1776)**: The first successful application of Enlightenment natural rights arguments to throw off colonial rule. The Declaration of Independence's "all men are created equal, endowed with unalienable Rights" is Locke in political form. Limits: slavery continued; only propertied white men voted initially.

**French Revolution (1789–1799)**: The most radical of the Atlantic Revolutions. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (1789) proclaimed universal rights. The Reign of Terror (1793–94) showed how quickly revolutionary idealism could collapse into violence. Napoleon (1799–1815) preserved some Enlightenment reforms (legal equality, meritocracy) while ending the republic.

**Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)**: The only successful large-scale enslaved people's revolt in history. Toussaint Louverture led 500,000 enslaved people to defeat French, British, and Spanish armies — forcing France to abolish slavery in its empire (temporarily) and establishing the world's first Black republic. Haiti terrified slaveholding nations everywhere, demonstrating that Enlightenment logic applied to enslaved people too.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Haitian Revolution is the AP exam's favorite Unit 5 comparison case. Know that: (1) it was inspired by French Enlightenment ideals but applied them more radically than France itself; (2) it succeeded militarily against three major powers; (3) its aftermath — Haitian leaders compelled to pay reparations to France — shows that the international order punished Black independence.

**Latin American Independence Movements (1810s–1820s)**: Creoles (European-descended people born in the Americas) led most independence movements — not indigenous or enslaved people. Simón Bolívar liberated northern South America; José de San Martín liberated southern South America. The cause was partly Enlightenment ideology but heavily driven by creole resentment of peninsulare (Spain-born) political privilege.

**European Nationalism (1820s–1871)**: Nationalism — the idea that people sharing a language and culture should have their own state — drove the unification of Germany (1871, under Bismarck) and Italy (1861, Risorgimento). The Revolutions of 1848 erupted across European cities, demanding constitutions and national self-determination. Most failed in the short term but accelerated reform over the following decades.

### [[Unit-5.3/Industrial Revolution Begins Overview|5.3 — The Industrial Revolution Begins]]

Britain industrialized first, and the reasons are specific: access to **coal** (abundant and near rivers), **rivers** for power and transport, **capital** accumulated through colonial trade, a **textile industry** hungry for mechanization, and a **legal environment** that protected property and rewarded innovation. No single factor is sufficient — all combined.

Key inventions, in causal order:

| Invention | Inventor / Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Spinning jenny | James Hargreaves, 1760s | Multiplied textile output per worker |
| Water frame | Richard Arkwright, 1769 | Powered spinning by water → first factories |
| Steam engine (improved) | James Watt, 1765 | Coal power → factories anywhere, not just rivers |
| Interchangeable parts | Eli Whitney, 1798 | Standardized components → assembly-line production |
| Bessemer process | Henry Bessemer, 1856 | Mass production of steel → bridges, rails, machines |

The **factory system** replaced the cottage industry (putting-out system) and the artisan workshop. Families who had worked together at home now separated — adults and children went to factories on factory schedules. This disruption of the family labor unit is the origin point for every social change in Topics 5.9 and 5.10.

> [!tip]
> Britain did NOT invent all these technologies. The spinning jenny is British; interchangeable parts emerged in the US; the Bessemer process was adapted by multiple nations. The key is Britain's *combination* of technologies, capital, and geography — not exclusive invention.

### [[Unit-5.4/Industrialization Spreads Overview|5.4 — Industrialization Spreads]]

Industrialization spread from Britain outward — but unevenly. The United States, Belgium, Germany, and France industrialized successfully in the mid-to-late 19th century. Russia industrialized under state direction (Trans-Siberian Railroad, 1891–1916). Japan industrialized most dramatically (see Topic 5.6).

The key pattern: **regions that could not protect their markets were locked into raw material roles.** India had begun to industrialize cotton textiles, but British tariffs and East India Company policies deliberately undercut Indian manufacturing. Egypt attempted to build a textile industry under Muhammad Ali but was blocked by British competition. By 1900, China's and India's share of world manufacturing had collapsed from ~55% combined in 1750 to under 10% — not because they lacked capacity, but because Western industrial dominance was enforced through trade policy and eventually colonial control.

> [!warning]
> Students often assume industrialization "spread naturally." It did not — it was actively blocked in colonized or semi-colonized regions by Western tariffs, forced trade treaties, and colonial extraction. The failure of Egypt, India, and China to industrialize was caused by Western policy, not lack of capability.

### [[Unit-5.5/Technology in the Industrial Age Overview|5.5 — Technology in the Industrial Age]]

The **Second Industrial Revolution** (roughly 1860s–1900s) introduced a new generation of technologies that transformed production, communication, and daily life:

- **Steel** (Bessemer process, 1856): enabled skyscrapers, railroads, bridges, and heavy machinery
- **Electricity** (first commercial power station, London 1882): factory lighting, electric motors, eventually household appliances
- **Petroleum** (commercial wells drilled mid-1800s): kerosene lamps → internal combustion engine
- **Telephone** (Alexander Graham Bell, 1876): transformed business and personal communication
- **Railroads** (transcontinental US completed 1869; Trans-Siberian 1891–1916): integrated national markets, opened interior resource regions

The railroad is the technology with the widest consequences: it made interior regions accessible for resource extraction, created integrated national markets, enabled mass movement of troops, and pulled people from rural villages into industrial cities. Every industrializing nation built railroads as a first priority.

**Consumer culture** emerged alongside Second Industrial Revolution production: cheap goods, advertising, and department stores created new patterns of spending and identity. The "cult of domesticity" was partly a product of advertising targeting middle-class households.

### [[Unit-5.6/Government's Role in Industrialization Overview|5.6 — Government's Role in Industrialization]]

Topic 5.6 asks a comparative question: when non-Western states faced the threat of Western industrial dominance, how did their governments respond — and why did some succeed while others failed?

| State | Response | Outcome | Key Reason for Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Japan** | [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration\|Meiji Restoration]] (1868): abolish feudalism, adopt constitution, build railways, create national education, send students abroad | **Success** — only non-Western industrialized nation by 1900 | Political consensus; willingness to restructure everything, including dismantling the ruling class's own power |
| **China** | Self-Strengthening Movement (1861–1895): build arsenals, shipyards, railways, while preserving Confucian political structure | **Failure** — defeated by Japan 1895, then foreign powers carve up spheres of influence | Internal court divisions; conservatives blocked restructuring; "Chinese learning as foundation" ideology prevented total reform |
| **Ottoman Empire** | Tanzimat reforms (1839–1876): Westernize law, military, administration within imperial framework | **Partial** — modernized surface but not underlying political system; continued decline | Sultan's authority undermined reform; no political consensus; diverse empire harder to transform |
| **Egypt** | Muhammad Ali's state-sponsored modernization: cotton agriculture, textile factories, new military, educational missions to Europe | **Undercut** — British competition and Ottoman/British pressure dismantled industrialization; Egypt became cotton exporter to British mills | Lacked political independence to protect domestic industry from British competition |

> [!exam-tip]
> Japan vs. China is the AP exam's most-tested comparison for this era. The difference was **political will** — Japan's Meiji oligarchs achieved consensus that the feudal order itself had to go; China's Qing conservatives blocked reform to preserve their own power. Resources were not the difference.

### [[Unit-5.7/Economic Developments and Innovations Overview|5.7 — Economic Developments and Innovations]]

By the late 19th century, the scale of industrial production had outgrown individual entrepreneurs. New **economic institutions** emerged to manage massive capital and coordinate global production:

- **Corporations** with limited liability: investors could share profits without risking personal bankruptcy, enabling massive capital pooling
- **Monopolies and cartels**: giant firms like Standard Oil (Rockefeller) and Carnegie Steel dominated entire industries — driving competitors out through price competition, then setting monopoly prices
- **Global banks and financial markets**: HSBC (Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, 1865), Deutsche Bank, and others financed colonial infrastructure and global commodity trade
- **Consumer culture**: mass production + advertising → new purchasing patterns; department stores and catalogs reached middle-class households
- **Global commodity trade**: cotton, rubber, tin, palm oil, metals — extracted from colonized regions, processed in Western factories, sold globally

The **Second Industrial Revolution** also generated a new geographic pattern of production: Western firms owned the factories; colonized regions supplied the raw materials; finished goods were sold globally. This economic architecture is the direct foundation of the imperialism described in Unit 6.

### [[Unit-5.8/Reactions to the Industrial Economy Overview|5.8 — Reactions to the Industrial Economy]]

Industrialization produced immense wealth — and immense misery. The reactions to that misery were ideological, political, and organizational.

**[[Concept Notes/Karl Marx|Karl Marx]]** (with Friedrich Engels): *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) diagnosed capitalism as structurally exploitative. The bourgeoisie (capital owners) extracted surplus value from the proletariat (wage workers) — not through personal malice but through the market system's competitive logic. Workers must unite, seize the means of production, and build a classless society. Marx's ideas became the ideological foundation of the Russian Revolution (1917) and the 20th century's great communist movements.

**Labor Unions**: Workers organized collectively — strikes, collective bargaining — to negotiate wages, hours, and conditions. Labor unions transformed individual worker powerlessness into collective political leverage. By the late 19th century, labor parties entered parliaments across Western Europe, translating union demands into legislation.

**Utilitarian Reformers** (John Stuart Mill): The greatest good for the greatest number. Government should intervene in the economy to protect children and workers — Factory Acts (1833), Mines Act (1842), Public Health Acts. This tradition produced gradual reform within capitalism, not revolution against it.

**[[Concept Notes/Otto von Bismarck|Bismarck's Welfare State]]**: Germany's conservative Chancellor introduced workers' accident insurance (1883), unemployment insurance (1884), and old-age pensions (1889) — not out of sympathy for workers, but to preempt socialist revolution. This was reform as continuity-preservation.

> [!tip]
> The key intellectual tension in Topic 5.8 is **reform vs. revolution**: utilitarian liberals and Bismarck argued that capitalism could be improved through regulation; Marx argued it had to be replaced. Most Western industrial nations chose the reform path — and avoided the revolution Marx predicted.

### [[Unit-5.9/Society and the Industrial Age Overview|5.9 — Society and the Industrial Age]]

Industrialization did not affect everyone the same way. Its social consequences varied sharply by class and gender.

**The Working Class** lived in overcrowded urban slums — tenements owned by the same factory owners who employed them. Children as young as five worked in textile mills and coal mines. Working days of 14 hours, six days a week, were common. Disease (cholera, typhoid) spread through polluted water. This is the empirical reality that Engels documented and Marx theorized.

**The Middle Class** expanded dramatically — factory managers, accountants, lawyers, doctors, insurance agents. They had access to consumer goods, education, and cultural life (theaters, concert halls, sports). Advertising targeted middle-class households with an ideology of domestic comfort.

**Women** experienced industrialization through their class position: working-class women labored in factories and domestic service while also managing households; middle-class women were constrained by the **cult of domesticity** — an advertising-driven ideology that defined the housewife as a status symbol. The cult's idealization of feminine domesticity generated its own reaction: the organized feminist movement, crystallized at the Seneca Falls Convention (1848).

**The Environment**: Coal-burning factories created toxic smog. Industrial waste contaminated rivers. London's Thames spread cholera throughout the city until the 1860s sewage system was built. This is the first large-scale, systematic environmental crisis in human history.

### [[Unit-5.10/Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age Overview|5.10 — Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age]]

Topic 5.10 is the CCOT synthesis for the entire unit. It asks: across all the economic, social, and political dimensions of industrialization, what actually changed — and what persisted?

| Dimension | What Changed | What Continued |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Mercantilism → capitalism; artisan craft → mass production; new class hierarchy | Poverty and inequality; global commodity trade; raw material regions stayed exporters |
| **Social** | New working class; expanded middle class; industrialists displaced aristocracy; labor unions | Women's subordination; limited social mobility; rural patterns outside industrial zones |
| **Political** | Gradual male suffrage expansion; welfare state; labor parties | Women excluded from politics; authoritarian governments in non-Western states; colonial domination |
| **Global** | Steam technology deepened global trade; Western manufacturing dominance | Non-Western regions as raw material suppliers; colonial relationships intensify |

The global CCOT is the most important for AP essays: industrialization produced **convergence** among industrializing nations (they all got factory systems, urban working classes, reform politics) but produced **divergence** between industrializing and non-industrializing regions — the latter getting poorer relative to the former throughout the 19th century.

## AP Themes Across Unit 5

**Economic Systems**: The defining economic change is **mercantilism → capitalism** — from state-regulated trade accumulating national gold to private market competition generating profit. But capitalism produced its own contradictions: mass poverty alongside mass wealth, global inequality deepening alongside global trade integration. Adam Smith and Karl Marx represent capitalism's two great interpreters.

**Social Structures**: Unit 5 produced a fundamentally new class structure in industrializing societies: the working class (factory wage laborers), the expanded middle class (white-collar managers and professionals), and the industrial capitalist class (who displaced the landed aristocracy). Across all these new formations, women's political and economic subordination persisted — a major continuity.

**Governance**: The Enlightenment generated constitutionalism and representative government; revolutions installed them; industrialization gradually extended them (Reform Bills, voting rights for working-class men). But the process was slow, contested, and incomplete — women were excluded throughout Period 3. Outside industrializing Western nations, authoritarian governments persisted or strengthened.

**Technology & Environment**: Steam power → factory concentration → urbanization → slums → disease. The Second Industrial Revolution added steel, electricity, petroleum, and railroads — extending industrial reach globally. The environmental costs (coal smog, water contamination, ecosystem disruption) were borne disproportionately by the urban poor — an early instance of environmental inequality.

**Cultural Developments**: Unit 5 produced three great intellectual traditions that defined the 20th century: **liberalism** (individual rights and free markets), **nationalism** (cultural unity as political legitimacy), and **socialism/Marxism** (collective ownership and worker power). All three emerged as responses to the Enlightenment and industrialization's combined effects.

**Human Migration**: The Industrial Revolution drove one of history's great internal migrations — rural populations into industrial cities. It also drove international migration: Irish, Eastern European, and Chinese workers moved to industrial centers in Britain and the United States. And it intensified the flow of colonized peoples as raw material laborers — the economic migration that prefigures Unit 6's imperialism.

## Key Comparisons for the AP Exam

**American Revolution vs. French Revolution**: Both drew on Enlightenment natural rights. The American Revolution was more conservative — it preserved existing social hierarchies (slavery, property requirements for voting) while changing political sovereignty. The French Revolution was far more radical — it challenged aristocracy, church authority, and eventually republican government itself (Reign of Terror → Napoleon). The American result was more stable; the French result was more disruptive.

**French Revolution vs. Haitian Revolution**: The Haitian Revolution took French Enlightenment ideals more seriously than France did — applying "liberty, equality, fraternity" to enslaved people. While France eventually restored slavery (1802), Haiti achieved permanent emancipation. The Haitian Revolution is the most radical application of Enlightenment logic in this period.

**Japan (Meiji) vs. China (Self-Strengthening)**: Same stimulus (Western military pressure); opposite outcomes (successful vs. failed industrialization). The difference: political will to dismantle the existing order. Japan's Meiji oligarchs abolished feudalism; China's Qing court resisted restructuring to protect its own power.

**Capitalism (Adam Smith) vs. Marxism (Karl Marx)**: Both analyzed industrial capitalism; they reached opposite conclusions. Smith: markets distribute benefits broadly; private pursuit of profit → general prosperity. Marx: markets structurally exploit workers; capitalism must be replaced by collective ownership. This tension defined 20th-century political history.

**British Reform (gradual) vs. Continental Revolution**: Britain responded to industrial social pressure through parliamentary reform bills (1832, 1867, 1884) — gradual, legislative, without revolution. France, Germany, and the Austrian Empire faced revolutionary uprisings (1848) that were suppressed but eventually produced reform. Britain's stable path reflected its stronger parliamentary tradition and larger middle class.

**Working-Class Women vs. Middle-Class Women**: Both groups experienced industrialization's gender inequality — but differently. Working-class women worked in factories and domestic service, managing wage labor and household simultaneously. Middle-class women were confined by the cult of domesticity — an ideology that made housewifery a status symbol. The experience of "women under industrialization" varied radically by class — a key complexity for AP essays.

## Practice Thesis Statements

**Prompt**: *"Evaluate the extent to which the Industrial Revolution produced significant changes in social structures in the period c. 1750–c. 1900."*

**Strong thesis**: "The Industrial Revolution produced significant but incomplete changes in social structures between c. 1750 and c. 1900: the emergence of a new industrial class hierarchy — with factory workers, an expanded middle class, and industrial capitalists replacing the landed aristocracy — represented a fundamental transformation in the basis of social power, while the persistence of women's political and economic exclusion across all classes demonstrates that industrialization's social revolution had clear limits."

---

**Prompt**: *"Evaluate the extent to which the period c. 1750–c. 1900 was a turning point in global economic integration."*

**Strong thesis**: "The period c. 1750–c. 1900 was a decisive turning point in global economic integration because industrial capitalism created the first truly global production system — with Western factories, colonized raw material regions, and interconnected commodity markets — though this integration deepened existing global inequalities rather than producing shared prosperity, as non-Western manufacturing output collapsed while Western output surged."

---

**Prompt**: *"Compare the responses of TWO non-Western states to the economic and military challenges posed by industrializing Western powers in the period c. 1750–c. 1900."*

**Strong thesis**: "Japan's [[Concept Notes/Meiji Restoration|Meiji Restoration]] and China's Self-Strengthening Movement both responded to Western industrial pressure by attempting to modernize, but produced opposite outcomes because Japan's government achieved political consensus around abolishing the feudal order entirely, while China's Qing court remained internally divided and unwilling to restructure the political system that conservative elites depended on for their own power."
