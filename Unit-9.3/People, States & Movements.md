## Key Figures

**Rachel Carson (1907–1964)**
American marine biologist and author of *Silent Spring* (1962), which documented how synthetic pesticides — especially DDT — moved through food chains and devastated bird populations and ecosystems. Carson's work launched the modern environmental movement in the United States by showing that industrial chemicals had consequences far beyond their intended targets. *Silent Spring* directly contributed to the banning of DDT in the U.S. (1972) and inspired the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). She represents the role of scientific writing in shaping public policy.

> [!exam-tip]
> Rachel Carson = origin point of modern environmentalism. The AP may pair her with later activists (Maathai, Thunberg) to ask about continuity in environmental activism across the 20th–21st centuries.

**Wangari Maathai (1940–2011)**
Kenyan environmental and political activist who founded the **Green Belt Movement** in 1977. Mobilizing Kenyan women to plant trees, Maathai connected environmental conservation to women's rights, community empowerment, and resistance to authoritarian development policies. The movement planted over 51 million trees in Kenya, reducing soil erosion and greenhouse gases while creating local employment. Maathai was awarded the **Nobel Peace Prize in 2004** — the first African woman to receive it — for her contributions to sustainable development, democracy, and peace. She exemplifies how grassroots, community-led activism (especially by women) can produce measurable environmental change in the developing world.

> [!tip]
> Maathai links ENV + SIO themes: environmental protection through women's empowerment. The Nobel Peace Prize framing also signals that the UN recognized climate activism as a peace and security issue, not just an environmental one.

**James Hansen (b. 1941)**
NASA climate scientist who testified before the U.S. Senate in 1988 that global warming had begun. Hansen's testimony was a turning point in bringing climate science into the political arena, helping build the public and governmental awareness that eventually produced the Kyoto Protocol (1997). He represents the role of scientific institutions and expert testimony in driving international policy.

**Ban Ki-moon (b. 1944)**
South Korean diplomat who served as UN Secretary-General from 2007 to 2016. His statement — *"Climate change does not respect borders; it does not respect who you are — rich and poor, small and big"* — opens the AMSCO chapter (p. 649) and captures the core argument for global collective action on climate. His leadership helped build momentum for the Paris Agreement (2015).

**Greta Thunberg (b. 2003)**
Swedish climate activist who began a solo protest outside the Swedish parliament at age 15, demanding stronger government action on climate change. Her speech at a 2018 UN climate conference — *"You say you love your children above all else, and yet you are stealing their future in front of their very eyes"* — went viral. She inspired the global **School Strike for Climate (Fridays for Future)** movement, which drew over 1.6 million participants in 125+ countries. Thunberg represents the emergence of youth-led, globally networked climate activism in the 21st century.

> [!tip]
> Thunberg's global strike shows how the **internet and social media** ([[Unit-9.1/Advances in Technology & Exchange Overview\|Topic 9.1]]) amplified environmental activism to a scale impossible before the Information Age. Technology caused the problem; technology also enables coordinated global response.

## Key Organizations & Movements

**Club of Rome (est. 1968)**
A European organization of scientists, industrialists, and diplomats formed to promote solutions to global challenges. Its landmark 1972 report *The Limits to Growth* warned that continued resource depletion and pollution would eventually limit economic growth — an early articulation of sustainability principles. The Club of Rome elevated environmental concerns from scientific literature into global policy discussion.

**Green Parties**
Political parties emerging globally in the late 20th century that made environmental protection their central platform. Distinct from traditional left-right alignments, Green Parties grew most influential in Western Europe (Germany's Greens became a significant parliamentary force). They represent the translation of environmental activism into electoral politics.

****Green Belt Movement****
Founded by Wangari Maathai in Kenya (1977). Organized communities — particularly women — to plant trees, fight soil erosion, and protect wilderness from urban sprawl. By the early 21st century, the movement had planted over 51 million trees in Kenya. Global model for linking environmental conservation with gender equity and grassroots development.

**Extinction Rebellion (est. 2018)**
A British civil-disobedience climate movement that blockaded bridges and intersections in London, chained activists to corporate headquarters, and disrupted "business as usual" to pressure governments to declare climate emergencies. About 1,000 activists were arrested, but the group succeeded in prompting the UK Parliament to convene a citizens' assembly on climate policy. Represents the radicalization of climate activism as scientific warnings grew more urgent.

**UN Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)**
The body of international scientists that synthesizes global climate research and issues authoritative reports on the state of climate change and its projected impacts. IPCC reports provided the scientific foundation for both the Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement. James Hansen's 1988 testimony preceded the IPCC's first assessment report (1990), which formalized international scientific consensus on anthropogenic (human-caused) warming.

## Key States & Their Positions

**United States** — Largest per-capita carbon emitter; refused to ratify the Kyoto Protocol; withdrew from the Paris Agreement in 2017 under Trump; re-entered in 2021 under Biden. Represents the tension between economic interests in fossil-fuel industries and international climate commitments.

**China** — Surpassed the U.S. as the world's largest total emitter of CO₂ in 2007. As a developing nation, China was exempt from Kyoto's strictest terms but signed the Paris Agreement. China's position exemplifies the developing-nation argument: per-capita emissions remain far lower than developed nations even as total output grows.

**Western European nations** — Generally most committed to international climate agreements; Green Parties influential in Germany, France, and Scandinavia; host countries for key agreements (Kyoto, Japan; Paris, France).

**India, Russia, Brazil** — Major developing economies exempt from Kyoto's binding limits. Their rapid industrialization significantly increased global emissions while they argued that developed nations — having already industrialized on coal and oil — bore primary historical responsibility.
