# Technology & the Environment Overview

**AP Period 4** | Unit 9: Globalization | Printed pp. 649–656

**Essential Question:** What were the causes and effects of environmental changes from 1900 to the present?

**AP Themes:** ENV (Environment), TEC (Technology), ECN (Economic Systems)
**AP Skills:** Causation, Continuity and Change Over Time

---

## The Big Picture

The industrial technologies that drove unprecedented economic growth in the 20th century also imposed unprecedented costs on the planet. Population growth, urbanization, and the global spread of industrialization combined to create four interlocking environmental crises: deforestation, desertification, declining air quality, and freshwater scarcity. These were not random natural events — they were caused by human decisions about land use, energy, and production.

The most consequential long-term effect was the accumulation of **greenhouse gases** in the atmosphere, primarily from burning **fossil fuels**. By the late 20th century, scientific consensus had formed around **global warming** as a human-caused emergency. The international response — the **Kyoto Protocol** (1997) and the **Paris Agreement** (2015) — reflected both the urgency of the problem and the deep tensions between developed and developing nations over who bore responsibility for fixing it.

> [!exam-tip]
> The AP exam tests causation for this topic heavily. Know the **chain**: industrialization → population growth → deforestation/emissions → greenhouse gases → global warming → international agreements. Also know **why the agreements were limited**: U.S. refusal to ratify Kyoto; developing nations (China, India) exempt; U.S. withdrawal from Paris in 2017.

## Causes of Environmental Change

Four interconnected forces drove environmental change after 1900.

**Population growth** was the foundational pressure. World population grew from 1.6 billion in 1900 to 6.12 billion in 2000 — nearly a fourfold increase. More people required more cropland, more water, more energy. Agricultural expansion drove **deforestation**, soil erosion, and habitat loss. Overfishing depleted ocean fisheries like cod. Demand for freshwater outpaced supply in many regions.

**Urbanization** intensified the pressure. By 2025 estimates, 5.1 billion people will live in cities. Urban food demand pushed intensive farming methods that depleted soil and cleared forests. Urban waste streams polluted the water supplies cities depended on.

**Globalization and industrialization** spread the problem globally. As manufacturing moved to developing countries, energy demand and resource extraction followed. Emerging middle classes in developing countries created new markets for cars and consumer goods — increasing both resource consumption and pollution output.

**Resource depletion** became an existential concern. By the late 20th century, roughly half of Earth's petroleum reserves had been consumed since industrial extraction began in the mid-1800s. The Club of Rome (est. 1968) warned that continued resource depletion would limit economic growth — a warning that proved prescient.

> [!warning]
> Don't conflate cause and effect. Population growth is a **cause** of deforestation and water scarcity; global warming is an **effect** of greenhouse gas emissions from industrialization. The AP exam rewards students who can trace these chains precisely.

## Environmental Changes: What Was Happening

The four major categories of environmental change that AMSCO emphasizes (p. 649):

**Deforestation** — Loss of forest cover due to agricultural expansion, logging, and urban growth. Forests store carbon; their removal both releases stored CO₂ and eliminates a key carbon sink.

**Desertification** — Fertile land degrading into desert through removal of vegetation, overgrazing, and unsustainable farming in arid and semi-arid regions. Particularly severe in Sub-Saharan Africa and Central Asia.

**Declining air quality** — Industrial emissions, vehicle exhaust, and factory smoke increased atmospheric pollutants, creating health crises in industrial cities and contributing to greenhouse gas buildup.

**Water scarcity** — According to the UN, 31 countries faced water scarcity by the early 21st century; over 1 billion people lacked clean drinking water. Corporate extraction and pollution compounded the problem. Water scarcity disproportionately burdened women and girls in developing countries, who bore primary responsibility for water collection — time that could otherwise be spent in school or earning income.

> [!tip]
> Water scarcity connects to gender inequality — a favorite AP cross-theme link. A study in Ghana showed that a 15-minute reduction in water collection time increased girls' school attendance by 8–12%. This links ENV to SIO.

## Changes in the Atmosphere and the Energy Response

Industrial processes released massive quantities of carbon dioxide and other **greenhouse gases** that trapped solar heat in the atmosphere. Simultaneously, forests — Earth's natural carbon sinks — were shrinking.

In response, companies and nations began investing in **renewable energy** — solar, wind, tidal, and geothermal — as sustainable alternatives to **fossil fuels**. By 2018, projections suggested that by 2050, half the world's electricity could come from wind and solar. But in the early 21st century, renewables still provided only about 7% of global energy needs.

## The Global Warming Debate

By the late 20th century, scientific consensus — backed by the UN's Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) — held that human emissions were causing **global warming**. The consequences: more powerful hurricanes, severe droughts, rising sea levels.

But the debate was politically charged. Climate-change skeptics questioned whether human activity was driving warming. Energy industry interests argued that market forces, not government regulation, should drive the response. Developed and developing nations clashed over responsibility: developed nations argued that fast-growing China, India, Russia, and Brazil needed to cut emissions; developing nations countered that they emitted far less per capita and deserved the same right to industrialize that developed nations had exercised.

> [!exam-tip]
> The **developed vs. developing divide** on climate policy is a high-frequency AP comparison. Developed nations: "your emissions are growing fast." Developing nations: "you already industrialized on coal and oil — our per-capita emissions are far lower." This tension explains why both Kyoto and Paris were compromises.

## International Agreements

**Kyoto Protocol (1997)** — The first major binding international agreement to reduce carbon emissions. Industrialized nations agreed to cut emissions; developing nations (China, India, Russia, Brazil) were not required to meet the strictest terms. The United States refused to ratify it, severely limiting its impact.

**Paris Agreement (2015)** — 195 nations committed to limiting global temperature increases to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels. Unlike Kyoto, it included both developed and developing nations, including China and the United States. In 2017, President Trump withdrew the U.S. from Paris; President Biden re-entered in 2021.

> [!warning]
> Know the **difference** between Kyoto and Paris: Kyoto was binding but excluded developing nations and was never ratified by the U.S. Paris was broader but less binding. Both showed that international climate cooperation was possible but politically fragile.

## Environmental Activism

Growing awareness of environmental crisis generated new forms of political activism. **Green Parties** emerged globally, making environmental protection a core political platform. The **[[Concept Notes/Green Belt Movement\|Green Belt Movement]]**, founded by Kenyan activist Wangari Maathai in 1977, organized communities — especially women — to plant trees across Kenya, planting over 51 million trees and creating employment while reducing greenhouse gases.

Youth-led activism intensified in the 21st century. Swedish teenager **Greta Thunberg** sparked a global climate strike with 1.6 million participants in 125+ countries (2018–2019). **Extinction Rebellion** (est. 2018) used civil disobedience in London — blocking bridges, chaining activists to corporate headquarters — to pressure governments into declaring climate emergencies.

## The Anthropocene

Some scientists proposed calling the current geological era the **Anthropocene** ("new man") — replacing the traditional Holocene epoch (which began ~11,700 years ago after the last ice age). The Anthropocene concept holds that human activity has become the dominant force shaping Earth's climate and ecosystems. In 2019, a panel of scientists voted to approve the name. It represents both a scientific classification and a moral reckoning: humans have remade the planet, for better and worse.

> [!tip]
> **Anthropocene** is a great essay term because it synthesizes the entire topic: human technology and industry, operating at global scale, have crossed a threshold where we are the primary agent of geological and ecological change. Use it in LEQ conclusions.
