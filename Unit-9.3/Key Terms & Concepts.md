# Key Terms & Concepts

**AP Period 4** | Topic 9.3: Technology and the Environment | Printed pp. 649–656

---

**Green Party**
A political party that prioritizes environmental protection, sustainability, and ecological issues as its central platform. Green Parties emerged globally in the late 20th century as awareness of industrialization's environmental damage grew, representing a new form of political activism outside traditional left-right alignments. Their rise reflected a broader shift toward single-issue and values-based politics in democratic societies.

**[[Concept Notes/Green Revolution\|Green Revolution]]**
The mid-20th century transformation of global agriculture through high-yield crop varieties, synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and irrigation (see Topic 9.1). Relevant here because the Green Revolution's intensive farming methods contributed to soil depletion, water overuse, and pesticide pollution — showing that technological solutions to one problem (food production) can create new environmental problems.

**Green Belt Movement**
An environmental and social movement founded in Kenya by Wangari Maathai (1977) that organized communities — especially women — to plant trees, conserve soil, and protect wilderness from urban sprawl. The movement planted over 51 million trees in Kenya, created employment, improved soil quality, and reduced greenhouse gases. It became a global model linking environmental activism with women's empowerment and community development.

**deforestation**
Clearing of forests for agriculture, fuel, urban expansion, or logging. A recurring environmental consequence of population growth and agricultural expansion across world history, deforestation accelerated dramatically in the industrial era. It causes biodiversity loss, soil erosion, increased runoff, and reduced carbon absorption — making it a major driver of both local environmental degradation and global climate change.

**desertification**
The process by which fertile land becomes desert, typically caused by the removal of natural vegetation cover through deforestation, overgrazing, or unsustainable agriculture. A major environmental consequence of industrialization and population growth, particularly acute in Sub-Saharan Africa and Central Asia. Closely linked to water scarcity, food insecurity, and forced migration.

**air quality**
The cleanliness of the atmosphere as affected by industrial pollutants, vehicle emissions, and other human activities. Declining air quality — from factory smoke, carbon emissions, and smog — became a major public health and environmental issue in the 20th century, driving international environmental regulation. Urban industrial centers from London to Beijing to Los Angeles faced severe air pollution crises.

**greenhouse gases**
Gases — primarily carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide — that trap heat in Earth's atmosphere. Emitted by burning fossil fuels, industrial processes, and agriculture, the rapid increase of greenhouse gases since industrialization is the leading driver of global warming and climate change. Forests are key natural absorbers of greenhouse gases, making deforestation a double threat.

**fossil fuels**
Carbon-based energy sources — coal, oil, petroleum, and natural gas — formed over millions of years from decomposed organic matter. The primary energy source of industrial economies since the mid-1800s, fossil fuels release greenhouse gases when burned and are nonrenewable. As roughly half of Earth's petroleum reserves had been consumed by the late 20th century, concerns about both depletion and climate change drove investment in alternatives.

**water scarcity**
The lack of sufficient clean, accessible drinking water to meet human needs. Caused by pollution, population growth, climate change, and unequal distribution, water scarcity affected over 1 billion people by the early 21st century. It is closely linked to poverty, political instability, and gender inequality — in developing countries, women and girls disproportionately bear the burden of water collection, reducing time for education and paid work.

**renewable energy**
Energy derived from naturally replenishing sources — solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal, and tidal power — that do not deplete finite resources or produce significant greenhouse gas emissions. Investment in renewable energy expanded significantly from the 1970s onward as concern over both fossil fuel depletion and climate change grew. By the early 21st century, renewables still provided only ~7% of global energy but projections pointed toward rapid growth.

**global warming**
The long-term rise in Earth's average surface temperature due primarily to increased concentrations of greenhouse gases from human industrial activity. Confirmed by the UN's Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), global warming drives climate change, sea-level rise, and extreme weather events. Without reduced carbon emissions, experts predicted more powerful hurricanes, severe droughts, and coastal flooding. The term is sometimes replaced by "climate crisis" or "climate emergency" to convey urgency.

**carbon footprint**
The total amount of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases produced by an individual, organization, or nation's activities. Reducing carbon footprints — through energy efficiency, renewable energy adoption, and consumer behavior changes — became a central goal of environmental policy in response to global warming. Debates over who bore responsibility (developed vs. developing nations) shaped international climate negotiations.

**Anthropocene**
An informal geological epoch proposed by scientists to describe the current era in which human activity has become the dominant force shaping Earth's climate, ecosystems, and geology. Replacing the Holocene (which began ~11,700 years ago), the Anthropocene captures how industrialization, deforestation, pollution, and mass extinction have altered the planet at a scale comparable to major geological events. A panel of scientists voted to approve the name in 2019.

> [!exam-tip]
> The Anthropocene concept is excellent for AP argument writing because it provides a periodization framework: human impact has crossed a qualitative threshold where it is now *geological* in scale. Use it to argue that the environmental changes of the 20th–21st centuries represent a fundamental break from prior patterns.

**Kyoto Protocol**
An international treaty signed in 1997 in Kyoto, Japan, in which industrialized nations agreed to reduce greenhouse gas emissions to combat global warming. It was the first binding international commitment to limit carbon emissions. Key limitations: the United States — the world's largest emitter at the time — refused to ratify it; developing nations (China, India, Russia, Brazil) were exempt from the strictest terms. Despite these gaps, Kyoto established the precedent for binding international climate agreements.

**Paris Agreement**
A landmark 2015 international climate accord in which 196 nations committed to limiting global temperature increases to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels. Unlike the Kyoto Protocol, it included both developed and developing nations, and the United States and China — then the world's two largest emitters — both signed on. U.S. withdrawal in 2017 under President Trump and re-entry in 2021 under President Biden highlighted the political volatility of climate policy. The Paris Agreement represents the most comprehensive international climate commitment to date.

> [!tip]
> **Kyoto vs. Paris — a key comparison**: Kyoto (1997) = binding, limited to developed nations, U.S. never ratified. Paris (2015) = near-universal, includes developing nations, voluntary national targets, U.S. withdrew then re-entered. Both show that international climate governance requires reconciling sovereignty with collective action.
