# Prologue: History before 1200 C.E.

> [!summary] About this note
> The AMSCO Prologue (pp. xli–lxii) covers the three eras that set the stage for AP World History: Modern. This content is **background context**, not directly testable on the AP exam. But understanding it is essential — Unit 1 assumes you know it, and contextualization points often require reaching back before 1200.

---

## Part 1: Human Development to c. 600 B.C.E.

### The First Migrations

Modern humans (*Homo sapiens*) appeared in East Africa between 200,000–100,000 B.C.E. They were hunter-gatherers living in small bands, practicing **animism** (reverence for nature-based deities) and showing early signs of **patriarchy**. Between 100,000 and 60,000 B.C.E., populations migrated out of Africa. By 10,000 B.C.E., humans lived on every continent except Antarctica.

### The Agricultural Revolution

Around 8000 B.C.E., a warming climate prompted humans to plant crops and raise animals — beginning in the Middle East. This shift transformed everything:

- **Food surplus** → specialization of labor (artisans, priests, merchants)
- **Larger settlements** → cities, then civilizations
- **New technology**: irrigation, the wheel, bronze and iron tools
- **Writing** invented to track trade and taxes
- **Social stratification increased**; women's status generally declined

### The First Civilizations

Most early civilizations developed in river valleys (fresh water + fertile land):

| Civilization | Location | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Mesopotamia** | Tigris-Euphrates (Iraq) | City-states; **cuneiform** (first writing); ziggurats; polytheistic; code of laws |
| **Sumer** | Southern Mesopotamia | First written laws; complex taxation |
| **Egypt** | Nile River | Centralized under pharaoh; **hieroglyphics**; pyramids; women had property/legal rights |
| **Indus (Harappa/Mohenjo-Daro)** | South Asia | Long-distance trade; indoor plumbing; urban planning; language undeciphered |
| **China (Huang He)** | Northern China | Highly patriarchal; ancestor veneration |
| **Olmec / Chavín** | Mesoamerica / Andes | Did not develop in river valleys; still had extensive trade networks |

### Early Religions: Hinduism and Judaism

**Hinduism** — origins ~3,500 years ago; Indo-European Aryans brought the **Vedas** southward into India. Teaches reincarnation (soul reborn until liberated), and organizes society into the **caste system** (no social mobility). Over time, many deities came to be seen as expressions of one supreme deity.

**Zoroastrianism** — early Persian monotheism; focuses on free will and the eternal battle between good and evil.

**Judaism** — most influential early monotheism. Hebrews (Jews) trace their covenant with God (Yahweh) to Abraham (~2000 B.C.E.). Codified in the Hebrew Scriptures. Foundational to both Christianity and Islam.

> [!tip]
> Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are all Abrahamic faiths — they all trace spiritual lineage to Abraham and share a belief in one God. This is a common comparison point on the exam.

## Part 2: The Classical Era, c. 600 B.C.E.–c. 600 C.E.

The great classical empires provided political stability, sponsored trade, and established cultural foundations that would define civilizations for centuries after their collapse.

### South Asia: Buddhism and the Mauryan/Gupta Empires

**Buddhism** — founded by Siddhartha Gautama (~530 B.C.E.), who sought to understand suffering. His teachings — the **Four Noble Truths** and the **Eightfold Path** — offered a path to enlightenment (**nirvana**) and rejected the caste system. Unlike Hinduism and Judaism, Buddhism was a **universalizing religion** (actively sought converts) and spread through missionaries and merchants along the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]] and Indian Ocean.

**Mauryan Empire** (322–187 B.C.E.) — India's first unified state. Emperor **Ashoka** converted to Buddhism, promoted its spread, built roads, and inscribed laws on pillars. His reign represents one of history's rare examples of a ruler adopting a new faith and using it for benevolent governance.

**Gupta Empire** (c. 320–550 C.E.) — India's "Golden Age." Advances in medicine (inoculations), mathematics (**zero and place value** — the system used worldwide today), and literature. Strongly supported Hinduism as a unifying force.

### East Asia: Confucianism and the Qin/Han Dynasties

**Confucianism** — teachings of Confucius (551 B.C.E.); recorded in the *Analects*. Emphasis on education, benevolence, hierarchy, respect for authority, **filial piety** (honoring ancestors), and patriarchy. Not focused on deities — a philosophy of social order. The most influential belief system in Chinese history.

**Daoism** — arose alongside Confucianism; focused on harmony with *nature* (internal reflection) rather than harmony with society (external behavior).

**Mandate of Heaven** — Zhou Dynasty legacy. "Heaven" justified an emperor's rule; natural disasters signaled that a ruler had lost the mandate — a justification for rebellion used repeatedly in Chinese history.

**Qin Dynasty** (221–207 B.C.E.) — Brief but transformative. Standardized script, weights/measures, built canals and roads. The foundation for everything that followed.

**Han Dynasty** (206 B.C.E.–220 C.E.) — China's first "Golden Age." Invented paper, gunpowder, and the magnetic compass. Established the **civil service exam** (Confucian-based, merit-driven bureaucracy). Silk Roads trade flourished — luxury goods from Chang'an to the Mediterranean.

### Western Eurasia: Persia, Greece, Rome, and Christianity

**Persian Empire** (c. 559 B.C.E.–330 B.C.E.) — Cyrus the Great; ethnically and religiously diverse; governed through regional provinces (satraps); practiced **religious toleration**. Road network fostered trade.

**Greece** — ~1,000 city-states, united by language and religion. Athens developed **democracy** (free adult males only) and made advances in philosophy, architecture, and theater. Sparta organized around military discipline. Alexander the Great (300s B.C.E.) spread Greek culture — the **Hellenistic world** — from Egypt to India.

**Rome** — borrowed heavily from Greeks. Key innovations: **representative government** (Senate), written laws (Twelve Tables), "innocent until proven guilty," public infrastructure (aqueducts, roads). At its peak ruled from Scotland to North Africa to the Middle East. Declined due to military overextension, corruption, epidemics (smallpox, bubonic plague), and invasions.

**Christianity** — emerged from Judaism in the Roman Empire. Jesus's teachings spread despite Roman persecution. Under Emperor **Constantine** (4th century), Christianity became legal — and eventually the empire's official religion. Like Buddhism, it was a **universalizing religion**, actively seeking converts and adaptable across cultures.

**Byzantine Empire** — Eastern Roman Empire, capital at Constantinople. Survived for 900 years after Rome's fall. Under Justinian (527–565): built **Hagia Sophia**; codified Roman law as the **Justinian Code** (foundation of European law into the 19th century). Constantinople was a major **entrepôt** (coastal trading hub) connecting northern Europe, Egypt, and Asia.

### The Americas: Teotihuacan and the Maya

Both developed in isolation from Afro-Eurasia during the Classical Era.

**Teotihuacan** — near modern Mexico City; population ~125,000 by the 6th century C.E. (one of the world's largest cities). Multicultural, grid-planned, monumental sun and moon temples. Abandoned by 650 C.E.; later influenced the Aztecs.

**Maya** — Mesoamerica's most influential classical civilization. Complex written language, accurate calendar, concept of zero, advanced astronomy. Height: 250–900 C.E. Decline caused by environmental degradation, drought, and warfare.

### Classical Empires — Common Causes of Decline

By 600 C.E., most classical empires were fragmenting. Shared causes:

- Tax collection failures → weakened government
- Declining trade → reduced access to goods
- Epidemic disease (smallpox, plague) → reduced urban populations
- Growing wealth gap → social conflict
- Military overextension → security failures
- Invasions from outside groups

**Religion as a stabilizing force post-collapse:** Christianity in Europe, Confucianism in China, Hinduism/Buddhism in South Asia all helped hold societies together after governments failed — and provided the cultural foundation for new empires to arise.

**Government in the Classical Period:**

| Empire | Form | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Mauryan/Gupta | Centralized empire | Sophisticated bureaucracy; religious toleration (Gupta) / Hinduism (Gupta) |
| Qin/Han | Centralized empire | Civil service exam; Confucian merit-based bureaucracy |
| Persian | Centralized empire | Regional provinces; religious toleration |
| Greek | Decentralized city-states | Athenian democracy (free adult males) |
| Roman | Republic then empire | Senate; representative elements; written law |
| Byzantine | Centralized empire | Justinian Code; state-appointed religious leaders |
| Maya | Decentralized city-states | Theocracy; rulers as divine descendants |

## Part 3: Postclassical Civilizations, c. 600–c. 1200

After the collapse of classical empires, trade networks revived and expanded. The [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]], Indian Ocean routes, and trans-Saharan routes carried porcelain, ivory, spices, silk — and ideas: the compass, astrolabe, new credit systems, paper money, and new religious beliefs. Disease also spread along these routes.

### The Spread of Islam

**Muhammad** (Arabian Peninsula, early 7th century) received revelations recorded in the **Quran**. Core obligations: the **Five Pillars** (monotheism/Allah, prayer, almsgiving, fasting, pilgrimage to Mecca). Legal framework: **sharia** (no separation of religious and civil law).

**Sunni/Shi'a split** — arose after Muhammad's death in 632 over who should be caliph. Sunnis: any qualified leader; Shi'a: must be a blood relative of Muhammad. This division persists today.

**Expansion of Islam** — Within a century: southern Spain, North Africa, Middle East, parts of India → **Dar al-Islam** (House of Islam).

**Abbasid Caliphate** (750–1258) — Islamic golden age. Baghdad became the world's center of learning: medicine, astronomy, algebra, the astrolabe, preservation of Greek texts. Facilitated Silk Roads revival (with Tang China), trans-Saharan trade, and Indian Ocean commerce. The **jizya** (tax on non-Muslims) allowed religious toleration. Women gained some rights (end of female infanticide; stronger marriage/property rights) while veiling and polygamy continued.

### China: Sui, Tang, and Song Dynasties

**Sui Dynasty** (581–618) — Brief but critical. Reunified China after three centuries of chaos. Built the **Grand Canal** (1,000+ miles, connecting agricultural south to population centers in north).

**Tang Dynasty** (618–907) — Expanded China's territory (Mongolia, Central Asia, Vietnam). Invented **gunpowder** and **paper money**. Fast-ripening rice varieties → population growth. Expanded civil service exam. Second golden age of the Silk Roads.

**Song Dynasty** (960–1279) — Continued the golden age. World's leading manufacturer (iron, steel, silk, porcelain). **Neo-Confucianism** (synthesis of Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist thought) flourished. Magnetic compass and paper money exported globally. **Tributary system**: surrounding kingdoms paid tribute to China in exchange for trade privileges.

**Japan** — By 800–1200, golden age of arts and literature. Eventually decentralized into competing clans. **Shogun** (military general) at top; **daimyo** (land-owning nobles) beneath; **samurai** warriors pledged to daimyo. **Shinto** (veneration of ancestors and nature spirits) was the official religion; Buddhism adopted alongside it.

### Africa

Most of sub-Saharan Africa consisted of kin-based chiefdoms. The **Bantu migrations** (c. 1000 B.C.E.–c. 1500 C.E.) spread language, farming, and ironworking across the continent.

**Ghana** (c. 700–c. 1240) — West Africa. Trans-Saharan trade (gold and salt) made Ghana wealthy. Muslim merchants spread Islam into the region.

**Great Zimbabwe** (12th–15th centuries) — Southeast Africa. Dominated Indian Ocean trade in the region; declined likely due to reduced gold output.

East African coast: linked into the Indian Ocean network. Exported gold, ivory, enslaved people; imported porcelain, silk, spices.

### South Asia and Southeast Asia

After the Gupta fell (550 C.E.), South Asia was fragmented. Hinduism and the caste system kept southern India culturally unified despite political decentralization. India's position made it the **hub of Indian Ocean trade** — goods from East Asia, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and Africa all moved through it. Islam reached India in 711 C.E.

Religion and trade spread **Buddhism** and **Hinduism** — and later **Islam** — into Southeast Asia. Muslim merchants settled with families, spreading Islam organically. Islam became dominant in the Spice Islands and Malay Peninsula.

### Europe

Western Roman Empire's collapse left a power vacuum filled by thousands of small feudal territories. **Vikings** added to the cultural mix. Without strong central governments, Europe could not protect merchants and missed out on Afro-Eurasian trade.

**Christianity** unified central and western Europe culturally. Pope crowned **Charlemagne** "Emperor of the Romans" (800). But in **1054**, Christianity split: **Roman Catholic Church** (west) vs. **Orthodox Church** (east).

**The Crusades** (beginning 1095) — failed to hold Jerusalem but exposed Europe to Middle Eastern and Asian achievements, stimulating intellectual interest and eventually contributing to European expansion.

### The Americas: c. 600–1200

Maya golden age ended by ~900 (environmental degradation, drought, warfare). Two new civilizations:

**Mississippian** — near present-day St. Louis; flourished 8th–16th centuries. City of **Cahokia** was larger than contemporary London — a major trade hub.

**Toltecs** — emerged 10th century in Mesoamerica; adopted Mayan practices; heavily influenced the later Aztecs.

## The World in 1200

By 1200, the world had recovered from classical collapse and entered a new era of intensifying exchange:

- Africa, Europe, and Asia connected via Indian Ocean, Silk Roads, and trans-Saharan routes
- Byzantine Empire and Islamic empires providing stability from Eastern Europe through the Middle East to South Asia
- China and Dar al-Islam as the world's leading centers of learning and innovation
- Western Europe and Japan: **decentralized**, land-owning noble classes, weak central government
- Africa: mostly stateless except for West/East African regions within Dar al-Islam
- Afro-Eurasia, the Americas, and Oceania still developing in isolation from each other

What no one in 1200 could predict: a group of Central Asian nomads — the Mongols — would upend Eurasia within decades. And in 1492, a European voyage would reshape the entire world.

## Reflect on the Prologue

> [!tip] 1. Comparison — How are Judaism, Islam, and Christianity alike?
> All three are **Abrahamic monotheisms** — they trace spiritual lineage to Abraham, believe in one all-powerful God, and share sacred texts (the Hebrew Bible underpins all three). All three are universalizing religions with ethical codes governing human behavior. Islam and Christianity actively sought converts; Judaism less so.

> [!tip] 2. Comparison — Centralized vs. decentralized civilizations
> **Centralized**: one ruler/government controls the whole territory (e.g., Han China, Persian Empire, Roman Empire). Promotes standardization, trade, and stability, but vulnerable to collapse if the center fails. **Decentralized**: power distributed among local rulers (e.g., Greek city-states, feudal Europe, Japan's shogunate). More politically resilient locally, but struggles to coordinate large-scale trade and defense.

> [!tip] 3. Causation — Three causes for the decline of classical civilizations
> (1) **Tax revenue failures** weakened central government capacity. (2) **Epidemic disease** (smallpox, bubonic plague) killed urban populations and disrupted trade. (3) **Invasions by outside groups** (Huns, Vandals, nomadic groups) overstretched military resources. Supporting causes: military overextension, wealth inequality, and loss of popular support for leadership.

> [!tip] 4. Causation — How trade networks spread Islam and Buddhism
> Both religions spread because **merchants traveled the same routes that carried goods**. Buddhist missionaries used the [[Concept Notes/Silk Roads|Silk Roads]] and Indian Ocean routes; Muslim merchants settled in new regions with their families, spreading Islam organically rather than through conquest alone. Trade networks created constant cross-cultural contact — and religions traveled wherever merchants went.

> [!tip] 5. Continuity — What kept southern India unified after the Gupta fell?
> **Hinduism and the caste system** provided cultural and social continuity. Even without a strong central government, these shared religious beliefs and social structures organized daily life, maintained hierarchy, and preserved cultural identity across the politically fragmented region.

> [!tip] 6. Change — One new historical development after 600 C.E.
> Many valid answers: the rise of **Islam** and Dar al-Islam; the **Abbasid golden age** of learning; the **Tang/Song dynastic revival** in China and its technological innovations; the **trans-Saharan trade network's** expansion; the development of **feudalism** in Europe and Japan; or the emergence of **Neo-Confucianism**. The key is identifying something that did not exist before 600 C.E.
